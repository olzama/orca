Orca: Open Replayable Constraint-based Analysis

How a sentence gets its analysis, on four real examples

A neural tagger proposes a complete derivation of the English Resource Grammar. The grammar then replays that derivation by unification and either accepts it or says where it breaks. An accepted derivation yields the full HPSG feature structure, and with it the semantics. No chart search is involved. The sentences are from the development set of the 2025 release.

How good is it right now?

Measured on 2,062 English sentences the system never saw in training: the development set of the grammar's 2025 treebank (customer e-mails about electronics and Wikipedia articles on linguistics). For each sentence the treebank holds the analysis a human annotator chose, which we call gold.

73.3%of sentences get exactly the gold meaning (the same MRS)
88.7%get a complete analysis that the grammar accepts, with full structure and semantics
71.8%get exactly the gold tree

Meaning is the measure that matters most: different trees can express the same meaning, and the tree is there to get the meaning right. A sentence counts only if its whole semantic structure matches; partly correct analyses count as wrong here. These figures are for the fast pipeline with the best tagger so far, trained on the treebank and on grammar parses of literary and news text (the last Orca row of the table and the section on training data below).

More training data: grammar parses of other text

The treebank is small. Two corpora had already been parsed with the grammar by ACE, keeping its first-ranked analysis, for studies comparing human and LLM-written text: a literary corpus (excerpts from novels of 1838 to 1921, and four sets of LLM-written excerpts matched to them) and a news corpus (New York Times articles from 2023 and 2025, and eleven sets of LLM-written continuations). These analyses are not checked by an annotator. Each is replayed in Orca's engine, and only those that replay are used, so every one is an analysis the grammar licenses: all of the literary corpus, and 99.2% of the news corpus, which was parsed with a slightly different version of the grammar. Part of each corpus is kept out of training (30 excerpts in all five literary sets; the last tenth of each news set) for later tests on these corpora. The tagger is retrained with the same settings, and the full pipeline is measured on the same 2,062 development sentences.

From raw text

All the figures above start from the treebank's own tokens: the words after the grammar's preprocessing (tokenization, part-of-speech tags, and the grammar's token mapping rules), as stored with each gold analysis. For new text these tokens have to be made. ACE runs the grammar's preprocessing and stops before parsing, and prints its token chart; Orca reads the chart and keeps, for each word, every token the grammar proposes (a known word, a name, an unknown word), and replay picks the one the lexical entry accepts. This takes no parsing. As a check, the gold derivations of the development sentences were replayed over the tokens made from raw text.

The tokens made from raw text are as good as the treebank's for the grammar: the gold analyses replay over them with the same meaning. What changes is what the tagger sees. In the treebank, a word analyzed as a name keeps its capital letters and other words are lower-cased, so the form depends on the analysis. From raw text the analysis is not known yet; a word keeps its capitals when the grammar proposes a name reading and the lexicon does not have the word. Where this gives the treebank's forms, raw text does as well as the treebank's tokens. Nearly all of the loss is in the sentences where it does not, mostly words the lexicon has but the treebank analyzes as names ("Palm", "East", "Church"). Retraining the tagger on forms that do not depend on the analysis should remove most of this gap. The preprocessing step is a separate ACE process per sentence, about a third of the time; run in the same process it would cost much less. This is the pipeline behind the interactive demo (scripts/demo_server.py).

Collecting up to 30 accepted derivations costs much more than stopping at the first, and buys the gain from ranking. The LLM step is three orders of magnitude slower than the rest, which is why it is used only for the sentences the fast path cannot analyze.

Tested: an LLM that repairs derivations by reading the grammar

For a sentence where repair finds nothing, a language model (Claude Opus) is shown the rejected derivation and every point where replay fails, with the feature path and the two types that clash. It also gets the grammar's own definitions of the rules and types involved, and the lexical types the lexicon allows for each word. It works out from the definitions what the grammar requires and answers with replacement subtrees. The grammar replays every answer, so nothing is accepted on the model's word; if replay still fails, the new failure points go back to the model. Two versions were compared on a random sample of 60 of the 390 development sentences that repair could not analyze; the second was then run on all 390, and the first table includes that run.

Every sentence the first version got accepted, the second version got accepted too. Long sentences usually break in several independent places. The first version reported one place at a time, so after each fix replay stopped at the next one and three rounds ran out. Showing all of them at once is what made long sentences reachable. The analyses it finds for long sentences are close to gold in their parts but rarely identical as a whole: most predicates and links agree, yet one wrong attachment is enough to make the meaning differ. Usage is what the calls would cost through the API; they were made through a Claude subscription. Calls take about 20 seconds each, so this step is for the sentences the fast path cannot do. On all 390 sentences, short sentences did as well as in the sample and long ones somewhat worse. The full run reuses the 60 sample sentences and the completed part of an earlier run that the subscription's usage limit cut off; calls that hit the limit are not counted. Accepted repairs are not used as training data, since they come from the development set; the rules and types they change can instead become patterns for the ordinary repair step.

The same 390 sentences that repair leaves without an analysis, given instead to ACE (its time includes retries with more memory). For English, where a chart parser and its ranking model exist, ACE is the better fallback on both speed and accuracy. ACE also spends most of its total time on exactly these sentences, so with an ACE fallback Orca would be only modestly faster than ACE alone; the speed advantage depends on Orca itself failing less often. The plan is therefore to use the LLM step offline, for training and for expanding the grammar: on sentences of training or silver data that Orca cannot analyze, where its accepted repairs can become repair patterns or training examples; on sentences too long for ACE's memory; and to diagnose why the grammar rejects an analysis.

The steps

  1. TagEach word gets a leaf label (lexical type and unary rules) and a tree label.
  2. DecodeThe tree labels say how each word joins the next. Together they fix exactly one tree.
  3. Look upEach word's lexical entry is found in the grammar's lexicon from its type and rules.
  4. ReplayBottom-up, the grammar unifies each rule with its daughters. One pass, no search.
  5. RepairIf replay fails, the tagger's next-best labels under the failing node are tried. Up to 30 accepted derivations are collected.
  6. RankThe ranking model that ships with the grammar, the one ACE uses, picks the most likely of them.
  7. Read offThe result is the full sign. Its semantics is written out as MRS and DMRS.
phrasal rules leaf: lexical rules and lexical type node where replay fails node that differs between two trees

Reading a tree label: 1|hd-cmp_u_c means "the node that joins this word's phrase with what follows is one level deeper than the previous such node, and it is the rule hd-cmp_u_c". Negative numbers close levels.
