Orca: Omnilingual Replayable Constraint-based Analysis

How a sentence gets its analysis, on four real examples

A neural tagger proposes a complete derivation of the English Resource Grammar. The grammar then replays that derivation by unification and either accepts it or says where it breaks. An accepted derivation yields the full HPSG feature structure, and with it the semantics. No chart search is involved. The sentences are from the development set of the 2025 release.

How good is it right now?

Measured on 2,062 English sentences the system never saw in training: the development set of the grammar's 2025 treebank (customer e-mails about electronics and Wikipedia articles on linguistics). For each sentence the treebank holds the analysis a human annotator chose, which we call gold.

67.2%of sentences get exactly the gold meaning (the same MRS)
81.1%get a complete analysis that the grammar accepts, with full structure and semantics
58.4%get exactly the gold tree

Meaning is the measure that matters most: different trees can express the same meaning, and the tree is there to get the meaning right. A sentence counts only if its whole semantic structure matches; partly correct analyses count as wrong here.

"Partly right" counts the predicates and the semantic links of each analysis that also occur in the gold analysis, over the whole set; a sentence without an analysis contributes nothing, which lowers recall. Precision alone is the same for both systems: where Orca gives an analysis, 98.4% of its predicates and 96.2% of its links are in the gold analysis (ACE: 98.3% and 95.4%), and 82.9% of its analyses have exactly the gold meaning. The gap to ACE is mostly the 19% of sentences for which Orca finds no accepted derivation; an LLM step for those sentences is being tested (see below). Every Orca row requires each word to have an entry of the proposed type in the lexicon (or an unknown-word entry), as the parser does; an earlier version replayed a bare lexical type for such words, which accepted more sentences, often with readings the parser would never build. The ranking model is the one ACE uses, shipped with the grammar; it scores Orca's derivations exactly as ACE scores its own. ACE is the parser the treebank was made with: its analyses for these sentences include the gold one, and its ranking model chose the first-ranked one. The four sentences it could not analyze ran out of memory at 28 GB. The last row shows that when the engine is given the annotators' derivations it reproduces their semantics for every sentence, so the losses in the rows above come from the tagger's proposals. What is not yet measured: word segmentation is taken from the treebank, tense and number on variables are not compared, and long sentences do much worse than short ones (see example 4 below).

Orca and the ACE chart parser, side by side

ACE is the parser the treebank was built with. It searches a chart of all possible analyses and then ranks them with a statistical model. Orca proposes one derivation per sentence with the tagger and lets the grammar check it, so it never builds the chart.

ACE's average is dominated by its longest sentences: half of the sentences take under a twentieth of a second, while the hardest ran out of 28 GB of memory after building up to 230,000 chart edges. Orca's time grows roughly in proportion to sentence length. These are short sentences (about 12 words on average); the comparison on long sentences, where ACE often fails, is still to be made. Orca's 165 seconds are 18 for tagging on a GPU, 144 for replay and repair on one CPU core (29 on six cores), and 2 for ranking. Collecting several readings per sentence costs about three times as much as stopping at the first. ACE's total includes the retries of its 14 hardest sentences with more memory. Loading the grammar (about 17 seconds for Orca, 1 for ACE) is not included.

Being tested: an LLM that repairs derivations by reading the grammar

For a sentence where repair finds nothing, a language model (Claude Opus) is shown the rejected derivation and every point where replay fails, with the feature path and the two types that clash. It also gets the grammar's own definitions of the rules and types involved, and the lexical types the lexicon allows for each word. It works out from the definitions what the grammar requires and answers with replacement subtrees. The grammar replays every answer, so nothing is accepted on the model's word; if replay still fails, the new failure points go back to the model. This step has been tried on a random sample of 60 of the 390 development sentences that repair could not analyze. It is not part of the numbers above.

Every sentence the first version got accepted, the second version got accepted too. Long sentences usually break in several independent places. The first version reported one place at a time, so after each fix replay stopped at the next one and three rounds ran out. Showing all of them at once is what made long sentences reachable. The analyses it finds for long sentences are close to gold in their parts but rarely identical as a whole: most predicates and links agree, yet one wrong attachment is enough to make the meaning differ. Usage is what the calls would cost through the API; they were made through a Claude subscription. Calls take about 20 seconds each, so this step is for the sentences the fast path cannot do, and accepted repairs can become training data for the tagger on exactly those sentences.

The steps

  1. TagEach word gets a leaf label (lexical type and unary rules) and a tree label.
  2. DecodeThe tree labels say how each word joins the next. Together they fix exactly one tree.
  3. Look upEach word's lexical entry is found in the grammar's lexicon from its type and rules.
  4. ReplayBottom-up, the grammar unifies each rule with its daughters. One pass, no search.
  5. RepairIf replay fails, the tagger's next-best labels under the failing node are tried. Up to 30 accepted derivations are collected.
  6. RankThe ranking model that ships with the grammar, the one ACE uses, picks the most likely of them.
  7. Read offThe result is the full sign. Its semantics is written out as MRS and DMRS.
phrasal rules leaf: lexical rules and lexical type node where replay fails node that differs between two trees

Reading a tree label: 1|hd-cmp_u_c means "the node that joins this word's phrase with what follows is one level deeper than the previous such node, and it is the rule hd-cmp_u_c". Negative numbers close levels.
