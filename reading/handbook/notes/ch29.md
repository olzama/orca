# Chapter 29: HPSG and Categorial Grammar (Yusuke Kubota)

Handbook pages 1331-1394 (text 1331-1376, references from 1376). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter compares HPSG with Categorial Grammar (CG), written for readers who know HPSG. Section 2 introduces the two main variants of CG: Combinatory Categorial Grammar (CCG), a rule-based extension of the AB Grammar with Type Raising, Function Composition, Crossed Composition and Substitution, and Type-Logical Categorial Grammar (TLCG), in which grammar rules are inference rules of a logic and "hypothetical reasoning" plays the role that movement plays in transformational grammar. Section 3 compares the architectures: both theories are lexicalist and encode valence lexically, but HPSG keeps a rigid phrase-structure syntax and handles the complex mapping to meaning with underspecified semantics (MRS, LRS), while CG keeps a tight syntax-semantics mapping and makes syntax flexible. Section 4 compares analyses of long-distance dependencies, nonconstituent coordination, Gapping and Stripping, ellipsis, right-node raising, and binding. Section 5 has two pages on processing and implementation.

The chapter is a theory comparison. It does not mention DELPH-IN, the ERG, LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, derivation trees as stored objects, or Universal Dependencies. Its only statements on implementation are in Section 5 (pp. 1374-1375) and in a few asides (footnote 10, p. 1340; Section 4.2.2, p. 1366). Every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is therefore "not discussed" unless stated. The bearing on the parser is real, though, because most of Section 4 concerns phenomena where the published HPSG analyses use word order domains, surface deletion, or underspecified semantics, and those are the places where a continuous derivation tree with MRS as a by-product is under the most pressure.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Nonconstituent coordination: the main HPSG analyses use word order domains and surface deletion, and get quantifier scope only by stipulation

- Severity: high
- Assumption affected: continuity, leaves-equal-tokens, semantics
- Reference: Section 4.2.1, pp. 1361-1363; also Section 2.5.2, p. 1350 (examples 38-39) and Section 3.2, p. 1355
- What the chapter says: The two representative HPSG analyses, Beavers and Sag (2004) and Yatabe (2001; Yatabe and Tam 2021), both adopt linearization-based HPSG together with a version of MRS. Beavers and Sag treat argument cluster coordination and right-node raising as coordination of full clauses, with part of one conjunct "underlyingly present" and deleted in the prosodic representation (example 42: "Terry gave no man a book on Friday or [Terry gave no man] a record on Saturday"). In its simplest form this gives the wrong scope between the shared quantifier and the conjunction; Beavers and Sag add a constraint, Optional Quantifier Merger (43), which "does not follow from any general principle and is merely stipulated" (citing Levine 2011; Kubota and Levine 2015). Yatabe moves semantic composition from the signs on tree nodes to the list of domain objects, so that the "compaction" of two quantifiers into one domain object yields wide scope; the distributive reading then needs "a complex disjunctive constraint on semantic interpretation" that Yatabe and Tam "simply stipulate". The same problems recur for symmetrical predicates ("same", "different"), summative predicates ("a total of X") and "respective" readings (p. 1363). The chapter states that licensing the correct scope between quantifier and conjunction in NCC "remains a challenging problem in the HPSG literature" (p. 1350).
- Why it matters: In the Beavers and Sag analysis the combinatoric structure contains material that has no token in the string, and in both analyses the order of words is computed in a domain representation separate from the tree. Neither fits a continuous tree whose leaves are exactly the tokens. In Yatabe's analysis the meaning is not computed node by node at all, so MRS would not fall out of replaying a tree. (These consequences for the parser are my inference; the chapter does not discuss parsing here.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter does not say how any implemented grammar treats NCC.

### 2. Gapping and Stripping: scope anomalies, and the HPSG account relies on scope underspecification in the conjunction

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics, leaves-equal-tokens
- Reference: Section 4.2.2, pp. 1364-1366 (examples 44-47, footnote 24)
- What the chapter says: In "Mrs. J can't live in Boston and Mr. J in LA" the modal can scope over the conjunction, which is unexpected if the gapped conjunct is interpreted by supplying the missing material (examples 45a-c, including determiner gapping "No dog eats Whiskas or cat Alpo"). Kubota and Levine (2016a) note difficulties for the earlier (H)PSG accounts (Sag et al. 1985; Abeille et al. 2014). The HPSG analysis of Park et al. (2019) and Park (2019), in Lexical Resource Semantics, underspecifies in the lexical entries of "and" and "or" the relative scope of the conjunction and the modal of the first conjunct; determiner gapping is left for future work. Stripping shows the same anomaly (47; Puthawala 2018). There is disagreement on whether Gapping is restricted to coordination (footnote 24).
- Why it matters: The second conjunct has no verb token, so a tree over tokens must license a verbless clause by some rule, and the reading in which the auxiliary scopes over the coordination has to come from the semantics of the conjunction. The published HPSG solution is stated in LRS; the chapter does not say whether it carries over to MRS. (Inference about the parser is mine.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter does say that the Kubota and Levine TLCG analysis, with higher-order operators in semantics and prosody, "is likely to make efficient computational implementation less straightforward", and that the Park et al. approach is "more in line with the usual practice" of HPSG, "writing an explicit grammar fragment that is constraint-based and surface-oriented" (p. 1366).

### 3. Linearization-based HPSG separates word order from combinatorics

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: continuity, deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 3.3.1, pp. 1355-1357 (footnote 21); Section 2.2, p. 1334; Section 3.2, p. 1355
- What the chapter says: Linearization-based HPSG (Reape 1994; Mueller 1995; Kathol 2000) separates surface word order from the underlying combinatorics; the idea goes back to Curry's (1961) phenogrammar/tectogrammar distinction. In such analyses "the left-to-right order of elements in the proof tree does not necessarily correspond to the surface order of words" (p. 1334). The chapter describes HPSG as retaining "a rigid phrase structure-based syntax (modulo the flexibility entertained with the use of the linearization-based architecture)" (p. 1355). Linearization-based HPSG "is by no means monolithic": Yatabe and Tam (2021) do semantic composition at the level of word order domains (footnote 21). Kubota (2014) gives data from Japanese complex predicates that, according to him, call for close interaction between the two components; whether that criticism carries over to linearization-based HPSG is left open (p. 1357).
- Why it matters: A derivation in this style is a tree plus an order computed elsewhere, so the tree alone is not a recipe for the string. The planned tree encoding assumes continuous constituents. This matters most for the multilingual goal, since the phenomena cited (Dutch cross-serial dependencies, French clitic climbing, Japanese complex predicates) are outside English. (Inference about the parser is mine.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Right-node raising is not a unitary phenomenon and admits syntactic mismatches

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: continuity, leaves-equal-tokens, UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 4.2.4, pp. 1367-1368
- What the chapter says: RNR is not restricted to coordination. Abeille et al. (2016) and Shiraishi et al. (2019) show (with French data) that the raised material can mismatch syntactically with the selecting head in a non-adjacent conjunct. "The current literature seems to agree that RNR is not a unitary phenomenon, and that at least some type of RNR should be treated via a mechanism of surface ellipsis", modeled as deletion of syntactic or prosodic objects or by an anaphoric mechanism (Chaves 2014).
- Why it matters: Under a surface-ellipsis account the shared material is licensed by a head it does not combine with in the tree, and with mismatches it would not even unify with that head's requirement. One rule and one tree shape for all RNR would not match the theory. For the UD bridge, the shared dependent has two governors. (Inference about the parser and UD is mine.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Scope is underspecified in the semantics; a derivation does not fix scope readings

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 3.2, pp. 1354-1355; Section 2.5.2, pp. 1348-1350
- What the chapter says: Recent HPSG work on the syntax-semantics interface "makes heavy use of underspecification" (MRS, Copestake et al. 2005; LRS, Richter and Sailer 2004). The contrast with CG is that in TLCG the two scope readings of "Someone talked to everyone today" correspond to two derivations of the same string (example 35), while HPSG keeps one syntactic structure and leaves scope open in the semantic representation.
- Why it matters: This fits the design (one tree, one underspecified MRS), with the limit that the MRS obtained by replay is a description of readings, and that the constraints which narrow the readings in hard cases (caveats 1 and 2) are extra stipulations or are stated in LRS.
- DELPH-IN stance: MRS is named as one of the two underspecification languages used in HPSG (p. 1355); no implementation is discussed.

### 6. Ellipsis is treated as anaphora through constructional schemata that refer to the context

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: semantics, deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 4.2.3, pp. 1366-1367
- What the chapter says: HPSG and CG both treat ellipsis as a form of anaphora with no invisible structure at the ellipsis site. Recent HPSG analyses (Ginzburg and Sag 2000: Chapter 8; Miller 2014) rely on constructions; syntactic connectivity effects are captured by schemata that encode syntactic information of the antecedent, for example the SAL-UTT feature in Ginzburg and Sag's analysis of sluicing. HPSG "is not equipped with an analogous abstract combinatoric component that assigns 'constituent' status to expressions that do not ... correspond to constituents in the surface representation". No concrete HPSG analysis of the interaction of VP ellipsis and extraction exists, as far as the author knows.
- Why it matters: A fragment or sluice is licensed by a schema whose constraints mention the antecedent utterance. Replay of a single sentence has no antecedent to unify with, so the resolved content cannot be a by-product of the derivation of that sentence alone. (Inference mine.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Long-distance dependencies: one filler with several gaps, two kinds of nonlocal feature, and islands left out of the grammar

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge, deterministic-replay, no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 2.4.2, pp. 1340-1343; Section 4.1, pp. 1358-1361
- What the chapter says: (a) CCG assumes no empty element at the gap site, "like (many versions of) HPSG" (p. 1340); the wording implies that some versions of HPSG do have one. (b) Parasitic gaps (25) need one filler for several gaps; this is a technical problem for CCG (Substitution rule) and TLCG (resource sensitivity). (c) Pied-piping in HPSG involves two types of long-distance dependency, REL and SLASH (Pollard and Sag 1994: Chapter 5), and the chapter sees a possible advantage for HPSG in "a general PS rule or constructional schema for licensing pied-piping relative clauses" (p. 1361). (d) Recent HPSG analyses "explicitly avoid directly encoding major island constraints within the grammar" (Sag 2010; Chaves 2012b) (p. 1359). (e) Extraction pathway marking has received much attention in HPSG (Levine and Hukari 2006).
- Why it matters: (b) gives dependency graphs in which one filler has two governors, which a UD tree cannot show without enhanced dependencies. (d) means a successful replay does not imply acceptability with respect to islands. (a) is a reminder that the no-empty-elements assumption is a choice among HPSG versions. (Inferences about UD and replay are mine.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Phrasal constructions that cannot be lexicalized

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding, binary-unary-rules
- Reference: Section 3.1, pp. 1353-1354
- What the chapter says: Constructional HPSG (Sag 1997; Ginzburg and Sag 2000) and SBCG capture patterns that are hard to lexicalize with a family of phrase-structure schemata organized in a type inheritance hierarchy. Many phenomena first used as evidence for constructions can be lexicalized (Mueller and Wechsler 2014), but "there remain some real challenges for a strictly lexicalist approach", for example the "N after N" construction. Whether such mechanisms should have a central role in the architecture "is still a highly controversial issue".
- Why it matters: The rule inventory that the tagger's labels must name is open-ended and partly language-specific, and the split between what a lexical type carries and what a rule name carries differs between analyses. The chapter does not say that such constructions are more than binary. (Inference mine.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Unary rules are a known parsing cost

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules, tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 2.4.2, p. 1340, footnote 10
- What the chapter says: Recent CCG (Steedman 2012: 80) treats Type Raising as an operation in the lexicon and no longer as a syntactic rule; "this choice seems to be motivated by parsing considerations (so as to eliminate as many unary rules as possible from the syntax)". Geach rules in Jacobson's fragment are "likely to have similar computational consequences".
- Why it matters: It is a parallel to our decision to fold the chain of unary and lexical rules into the token label, and a warning that unary chains are where the label space grows. The statement is about CCG, not HPSG.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 10. Lexical integrity is not assumed by all HPSG analyses

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens
- Reference: Section 3.1, p. 1352, footnote 16
- What the chapter says: Not all variants of HPSG or CG subscribe to the Lexical Integrity Hypothesis. In HPSG, Gunji (1999) analyzes Japanese causatives without it, in contrast to the strictly lexicalist analysis of Manning et al. (1999).
- Why it matters: For languages such as Japanese, what counts as a leaf (a word or a morpheme that combines in syntax) depends on the analysis chosen. (Inference mine.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 11. Binding conditions: argument-structure based for Principles A and B, configurational for Principle C

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 4.3, pp. 1368-1374
- What the chapter says: The HPSG Binding Theory (Pollard and Sag 1992; 1994: Chapter 6) states Principles A and B on argument structure, using the obliqueness hierarchy and syntactic indices, while Principle C refers to the configurational structure and so "has a somewhat exceptional status within the whole theory (which otherwise adheres to strict locality conditions)" (p. 1373).
- Why it matters: Principles A and B are local constraints of the kind replay can check. Principle C is a non-local condition on the whole tree; the chapter gives no indication that it would be checked by rule application. Coreference constraints are also not something the chapter presents as part of the underspecified semantic representation. (Inference mine.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 12. Case syncretism and feature neutralization need a richer type hierarchy

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (feature-structure engine)
- Reference: Section 3.3.2, pp. 1357-1358 (footnote 22)
- What the chapter says: The CG treatment of feature neutralization with meet and join connectives (Morrill 1994; Bayer 1996; Bayer and Johnson 1995) was recast in HPSG with inheritance hierarchies by Levy (2001), Levy and Pollard (2002) and Daniels (2002), first introduced by Levine, Hukari and Calcagno (2001).
- Why it matters: The replay engine's type hierarchy must be able to express such neutralized values for languages with case syncretism; plain atomic case values with unification would reject coordinations that need a syncretic form. (Inference mine.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- HPSG is surface-oriented, and this is close to CCG. "HPSG (at least the 'bare-bones' version of it) and CCG are more similar to each other than they are to TLCG in being surface-oriented" (Section 5, p. 1374). The abstract devices that would not fit a tree of rule names over tokens (hypothetical reasoning, prosodic lambda binding, higher-order operators) belong to TLCG, and the chapter itself says they make computational implementation less straightforward (Section 4.2.2, p. 1366).
- No empty element at the gap in (many versions of) HPSG; extraction is passed up node by node through SLASH, a dedicated feature, parallel to the CCG chain of Function Composition (Section 2.4.2, pp. 1340-1341; Section 4.1, p. 1358). This is information that local rule application can carry, which is what replay needs.
- Local dependencies (passive, raising, control, complex predicates) are handled by sharing valence information in the lexicon, with no movement and no empty categories (Section 3.1, p. 1352). This puts the work in the lexical type, which is what the tagger predicts.
- HPSG valence features are "closely tied to the specific phrase structure rules ... that dictate the ways in which hierarchical representations are built" (Section 3.1, pp. 1352-1353). A derivation is a hierarchical object built by named schemata, unlike a TLCG proof, which "a principle of grammar" cannot refer to (Section 4.3, p. 1368).
- HPSG keeps one rigid phrase structure and puts ambiguity of scope into an underspecified semantic representation such as MRS (Section 3.2, p. 1355). One tree then gives one MRS, with no need to enumerate derivations per reading.
- Ellipsis has no invisible hierarchical structure in HPSG (Section 4.2.3, p. 1366), which is consistent with leaves being tokens.
- CCG moved Type Raising into the lexicon to remove unary rules from syntax for parsing reasons (footnote 10, p. 1340): precedent for packing unary steps into the lexical label.
- Linguistically informed parsers (HPSG and CCG) are "attracting some renewed interest" through "the new trend of combining the insights of statistical approaches and linguistically-informed approaches", and an explicit logical meaning representation is the attraction (Section 5, p. 1375).
- The grammar need not encode island constraints (Sag 2010; Chaves 2012b; Section 4.1, p. 1359), which keeps the rule set that replay must apply simpler.

## Theory strains

1. Nonconstituent coordination and scope (Section 4.2.1, pp. 1361-1363; Section 2.5.2, p. 1350). Beavers and Sag's deletion analysis needs the stipulated Optional Quantifier Merger; Yatabe and Tam's compaction analysis needs a stipulated disjunctive constraint for the distributive reading. CCG and TLCG treat the same strings as ordinary constituents with a compositional meaning. The chapter frames the dispute as one about whether an analysis should explain or merely account for the generalizations.
2. Where semantic composition happens (Section 4.2.1, p. 1362; footnote 21, p. 1357). Standard HPSG composes meaning on the signs at tree nodes; Yatabe composes it on word order domains. This is a disagreement inside HPSG about the basic architecture.
3. Gapping (Section 4.2.2, pp. 1364-1366). Earlier (H)PSG accounts (Sag et al. 1985; Abeille et al. 2014) have difficulties with the scope anomaly; Park et al. (2019) repair this by lexical scope underspecification but leave determiner gapping open; Kubota and Levine use constituent coordination with higher-order operators. Also disputed: whether Gapping is restricted to coordination (footnote 24).
4. Rigid syntax with underspecified semantics versus flexible syntax with tight semantics (Section 3.2, pp. 1354-1355). The chapter presents this as the central architectural difference between HPSG and CG, and notes that CG itself has no consensus on strict compositionality.
5. Constructions versus lexicalism (Section 3.1, pp. 1353-1354; footnote 16, p. 1352). Whether phrasal constructions deserve a central place is "highly controversial"; "N after N" resists lexicalization; the Lexical Integrity Hypothesis is not accepted by all (Gunji 1999 versus Manning et al. 1999; Bruening 2018 versus Mueller 2018 and Mueller and Wechsler 2014).
6. Right-node raising (Section 4.2.4, pp. 1367-1368). No single mechanism covers it; at least part of it is surface ellipsis, and it shows syntactic mismatches. Summative agreement and extraposed relatives with split antecedents are called "puzzling idiosyncrasies" (p. 1363).
7. Ellipsis (Section 4.2.3, pp. 1366-1367). Syntactic sensitivity is the classic argument for covert structure; HPSG answers with construction-specific features such as SAL-UTT; there is no HPSG analysis of the interaction of ellipsis and extraction.
8. Binding (Section 4.3, pp. 1368-1374). Principle C is the one place where the HPSG Binding Theory refers to configuration, against the theory's locality; its status as a grammatical principle is itself controversial (Thai and Vietnamese lack the effect; a pragmatic account is possible). Branco (2002) is cited as an alternative view.
9. Islands (Section 4.1, p. 1359). Whether island constraints belong in the grammar is "a major open question"; recent HPSG leaves them out, CCG and Displacement Calculus build them in.
10. Pied-piping (Section 4.1, pp. 1360-1361, footnote 23). HPSG needs two nonlocal features (REL and SLASH); which categories can be pied-piped is "a rather thorny issue".
11. Phenogrammar and tectogrammar (Section 3.3.1, pp. 1356-1357). How closely the word order component and the combinatorics must interact is open; Kubota's Japanese data are said to be a problem for a clean separation, possibly including linearization-based HPSG.
12. Competence and performance (Section 5, pp. 1374-1375; Section 4.2.2, p. 1366). HPSG and CCG aim for a transparent relation; TLCG accepts an indirect one for the sake of theoretical elegance.

## Not covered here

All items in this section are my own remarks, not statements from the chapter.

- The chapter does not mention DELPH-IN, the ERG, LKB, ACE, TRALE or Enju, and says nothing about how implemented HPSG grammars treat nonconstituent coordination, Gapping, right-node raising or ellipsis. Whether the ERG handles these with continuous binary rules, and what MRS it produces for them, has to be checked in the grammar itself.
- The chapter does not mention CCG supertagging, although it cites Clark and Curran (2007). Supertagging is the closest established relative of our tagging step, and the CCG literature on it would be the natural comparison for the tree encoding.
- The chapter does not mention CCGbank-style or HPSG-to-dependency conversions, nor Universal Dependencies. The remarks above on shared dependents (right-node raising, parasitic gaps) and UD are my inferences.
- "Spurious ambiguity" in CCG (many derivations for one reading, a consequence of Type Raising and Composition) is not discussed as a parsing issue. It is relevant by contrast: an HPSG derivation tree is closer to one tree per reading, which helps a tagger trained on derivations.
- The chapter does not discuss the formal power of CCG versus HPSG (mild context-sensitivity versus unrestricted unification), which bears on whether replay without search is always sufficient.
- The Japanese and German word order phenomena that motivate word order domains are only pointed to here; Chapter 10 (constituent order) is the place to read on them.
