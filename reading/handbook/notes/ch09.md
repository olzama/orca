# Chapter 9: Argument structure and linking (Anthony R. Davis, Jean-Pierre Koenig, Stephen Wechsler)

Handbook pages 315-367 (text 315-358, references 358-367). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter presents ARG-ST as an autonomous lexical representation that sits between lexical semantics (CONTENT) and the valence lists (SUBJ, COMPS, SPR). It covers two mappings: argument realization (ARG-ST to valence, with systematic exceptions for affixal, null, and extracted arguments, syntactic ergativity, and symmetrical objects) and linking (CONTENT to ARG-ST, stated as constraints on lexical types over a small set of proto-role attributes ACT, UND, SOA, with a KEY predication chosen among MRS-style elementary predications). It then treats argument alternations as meaning differences or KEY shifts, shows that the passive resists that treatment and has no agreed analysis, and surveys extensions of ARG-ST (resultatives, clause union, adverbs as complements, the DEPS list). It closes with the claim that ARG-ST may not be universal (Oneida) and with arguments for the lexical approach to argument structure over phrasal approaches.

The chapter is theoretical and lexicon-centered. It says nothing about parsing and almost nothing about implemented grammars: the only links are the use of an MRS-inspired CONTENT (Copestake et al. 2001, 2005) and one remark on a strategy of the CoreGram project (Müller 2015). Its bearing on the parser is mostly on the lexical side of the tag (lexical type plus lexical-rule chain), on the semantics, and on the UD bridge.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Non-canonical argument realization may leave no trace in the derivation tree

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 318-319; Section 3.1, pp. 320-321; Section 3.4 summary, p. 326
- What the chapter says: The Argument Realization Principle (ARG-ST = SUBJ + COMPS; Sag et al. 2003) is an "idealization". Signs that violate it (affixal arguments, extracted arguments, null anaphora) "have been approached in two ways". In one, a lexical rule takes a canonical entry and removes items from the valence lists, adding an affix or placing the item on SLASH. In the other, "a feature of each ARG-ST list item specifies whether the item is subject to the ARP" (canonical versus non-canonical signs, Bouma et al. 2001), with no rule involved.
- Why it matters: under the lexical-rule approach, each non-canonical realization is a named unary step, which is exactly what the tag encodes and replay applies. Under the feature-based approach, the choice (realized, extracted, affixal, dropped) is a resolution of an underspecified lexical constraint and has no node in the tree. Replay then succeeds only if later rule applications force the choice by unification; otherwise the engine would have to guess. A grammar written in the second style needs either a rule-based recoding or tags that distinguish the lexical variants.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter points to Davis and Koenig (Chapter 4), Miller and Sag (1997), and Bouma et al. (2001).

### 2. Arguments with no token: affixal pronouns, null anaphora, implicit agents

- Severity: medium for the multi-language goal, low for English
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / UD-bridge / semantics (and supports no-empty-elements, see below)
- Reference: Section 3.1, pp. 320-321 (Spanish *hablo*, ex. 5; Japanese null object, ex. 6); Section 4.5, pp. 335-336 (implicit agent of short passive controls a rationale clause, ex. 24); Section 5.3, pp. 348-349 (inert logical subject)
- What the chapter says: A pronominal affix contributes pronominal content to the verb; the argument is on ARG-ST (and subject to binding theory) but absent from SUBJ. Null anaphors are "present in ARG-ST but absent from valence lists" and receive the binding type of ordinary pronouns (ppro), a language-specific option captured "by valence and ARG-ST defaults in the lexical hierarchy". The implicit agent of a short passive can control a rationale clause, which suggests it is on ARG-ST, while the middle has no such argument.
- Why it matters: the MRS will contain arguments (sometimes pronoun-like, sometimes syntactically active as controllers) that correspond to no token and to no UD node, so a conversion in either direction has to create or discard them. The chapter's mention of "defaults" for the pro-drop option is also relevant if the engine supports only monotonic unification.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Passive: no agreed analysis, and the by-phrase has three competing statuses

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics / UD-bridge / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 5.3, pp. 344-350; Section 5.4, pp. 350-351
- What the chapter says: Two analyses are laid out. The "non-canonical linking" analysis (Pollard and Sag 1987; Manning et al. 1999; Müller 2003; rule 42) removes the first ARG-ST item or re-adds it last as an optional PP[by]. The "non-canonical argument realization" analysis (Davis 2001; rule 43) keeps the active ARG-ST and changes the mapping to valence, which then needs an "inert" or null index for the logical subject, introduced "by stipulation". The by-phrase is treated as an oblique complement on ARG-ST, as an adjunct (Höhle 1978; Müller 2003; Jackendoff 1990), or as a "thematically bound adjunct" on SLASH (Sanfilippo 1998). Binding data (ex. 44, 47, 48) and control data (ex. 24, 45) pull in different directions. "There is as yet no consensus within the HPSG community on the correct analysis of passives."
- Why it matters: (a) the tag must encode the passive lexical rule and, where the by-PP is an optional complement, which variant applies; (b) whether the by-phrase attaches by a head-complement or a head-adjunct rule changes the derivation tree, the route by which the agent role is filled in the MRS, and the HPSG-to-UD mapping for the agent phrase; (c) the non-canonical realization analysis yields two ARG-ST members for one semantic argument in long passives, which a dependency extractor reading ARG-ST would have to collapse. Grammars for different languages may make different choices, so the UD bridge cannot assume one passive shape.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The only implementation-related remark is that restricting linking constraints to basic (underived) lexemes "is the strategy adopted by the CoreGram project, Müller 2015" (p. 347), with a refinement by Müller (p. c. 2021) that linking constraints must apply to the output of lexical rules that alter semantics (causatives, applicatives) but not to passives.

### 4. Lexical rules feed each other; the lexical-rule chain can be long and varied

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / binary-unary-rules
- Reference: Section 1, p. 316; Section 5.3, pp. 345-346; Section 6, pp. 351-353; Section 8, pp. 356-358
- What the chapter says: Diathesis alternations, voice, and category-changing derivation are "all captured within the lexicon", by lexical rules or by the lexical type hierarchy. Rules shown or cited include the passive (42, 43), the resultative rule that adds an NP and AP and a cause-rel (Müller 2002; rule 54), the rule adding a negative adverb to ARG-ST (56), plus antipassive, causative, and applicative in other languages. "One lexical rule can feed another": verbal passive feeds adjective formation (57b, c). Levin (1993) lists around 50 alternations for English (p. 338).
- Why it matters: this is the "chain of unary/lexical rules" part of the label. The chapter confirms that the chain is real and can be several steps deep, and that many alternations are instead distinct lexical types (see caveat 9), so both the chain vocabulary and the lexical-type vocabulary of the tagger grow with coverage. Nothing here breaks the tree encoding; it bears on label-space size and sparsity.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter notes that lexical rules can be given a feature-based, non-procedural representation with a LEX-DTR attribute (Meurers 2001), which is the unary-rule view the replay engine assumes.

### 5. Argument composition and adverbs as complements: valence that is not fixed in the lexical entry

- Severity: medium (clause union: other languages; adverb-complements: claimed for English negation too)
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / UD-bridge / semantics
- Reference: Section 6, pp. 351-353 (ex. 50-52, constraints 53, 55, 56); Section 8, p. 356
- What the chapter says: In Romance clause union, the ARG-ST of the higher verb includes the syntactic arguments of its lexical verbal complement, by list composition (Hinrichs and Nakazawa 1994; constraint 55); these entries "are simply stipulated". Abeillé and Godard (1997) treat many French adverbs and negative elements as complements of the verb, and Kim and Sag (2002) extend this to some uses of English negation; such elements are semantic modifiers (they select the verb through MOD) and are nonetheless on ARG-ST. Bouma et al. (2001) add a DEPS list that appends an arbitrary list of adverbials to ARG-ST (constraint 53). Rule (56) uses the "shuffle" relation to insert the adverb anywhere in ARG-ST. Section 8 lists further "fates" of a verb's arguments: raising or argument composition transfers them to another head; partial fronting saves them for another position.
- Why it matters: (a) a lexical entry whose ARG-ST contains an open tail shared with another word's ARG-ST, or an open list of adverbials, has a length that is fixed only when the rules apply; replay handles this by unification if the tree is right, but there is no local check that the tag is right. (b) Shuffle is a relational constraint with several solutions, which plain unification does not resolve; the engine needs either a deterministic encoding or an extra bit in the tag. (c) For the UD bridge, dependents of the lower verb are complements of the higher verb in the derivation, and an adverb or negation is a complement in syntax while a modifier in semantics, so a head-complement daughter does not always map to a core UD relation. (d) The MRS for an adverb-complement has to come out as modification even though the rule is head-complement.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Subject is not always the first ARG-ST item: syntactic ergativity and voice systems

- Severity: low for English, medium for the multi-language goal and the relaxed fallback
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / semantics
- Reference: Section 3.3, pp. 323-325 (Balinese, ex. 7-11)
- What the chapter says: In Balinese Objective Voice the subject is any term except the ARG-ST-initial one; in Agentive Voice it is the initial one. Voice does not change ARG-ST order, and binding follows ARG-ST, not valence. Tagalog, Inuit, some Mayan languages, Chukchi, Toba Batak, Tsimshian languages, and Nadëb are listed as having similar alternative mappings (Manning 1996; Manning et al. 1999).
- Why it matters: any "coarse universal schema" or UD conversion that reads subject = first argument = agent is language-particular. The UD relation has to be read from the valence list a daughter saturates, and the semantic role from ARG-ST/CONTENT, as two separate sources.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Partially ordered ARG-ST (symmetrical objects)

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / other (list-based engine)
- Reference: Section 3.4, pp. 325-326 (Moro, ex. 12-13)
- What the chapter says: Ackerman, Malouf and Moore (2013, 2017) propose that the two objects of a Moro ditransitive are unordered on ARG-ST, which allows two mappings to COMPS; the string in (12) is ambiguous between the two role assignments.
- Why it matters: a partial order is not a list, so a list-based feature-structure engine needs two lexical variants (or a disjunction), and the tag must choose between them with no string evidence. The two derivations differ only in semantics.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Lexical decomposition and sublexical scope: semantics not fully fixed by the tree

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 4.1, p. 330; Section 4.5, pp. 334-335 (ex. 23); Section 4.6, pp. 336-337 (ex. 26); Section 5.1, pp. 339-340 (ex. 29-30)
- What the chapter says: CONTENT is a set of elementary predications "in a way similar to and inspired by Minimal Recursion Semantics", with one designated as KEY for linking; modal information is factored into SIT-CORE and MODAL-BASE (Koenig and Davis 2001). Decompositions are justified partly by sublexical scope: *again* in (23) can modify the whole buying event or only the resulting possess-rel inside the meaning of *buy*.
- Why it matters: (a) a single derivation tree corresponds to two readings of *again*, so the derivation is a recipe for an underspecified semantics at best, and only if the lexical entry exposes the inner predication; (b) the chapter's CONTENT geometry (KEY, RELS with several predications per verb, SIT-CORE, MODAL-BASE, proto-role attributes ACT/UND/SOA) differs from a one-predicate-per-verb semantics, so MRS obtained from replay will not contain these distinctions unless the grammar's lexicon is built that way.
- DELPH-IN stance: the chapter does not describe DELPH-IN grammars. It says only that Koenig and Davis (2006) "borrow from" MRS (Copestake et al. 2001, 2005) and add the KEY attribute.

### 9. Alternations as distinct lexical entries: many near-identical lexical types

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 5.1, pp. 338-341 (*spray*, ex. 27-30; causative-inchoative, ex. 31; direct-oblique, ex. 32); Section 5.2, pp. 342-344 (*substitute*/*replace*, ex. 34-38; source/final product, ex. 39-40)
- What the chapter says: Many alternants are separate lexical entries related through the type hierarchy, with different CONTENT or the same CONTENT and a different KEY. The authors avoid a lexical rule for (40) because a rule "requires selecting one alternant as basic".
- Why it matters: these alternants show up only as different lexical types in the tag, with no rule node, and the tagger must pick the alternant from context. Some pairs differ only in fine semantics (32c), so tag confusions there are harmless for syntax and visible only in the MRS.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 10. Shared dependents in lexical coordination

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / semantics
- Reference: Section 8, pp. 357-358 (ex. 58)
- What the chapter says: Two verbs with the same valence can be coordinated at the V0 level and "share their dependents", and a dependent can receive different semantic roles from the two verbs (*saddled and mounted his trusted horse*).
- Why it matters: the derivation is an ordinary continuous binary tree (supportive), but one token fills argument slots of two predicates. The MRS reflects this through shared indices; a basic UD tree gives the dependent one head, so the HPSG-to-UD direction loses information and the UD-to-HPSG direction has to recover the sharing.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 11. ARG-ST items without a semantic role; semantic arguments without an NP

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / semantics
- Reference: Section 2, p. 319; Section 3.3, p. 324; Section 4.1, p. 330; Section 4.2, pp. 331-332; Section 4.3, p. 333
- What the chapter says: ARG-ST includes raised phrases and expletives, which bear no role of the selecting verb. Conversely, some verbs with ACT and UND realize the undergoer as a PP (*impinge on*, *tinker with*). Selected prepositions are sometimes arbitrary (*hanker after*, *yearn for*) and often contentful (instrumental *with* contributing a use-rel, linked from RELS, constraint 20).
- Why it matters: a syntactic dependency does not imply a semantic argument relation and vice versa, and a selected PP may or may not contribute its own predication to the MRS. The UD bridge and any evaluation that compares UD relations with MRS arguments must allow for this.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 12. ARG-ST may not be universal

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (universal relaxed schemas; multi-language goal)
- Reference: Section 7, pp. 354-355
- What the chapter says: Koenig and Michelson (2014, 2015a, b) argue that Oneida has none of the properties that motivate ARG-ST; the only intermediate representation is INFL-STR, a list of semantic indices for (at most two) animate arguments that determines verbal prefixes. There is no syntactic agreement and no selection of dependent phrases through synsem objects.
- Why it matters: universal fallback schemas built on head-complement and head-subject saturation would have little to work with in such a language, and the relation between a verb and co-occurring nominals would not be valence saturation.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- No empty elements. "HPSG eschews the use of silent formatives like 'small pro' when there is no evidence for such items"; null and affixal arguments live on ARG-ST only (Section 3.1, p. 321). Extraction is likewise described as an ARG-ST item placed on SLASH and removed from valence, with no trace mentioned (pp. 320-321, 326).
- Argument structure is lexical. All diathesis, voice, and category-changing processes are in the lexicon, as lexical rules or lexical types (Section 1, p. 316; Section 8, pp. 355-358). A token label made of a lexical type plus a chain of lexical rules is therefore the right place to record them, and the phrase-structure part of the derivation stays small: "The same rules of syntax dictate the position of the subject, whether the verb is active or passive" (p. 357).
- Lexical rules as unary structures. The chapter writes lexical rules as feature structures with a LEX-DTR attribute and no procedural content (Meurers 2001; p. 345), matching the unary-rule replay model.
- Valence lists drive combination. SUBJ and COMPS "indicate its requirements for syntactic concatenation with dependents" (p. 319); the vocabulary is concatenative, in line with the continuity assumption. Nothing in this chapter uses order domains.
- Linking is declarative and lexical. Linking constraints are constraints on lexical types in the hierarchy (16-18, 20), with "no resort to additional mechanisms such as a thematic hierarchy or numerical comparison of entailments" (p. 332). They are resolved in the lexicon and add no search at replay time.
- MRS-style semantics. The linking theory presented as current (Koenig and Davis 2006) uses a flat set of elementary predications inspired by MRS (pp. 330, 339), so MRS as the by-product of replay is compatible with the chapter's own semantic assumptions.
- Lexical coordination is "simple V0 coordination" (p. 357), an ordinary binary structure.
- ARG-ST as a dependency source (my reading of the chapter, not a claim it makes): the ordered ARG-ST list with obliqueness ranking (subject, objects, obliques, predicative and clausal complements; Section 3.2, p. 322) gives a per-head list of dependents with a grammatical-function ranking, which is the kind of information a UD conversion needs.

## Theory strains

- Passive (Section 5.3, pp. 344-350; 5.4, pp. 350-351). Two analyses, each violating a constraint "that otherwise [has] strong support" (linking constraint 16 or the ARP). Open questions the authors list for both: how strong the violated constraint remains, what follows for binding, control, and discourse, and what the by-phrase is. "The implications of weakening canonical constraints under each of these analyses have not been thoroughly addressed"; no proposal limits non-canonical linking to passives; the inert-index analyses "have not been pursued"; cross-linguistic variation in binding and control under passive has had no systematic HPSG investigation. Explicitly: no consensus.
- Status of the by-phrase (pp. 348-350). Oblique on ARG-ST versus adjunct versus thematically bound adjunct on SLASH (Sanfilippo 1998). Binding by the by-phrase (ex. 47, 48) is problematic for the adjunct view; Russian subject-oriented *sebja* needs access to the active SUBJ or to an inert first ARG-ST item.
- Where linking constraints apply (p. 347). Options: only NP arguments; only lexemes, not words (Runner and Aranovich 2003); only underived lexemes (CoreGram), which runs into causatives and applicatives; Müller's (p. c.) repair that semantics-changing rules are re-linked.
- Stipulated inertness (pp. 348-349). A subtype of index (inert/null) that "by stipulation" cannot correspond to a discourse referent, or an A-INDEX of type null that "by stipulation" neither o-commands nor appears on valence lists (Davis 2001, after Kathol 1999).
- ARP exceptions: lexical rules versus a per-item feature (Section 3.1, p. 321). Two coexisting mechanisms for the same facts (extraction, clitics, pro-drop).
- History of the valence representation (Section 2, pp. 317-319; Section 3.4, p. 326). SUBCAT (Pollard and Sag 1987, 1994 Chapters 1-8) proved inadequate for "subject" (Pollard and Sag 1994 Chapter 9; German subjectless clauses), was split into SUBJ and COMPS, and ARG-ST was then reintroduced as a single list for binding. The ARG-ST/valence relation "was soon loosened", and ARG-ST "has now acquired an autonomous status", so the same arguments are represented two or three times with mappings that vary by language and construction.
- Total versus partial ordering of ARG-ST (Section 3.4, pp. 325-326).
- How much of linking is semantic (Section 4.3, pp. 332-333). Semantic determinism (UTAH, Baker 1988; Levin 1993) versus partly arbitrary subcategorization (Pollard and Sag 1987; Davis 2001): *spare*/*deprive*, *wait for*/*await*, raising, expletives, cross-linguistic "relational profiles" (Gerdts 1992). The chapter's conclusion is that "independent stipulations of subcategorization appear to be needed" (p. 337). Arbitrary preposition selection (*hanker after*, **yearn after*) is stipulated per entry (p. 331).
- Semantic vocabulary for linking (Sections 4.1, 4.4, pp. 327-334). Thematic role hierarchies (rejected: no consensus inventory, THEME undefined, arguments with no role, two roles, or shared roles) versus Dowty's proto-role counting versus HPSG's decomposition with ACT/UND/SOA. The truth conditions of act-und-rel are an open-ended disjunction ("or ..." in 15). Constraint (17) holds only for a class of verbs; *impinge on*, *tinker with* have an UND that is not an NP (p. 330). Koenig and Davis (2003) offer a different formulation with one attribute per entailment (p. 331).
- Embedded versus flat decomposition (Section 5.1, p. 339). Davis (1996, 2001) derive ARG-ST order from depth of semantic embedding; "the resulting complexity of some of the semantic representations raises valid questions about what independent evidence supports them". Koenig and Davis (2006) use flat RELS plus KEY. The semantic side of linking "has often been devised with an eye to syntax", with "a risk of stipulation".
- Choice of KEY (p. 330; Section 5.2, pp. 342-343). The KEY "becomes an issue demanding consideration"; for *substitute*/*replace* the two verbs have equivalent semantics and differ only in which predication is KEY, which is a lexical stipulation doing the work of the alternation.
- Beavers' Morphosyntactic Alignment Principle (Section 5.1, p. 341). It reintroduces comparison of entailment sets; the ordering of the ROLES list "is not fully worked out", and assessing which role is stronger "requires some additional mechanisms within HPSG that are not spelled out".
- Lexical rules need a direction (Section 5.2, p. 344). Choosing a basic alternant is "not always an easy decision" (causative versus inchoative; morphology points different ways in different languages), so the authors use related entries without a rule.
- Adjuncts on ARG-ST versus DEPS (Section 6, pp. 352-353). Modifiers realized as complements (French adverbs, English negation, French *en* with *mourir*) force either an extended ARG-ST, with the problem of their relative obliqueness, or an extra DEPS list "if one countenances such an additional list"; "not all verb modifiers can be part of the DEPS list"; footnote 5 notes possible binding conflicts when adjuncts in the nominal domain are treated this way (Müller 1999; Chapter 20). Clause-union entries are "simply stipulated".
- Universality of ARG-ST (Section 7, pp. 354-355). Oneida, if Koenig and Michelson are right.
- Lexical versus phrasal argument structure (Section 8, pp. 355-358). Against Construction Grammar (Goldberg), little v and Distributed Morphology, and Hale and Keyser (1993): lexical rules feed one another, and coordinated verbs share dependents with different roles. The question whether ARG-ST should also apply to phrases or constructions is raised on p. 338.
- Competition with GB/Minimalism on binding (Section 3.3, p. 325 and footnote 2). Configurational binding lacks an analogue of ARG-ST; Balinese needs the subject position to be both an A-bar and an A position (Wechsler 1999).
- Demotion versus advancement (p. 345). Passive as subject demotion (Blevins 2003) against Relational Grammar's object advancement (Perlmutter and Postal 1983).

## Not covered here

All items in this section are my own remarks, not claims of the chapter.

- The chapter never says how any implemented grammar (ERG, other DELPH-IN grammars, TRALE grammars, Enju) represents argument structure, whether such grammars have an ARG-ST feature at all, or how they implement passive, extraction, or optional complements. The DELPH-IN stance fields above are therefore almost all "not discussed"; this needs to be checked against the ERG itself.
- The chapter does not relate its CONTENT geometry (KEY, SIT-CORE, MODAL-BASE, ACT/UND/SOA) to the predicate and role inventory of MRS as used in implemented grammars, so it is open how much of the linking theory is visible in MRS output.
- Optional complements and optional by-phrases are mentioned only in passing (the optional PP in rule 42); the chapter does not say whether optionality is a lexical disjunction, a lexical rule, or a unary phrasal rule, which matters for the tag inventory.
- Extraction is covered only as "placement on the SLASH list" within ARP exceptions; the trace versus lexical rule versus unary extraction rule question is left to other chapters.
- Nothing is said about processing cost, ambiguity, or parsing.
- Sag's Sign-Based Construction Grammar work, which the user named as important, is not cited in this chapter; the only Sag works used are Pollard and Sag 1987/1994, Sag et al. 2003, Miller and Sag 1997, Manning et al. 1999, Bouma et al. 2001, and Kim and Sag 2002.
