# Chapter 31: HPSG and Dependency Grammar (Richard Hudson)

Handbook pages 1447-1495 (text 1447-1486, then references).

## Summary

The chapter compares phrase structure (PS), on which HPSG is built, with dependency structure (DS), using Hudson's own Word Grammar (WG) as the DS representative. A historical section argues that DS is the older tradition and PS a twentieth-century arrival, and notes that DS dominates treebanks and NLP, with Universal Dependencies named explicitly. The core sections go through the properties that separate the two traditions: containment and continuity (semantic phrasing, coordination, phrasal edges, word order) and asymmetry (headless phrases, multiple and mutual dependencies, grammatical functions). The chapter closes with a sketch of a "neo-HPSG" without phrases, in which a sentence is an ordered list of words, each word carrying a stack of sub-tokens that correspond one to one to the phrasal AVMs of a standard HPSG analysis.

**Bearing on the parser: moderate, and mostly on the UD bridge.** The chapter is written from the DS side and is not about implemented grammars. It says almost nothing about DELPH-IN, LKB/ACE, TRALE or Enju; the only mentions of implementation are a reviewer's remark that large-scale HPSG grammars exist (pointing to Chapter 25) and the citation of Bender (2008) on Wambaya. Its value for us is as a catalog of the places where a phrase-structure derivation and a dependency tree do not line up.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Discontinuous phrases and free word order

- Severity: high for the multilingual goal (medium for English)
- Assumption affected: continuity
- Reference: Section 1, p. 1449; Section 4.4, pp. 1471-1474 (example 21, Figure 12)
- Description: Hudson states that the claim "there are no discontinuous phrases" is "clearly false", and that there are versions of HPSG that allow discontinuous phrases (Reape 1994; Kathol 2000; Müller 1995; 1996). In "basic HPSG" linear precedence rules apply only to sisters; with binary branching that is "clearly too rigid given the freedom of ordering found in many languages". The remedies listed are (a) alternative binary branchings for the same word combinations, (b) binary branching combined with flat structures held in lists, and (c) linearization domains, in which a verb and its arguments and adjuncts share a domain and can be realized in any order. The extreme case is Wambaya (21), where an accusative modifier 'grog' is separated from the noun 'milk' it modifies and the only fixed position is the second-position auxiliary; this "pose[s] problems for standard versions of HPSG".
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed as an engine matter. The chapter says Bender (2008) "suggests solutions" for Wambaya, and that an approach "not using linearisation domains but rather projection of valence information" is discussed in Müller (Chapter 10, Section 7); the domain-based analysis is in Chapter 10, Section 6.2. It also says remedy (a), alternative binary branchings, is available. Which of these implemented grammars use is not stated here.

### 2. Alternative binary branchings for the same words

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 4.4, p. 1471
- Description: One of the listed answers to free order within a continuous grammar is "allowing alternative binary branchings for the same word combinations" (pointing to Müller, Chapter 10, Section 3). The chapter adds that such solutions "involve extra complexity in other parts of the theory such as additional lists".
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed.
- My own remark (not from the chapter): for the tagger this means the tree shape is the carrier of word-order variation, so the tree-structure bits in the labels will vary more in free-order languages than in English.

### 3. Coordination: unlike categories, non-constituent conjuncts, shared dependents

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge; continuity; tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 4.2, pp. 1465-1469 (examples 5-13, Figures 9-10)
- Description: Three points. (i) Coordination is symmetrical, so it is no dependency; WG handles it with ordered sets (strings) outside the dependency system, and "the precise status of the conjunction *and* remains to be determined". (ii) A shared dependent, as *Mary* in "Mary came in and sat down", depends on both verbs (d1 and its copy d5 in Figure 10). (iii) Conjuncts need not be phrases: in "We hold parties for foreign boys on Tuesdays and girls on Wednesdays" the first conjunct is the string (boys, on, Tuesdays), "which is not a phrase defined by dependencies". Coordination of unlikes ("intelligent, a good linguist and in the right job") is called "a well-known challenge for PS-based analyses such as HPSG".
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed. For HPSG in general the chapter says unlike-category coordination can be accommodated by redefining categories as functions (for example a category "predicative"), and criticizes that move; it refers to Abeillé and Chaves (Chapter 16) for HPSG coordination.

### 4. Headless phrases

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 1, p. 1449; Section 5, p. 1474; Section 5.1, pp. 1475-1478 (examples 22-26, Figures 13-14)
- Description: "HPSG gives some constructions a headless analysis (Müller 2018: 654-666)", and the head/dependent asymmetry "has come under fire within the HPSG community". Candidate headless constructions: *the rich*, the comparative correlative, the NPN construction (*student after student*), verbless exhaustive conditionals (*however intelligent the students*), and Mad-Magazine sentences (*What, him smart?*). Hudson gives each a headed WG analysis (determiner as head; predicate as head of a verbless clause), but these are his proposals, and he admits "it is impossible to prove the non-existence of headless phrases". A conversion from derivation to dependency tree needs a head for every rule, so headless rules need a stipulated choice.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed.

### 5. Head choice differs between dependency theories

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 2, p. 1458; Section 5.1, p. 1475; Section 5.2, pp. 1478-1480
- Description: Hudson warns that "other DS theories would give very different answers to some of the questions that I raise". WG takes the determiner as head of the noun phrase ("the determiner-headed analysis of standard WG"), the wh-word as head of an embedded interrogative (*who* in "I wonder who came"), and the relative pronoun as head of the relative clause. A dependency tree is therefore no neutral target: each rule-to-dependency mapping encodes a choice among competing DS analyses.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed.

### 6. A word can have several heads; mutual dependency

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 5.2, pp. 1478-1480 (examples 27-29, Figure 15)
- Description: In WG a word can depend on more than one word, and two words can depend on each other: in "I wonder who came", *who* is object of *wonder* and subject of *came*, and *came* is complement of *who*. The same holds for relative clauses. HPSG gets "the same effect by means of re-entrancy" (Pollard and Sag 1994: 50, mutual selection of determiner and noun); structure sharing gives raising or lowering "within a single chain of dependencies", and whether HPSG allows other double dependencies is "less clear". The dependency information in an HPSG analysis is thus a graph, which a single-headed tree cannot hold completely.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed.

### 7. Grammatical functions are implicit, and adjuncts select their heads

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 1474-1475; Section 5.3, pp. 1480-1484 (examples 30-31, Figure 16)
- Description: HPSG leaves grammatical functions "implicit in the order of elements in ARG-ST": the direct object is "the second NP in the ARG-ST list", and "there is no label or specific category information attached to say the third element". ARG-ST lists can be subjectless (German), with an object in first position, so list position alone does not name the function. Versions of HPSG differ on whether a verb's subject is in SPR or SUBJ. Adjuncts are not on any list of the head; the head is the MOD value of the adjunct, so there is no unifying category "dependent" in most versions (exception: DEPS in Bouma, Malouf and Sag 2001). Labeled dependency relations therefore have to be computed from list positions and rule identity.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed.

### 8. Empty elements in analyses of edge phenomena and verbless clauses

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 4.3, pp. 1469-1470 (examples 16-20); Section 5.1, p. 1477
- Description: The "standard PS explanation" of Welsh soft mutation, the XP Trigger Hypothesis (Borsley et al. 2007), has mutation triggered on the first word of a subject or complement right after an XP boundary. It "relies on controversial assumptions about null subjects and traces", which "have to be treated as full phrases" to trigger mutation in (19)-(20). Separately, a covert verb is one option for verbless clauses; Hudson rejects it (Arabic case and order differ between overt and covert "be") and prefers a predicate-headed analysis following Müller (2009; 2012).
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed.
- My own remark (not from the chapter): the mutated form of a word depends on what phrase ends immediately before it, so the choice of a token's lexical rule is conditioned by its phrasal context; a tagger sees the form, but replay has to check it against a neighbor's phrase edge.

### 9. Pied-piping

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (feature percolation in replay)
- Reference: Section 5.2, p. 1480 (example 29)
- Description: "Pied-piping presents well-known challenges": in "the minister in the middle of whose sermon the dog barked" the relative word is deeply embedded in the fronted phrase. WG needs a special relation "pipee" that transfers positional properties up the dependency chain. The example is taken from Pollard and Sag (1994: 212); their analysis is not described.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed.

### 10. Token identity in the NPN construction

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (lexical identity constraint between leaves)
- Reference: Section 5.1, pp. 1476-1477 (Figure 14)
- Description: *student after student (after student)* requires the two nouns, and in the recursive case the prepositions, to be the same lexeme. Hudson says the lexical repetition "is beyond most theories of syntax"; the semantics "is harder and needs more research".
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed.

### 11. Non-intersective modification

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 1463-1465 (Figure 8)
- Description: *typical French house* means 'French house which is typical of French houses', so the semantics needs a node for *French house*; *fake* and *former* do not yield hyponyms of the noun meaning. For HPSG "this is not a problem, because every dependent is part of a new structure, semantic as well as syntactic". The point constrains the bridge in the UD-to-HPSG direction: a bare dependency tree does not record the order in which modifiers attach.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed (footnote 7 points to Koenig and Richter, Chapter 22, Section 3.2, on adjunct scope).

## Support for the design

- **Per-word decomposition of a phrase-structure analysis (Section 6, pp. 1484-1486, example 32, Figure 17).** The "neo-HPSG" sketch replaces phrasal boxes by "a single list of words", each with a chain of sub-tokens (enjoy, enjoy+o, enjoy+s), and "each boxed item in this diagram corresponds to an AVM in a standard HPSG analysis". Hudson describes the chain as what HPSG already does: "each dependent creates a new representation of the head by satisfying and cancelling a valency need and passing the remaining needs up". Section 4.1 (p. 1465) makes the same count: the WG analysis has the same number of syntactic nodes as the HPSG analysis. My own remark: this is the same observation the tree encoding rests on, that every phrasal node of a headed derivation can be assigned to one word.
- **Word-level analysis is what makes DS attractive in NLP (Section 2, p. 1459).** "The only units of analysis are words, so at least these units are given in the raw data and the overall analysis can immediately be broken down into a much simpler analysis for each word"; the analysis needs a global view of the sentence, "but at least in simple examples a bottom-up word-based view will also give the right result".
- **UD and dependency treebanks are the dominant resource (Section 2, p. 1458).** 228 of 274 treebanks listed on Wikipedia use DS; the UD site lists almost 200 treebanks for over 100 languages.
- **HPSG already contains the dependency information (Section 1, p. 1449; Section 5.3, pp. 1480-1482).** "HPSG implicitly (and sometimes even explicitly) recognises dependencies"; most constructions are headed; the schemata (Specifier-Head, Filler-Head, Head-Complement, Head-Adjunct) each identify a HEAD-DAUGHTER, and arguments and adjuncts are unified as non-head daughters. A phrase in DS is "a word plus all the words which depend, directly or indirectly, on it" (pp. 1449-1450), which is the definition a conversion between the two needs.
- **Semantics composed at every node (Section 4.1, p. 1464).** In HPSG "every dependent is part of a new structure, semantic as well as syntactic", so the phrase *French house* has its own content. This is the property that lets the semantics fall out of replay.
- **Monotonic constraint satisfaction (Section 3, pp. 1460-1462).** "Most versions of HPSG allow generalisations but not exceptions" ("If one formulates a restriction on a supertype, this automatically affects all of its subtypes", Müller 2018: 275); a sentence is grammatical if it can be modeled by combining structures "by inserting less complex structures into daughter slots of more complex structures". No defaults or overrides have to be resolved when a derivation is replayed.
- **Continuity is the default in both traditions (Section 4.4, p. 1474).** WG bans intersecting landmark relations by default; discontinuity is the exception that needs extra machinery.
- **Large-scale implemented grammars exist (Section 6, p. 1484).** A reviewer's argument, reported by Hudson, with a pointer to Bender and Emerson (Chapter 25, Section 3).

## Theory strains

- **PS versus DS as the basis of HPSG (Section 1, p. 1450; Section 6, pp. 1484-1486).** Hudson claims the choice of PS was "simply default syntax" and never argued for, that "the debate has hardly started", and that HPSG "could survive almost intact" as a DS theory. Footnote 1 records the counter-position: one dependency structure is argued to be insufficient for complex predicates (Abeillé and Godard 2010; Chapter 11) and negation (Kim and Sag 2002; Chapter 18).
- **Default inheritance (Section 3, pp. 1460-1462).** Accepted by some in HPSG (Lascarides and Copestake 1999), rejected by others (Müller 2018: 403); fundamental in WG.
- **Word order machinery (Section 4.4, pp. 1471-1474).** Sister-only LP rules with binary branching are too rigid; the competing repairs are alternative branchings, flat list structures, linearization domains (Reape, Kathol, Müller), and projection of valence information (Bender 2008 on Wambaya). Hudson calls all of them "extra complexity".
- **Coordination of unlikes (Section 4.2, pp. 1467-1468).** The functional-category repair ("predicative" as a category) "generates as many problems as it solves": the category duplicates a function, and groups otherwise unrelated categories arbitrarily. Non-constituent conjuncts strain any phrase-based account.
- **Headedness (Section 5, p. 1474; Section 5.1, pp. 1475-1478).** Headless analyses within HPSG (Müller 2018: 654-666; Jackendoff 2008 on NPN; Arnold and Borsley 2014 on exhaustive conditionals) versus Hudson's claim that all phrases are headed.
- **Empty elements (Section 4.3, p. 1470; Section 5.1, p. 1477).** The XP Trigger Hypothesis for Welsh mutation needs traces and pro to count as phrases, which Hudson calls controversial; covert copulas are rejected in favor of a raising-style predicate-headed analysis.
- **Adjuncts (Section 5.3, pp. 1481-1482).** Head as MOD of the adjunct (inherited from Categorial Grammar) versus adjuncts as dependents on a DEPS list (Bouma, Malouf and Sag 2001). Hudson finds "reasons for preferring" the unified treatment.
- **Grammatical functions as list positions (Section 5.3, pp. 1482-1484).** Ordered ARG-ST captures the obliqueness ranking (Relational Grammar; Keenan and Comrie 1977) but has no labels. Hudson asks why there should be a fixed universal list; the reply built into the text is that ARG-ST is not claimed to be universal (Koenig and Michelson 2015 on Oneida) and that list elements can be lexeme-specific. "This is clearly an area needing further research."
- **SPR versus SUBJ (Section 5.3, p. 1480).** Whether the subject of a verb is a specifier differs across "versions of HPSG".
- **Pied-piping and lexical repetition (Sections 5.1-5.2, pp. 1476, 1480).** Named as known hard cases for syntactic theories in general.
- **Limits on dependency geometry (Section 5.2, p. 1478).** Whether HPSG allows double dependency outside a raising chain is "less clear".

## Not covered here

All points below are my own remarks about gaps, not content of the chapter.

- The chapter never describes how any implemented grammar (ERG, other DELPH-IN grammars, TRALE, Enju) handles the phenomena it lists; every "DELPH-IN stance" above is therefore "not discussed" or a pointer to another chapter.
- UD is mentioned only as a treebank collection. Nothing is said about UD's own head choices (content-word heads, treatment of coordination, copulas, function words), which differ from the WG choices used in the chapter; the conversion design cannot be read off this chapter.
- No discussion of existing conversions between HPSG derivations or MRS and dependency representations.
- No discussion of how HPSG semantics (MRS or otherwise) relates to dependency structure beyond the *typical French house* example.
- Unbounded dependencies are touched only through wh-words and pied-piping; the SLASH mechanism and its treatment without traces are not discussed.
- The remedies for free word order are only named; their details are in Chapter 10.
