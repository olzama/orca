# Chapter 3: Formal background (Frank Richter)

Handbook pages 89-124. Notes taken strictly from the chapter; my own remarks are marked as such.

## Summary

The chapter gives a condensed formalization of the HPSG of Pollard and Sag (1994) in Relational Speciate Re-entrant Language (RSRL). Section 2 (pp. 91-100) is an informal overview: descriptions versus described objects, sort hierarchy and feature appropriateness, grammar principles as implications, relations, and the debate over what the models of a grammar are. Sections 3-4 (pp. 100-113) define signatures, chains, terms, formulas, descriptions, and their denotation. Section 5 (pp. 113-117) defines grammars, models, and King's exhaustive models. Section 6 (pp. 117-122) compares four model theories (Pollard and Sag 1994; King 1999; Pollard 1999; Richter 2007).

The chapter is about the declarative, model-theoretic formalism. It says almost nothing about parsing, word order, empty elements, or MRS, and it explicitly leaves out SBCG (p. 91). Its bearing on the parser is indirect: it marks the gap between the canonical formalism and what implementation platforms (and therefore our replay engine) actually compute. No caveat here is severe for an ERG-first design.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. "Unification" has no technical meaning in the 1994 formalism

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 1, pp. 90-91; Section 2, pp. 92-93; footnote 12, p. 108
- What the chapter says: HPSG 1987 was unification-based, with partial feature structures ordered in an algebra of partial information. In Pollard and Sag (1994) "that algebra did not exist anymore, feature structures were no longer partial but total objects in models of a logical theory, and unification was no longer defined" (p. 90). Conjoining two compatible descriptions is casually called unification, but "there is no technical interpretation of the term in this context" (p. 93). The word "type" also changed meaning (1987 types became 1994 sorts; "type" in 1994 means object type, p. 90).
- Consequence for us: a replay engine that unifies typed feature structures implements the 1987-style logic of partial information. That is a legitimate formalism, but it is a different one from the canonical formalism of the "real" HPSG book. Claims such as "licensed by the grammar" must be stated relative to the implementation formalism.
- DELPH-IN stance: no platform is named. The chapter says the ideas of partial-information algebras "are still essential for all current HPSG implementation platforms" (p. 93, pointing to Bender and Emerson, chapter 25), and that feature structures "are essential constructs for implementation platforms" (footnote 12, p. 108).

### 2. The description language is far richer than conjunctive unification

- Severity: medium (for the long-term goal of running grammars written in the theoretical tradition); low for the ERG
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 93, 96-98 (examples 3b-3d); footnote 1, p. 93; Section 3, pp. 101-105 (Definitions 1-4); Section 4, pp. 110-113
- What the chapter says: descriptions use classical negation, disjunction, implication, existential and universal quantification over components, and relation symbols (append, member, shuffle, o-command, set union, intersection, subset, set difference). Principles may have arbitrary complex descriptions as antecedents (3d, p. 98). Finite sets and parametric sorts are also used (p. 113). All these constructs "are necessary" to render the Pollard and Sag (1994) grammar "without reformulating the grammar" (p. 113).
- Consequence for us: a "small typed-feature-structure engine" that only unifies cannot replay a grammar stated in this formalism as written. Relational constraints such as shuffle or set-valued constraints are in general not functional, so applying a named rule may not yield a single result. Any grammar from this tradition has to be reformulated first, and the reformulation is where information can be lost.
- DELPH-IN stance: footnote 1 (p. 93) says implementation platforms "often provide only a restricted syntax of descriptions and may also supply additional syntactic constructs which extend their logic of partial information toward the expressiveness of a feature logic with classical interpretation of negation and relational expressions". No platform is named.

### 3. Phrase structure rules, lexical rules, and defaults are meta-notation

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules (also tree-encoding)
- Reference: Section 1, pp. 89-90; Section 3, p. 100
- What the chapter says: notations for phrase structure rules, lexical entries and the lexicon, lexical rules, and default values "do not (directly) belong to the syntax of the assumed feature logics" (p. 90). They can usually be re-interpreted in an HPSG-compatible formalism, but the re-interpretation "can deviate to a larger or lesser degree from what their users have in mind", is "sometimes trivial and sometimes nearly impossible, and different available re-interpretation choices lead to significantly different results" (p. 90). A phrase structure rule "is not the same as an ID Schema written in a feature logic"; in particular, "an ID Schema may easily leave the number of a phrase's daughters unspecified (and thus potentially infinite)" (p. 90).
- Consequence for us: our derivation tree, whose nodes are rule names with fixed arity one or two, is an object of the implementation platform. The theory's ID schemata do not guarantee bounded arity, and lexical rules have no single agreed formal status. The label inventory (lexical type plus chain of lexical rules) rests on platform conventions. Grammars for other languages written with flat or variable-arity schemata would need binarization before the tree encoding applies.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed for any specific platform. The chapter only says platforms "have their own formalisms" and "add their own technical and notational devices" that linguists then import into theory (pp. 89-91).

### 4. Licensing is global well-formedness of total objects

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 92, 95-97; Section 4, pp. 107-108 (Definition 7); Section 5, p. 114 (Definition 18)
- What the chapter says: linguistic objects exist only as complete objects; they are totally well-typed and sort-resolved (every node has a maximally specific sort and carries every appropriate attribute). Descriptions are partial and may denote many objects. A structure is licensed only "when it is well-formed in all its components with respect to all the grammar principles" (p. 97): every component must satisfy every principle, either by satisfying the consequent or by failing the antecedent.
- Consequence for us: the output of replay is an underspecified feature structure, that is, a description in the chapter's terms. Under the chapter's definitions, "replay succeeded" shows that the conjunction of rule and lexical constraints is consistent as a partial structure. Whether that entails the existence of a total, sort-resolved object satisfying every principle at every component is a separate question that the chapter does not address. (My remark: this follows from the definitions; the chapter does not draw this conclusion about implementations.) Principles with complex antecedents (3d, p. 98) apply to any component anywhere in the structure, so they are not tied to a rule application step.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. The lexicon is one disjunctive principle

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (leaves-equal-tokens)
- Reference: Section 2, p. 97 (example 3b, Word Principle)
- What the chapter says: words are licensed by the Word Principle, `word => (LE1 or LE2 or ... or LEn)`, where each disjunct is a lexical entry. Nouns may be infinite in number "due to morphological processes such as compounding, depending on the choice of morphological analysis" (p. 92).
- Consequence for us: the tagger's choice of lexical type amounts to choosing a disjunct of the Word Principle, which fits the design. The possible open-endedness of the word inventory means the label set cannot be assumed closed in the theory, only in a given implemented grammar.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The Word Principle is attributed to Hoehle (2019: 500).

### 6. Semantic principles can be relational and non-functional

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 97-98 (examples 3c, 3d; footnote 5)
- What the chapter says: the quantifier-storage principle of Pollard and Yoo (1998) is given as an example of a relational principle. It constrains QSTORE, POOL, and RETRIEVED with set-of-elements, subset, and set difference: the retrieved quantifiers are some subset of the pool. The set mentioned in the relation is not itself an attribute value, which is what motivates chains.
- Consequence for us: in this style of semantics, the syntactic structure does not fix the semantics; the principle admits several structures per tree, one per choice of retrieved subset. (My remark: the chapter presents the principle but does not comment on this multiplicity.) "Semantics as a deterministic by-product of the derivation" therefore holds only for semantic representations designed to be so. The chapter does not discuss MRS at all.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Word order is stated by a relation on PHON; shuffle is among the relations

- Severity: low (in this chapter)
- Assumption affected: continuity
- Reference: Section 3, p. 101; Section 5, p. 115 (example 8)
- What the chapter says: the only word order principle shown is for head-filler phrases, where the mother's PHON is the append of the filler's PHON and the head daughter's PHON (p. 115). The chapter also lists a ternary shuffle relation among relations that "grammars may also require" (p. 101), without saying what for.
- Consequence for us: in the canonical formalism, concatenation is a per-phrase-type stipulation through a relation; it is not built into the formalism. Nothing prevents a principle from using shuffle. The chapter gives no analysis that does so.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. A grammar denotes structures; it has no derivations

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (other)
- Reference: Section 2, p. 92; Section 5, pp. 113-117; Section 6, pp. 120-121
- What the chapter says: HPSG is a model-theoretic framework "as opposed to generative-enumerative grammar frameworks, which have rewrite systems that generate expressions from some start symbol(s)" (p. 92). A grammar is a signature plus a set of descriptions; its meaning is a class of models. Under two of the four model theories, one utterance corresponds to more than one structure in the denotation (pp. 120-121).
- Consequence for us: "derivation tree as recipe" is a procedural notion that belongs to the platform. In the theory, the tree is part of the sign itself (daughter attributes), and nothing is derived. This matters for wording in the paper and for the revised-theory notes; it does not threaten the engineering.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- Feature-structure models connect to efficient computation. The Pollard and Sag (1994) model theory (T1) "provides a bridge to the literature on feature logics, connecting linguistic theory to an interesting set of efficient computational methods", and this connection "is unaffected by whether feature structure models are deemed adequate for linguistic theory" (Section 6, p. 122).
- All current implementation platforms rest on partial feature structures and unification (Section 2, p. 93). A unification-based replay engine is in line with every existing platform.
- Convenience notations (rules, lexical rules, lexicon) can usually be given an interpretation in an HPSG-compatible formalism (Section 1, p. 90).
- Signatures are finite in actual grammars and have a top sort (Section 3, p. 102); theories are finite by "deliberate linguistic convention" (Section 5, p. 114). The type hierarchy and constraint set that the engine loads are finite objects.
- Feature inheritance and value narrowing down the sort hierarchy (pp. 94-95, 101) are exactly what a typed-feature-structure engine implements.
- Structure sharing is token identity of path values (p. 97); path equations are a primitive of the formalism (Definition 4, p. 105). Reentrancy in the engine corresponds directly.
- The one explicit word order principle in the chapter is concatenative (append on PHON in head-filler phrases, p. 115).
- Relations hold only among components of one sign, and quantification ranges only over components of one structure (pp. 110, 113). Constraint checking is local to the sign being built; no constraint refers to other sentences.
- Partial descriptions are used uniformly for phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics (p. 93), which supports getting the semantics from the same structure as the syntax.

## Theory strains

- 1987 versus 1994 foundations (Section 1, pp. 90-91; Section 2, pp. 92-93). Partial feature structures with unification versus total objects described by a classical logic. The notation (AVMs) is the same in both, which "contributes to obscuring substantial differences". Community terminology (unification, types, pieces of information) survives from 1987 "despite their lack of meaning in the standard formalism".
- The founding books are not precise (Section 1, p. 89). Pollard and Sag (1987; 1994) "do not present grammar formalisms with the intention to provide precise definitions"; the 1994 appendix lists "most (but not all)" components. "Both books leave room for interpretation."
- Theory shaped by implementation platforms (Section 1, pp. 89-91). Platforms bring their own formalisms, terminology, and devices, which linguists import into theoretical grammar writing; the logical reading of these notations can differ from what users assume from platform behavior.
- No agreed formal status for lexical rules, defaults, phrase structure rules, decorated sort hierarchies (Section 1, p. 90). Different re-interpretations "lead to significantly different results".
- AVM notation resists formalization (Section 3, p. 104). AVMs are "quite cumbersome to define as a rigorous logical language which meets all the expressive needs of HPSG".
- Chains (Section 2, footnote 5, p. 98; Section 3, p. 103; Section 4, p. 108). Starting with Pollard and Sag (1994), grammars use lists and sets in arguments of relations that are not attribute values anywhere in the structure. RSRL has to add pseudo-sorts and pseudo-attributes to every signature to make sense of this practice.
- Four competing model theories (Section 2, pp. 98-99; Section 6, pp. 117-122). T1 object types as abstract feature structures (Pollard and Sag 1994): criticized as ontologically dubious, superfluous, and only conventionally related to data. T2 exhaustive models of tokens (King 1999): requires non-actual "potential tokens", called contentious and by Pollard self-contradictory. T3 strong generative capacity (Pollard 1999): mathematical idealizations. T4 minimal exhaustive models of normal form grammars (Richter 2007). The status of the entities in the universe "has been debated fiercely" (p. 107).
- T1 and T3 fail their own goal (Section 6, pp. 120-121; Section 2, p. 99). They yield an unintended one-to-many relation between an utterance and structures in the denotation, including structures (reducts, substructures) that correspond to no utterance. The cause is partly "imprecisions of actual HPSG grammar specifications" (p. 99). The repair in T4 requires extending every grammar with extra axioms and signature.
- Plain models are too weak (Section 5, pp. 115-116). A model of a perfect grammar of English can consist of one sentence, so over- and undergeneration cannot be read off arbitrary models; an additional notion (exhaustive model) is needed.
- "Theory" as a technical term (Section 5, footnote 14, p. 114). A set of descriptions arguably is not a theory of a language in the traditional sense.
- SBCG is set aside (Section 1, p. 91) as a variant that this formalization does not cover.
- Model-theoretic versus generative-enumerative frameworks (Section 2, p. 92; Pullum and Scholz 2001): the contrast with other frameworks that the chapter draws.

## Not covered here

All items in this section are my own remarks, not claims of the chapter.

- The chapter names no implementation platform (LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju) and gives no detail on which constructs each supports. It defers to chapter 25.
- Nothing on empty elements, traces, discontinuous constituents, or word order domains. Shuffle is named once as a relation without a use.
- Nothing on MRS or any specific semantic representation; the Pollard and Yoo principle is only an illustration of relations.
- Nothing on the formal treatment of lexical rules (description-level versus meta-level), on defaults, or on the type-versus-sort logics behind the platforms (for example Carpenter's logic of typed feature structures), although the chapter says these issues exist.
- Nothing on complexity or decidability of RSRL, which would bear on whether a grammar in the full formalism can be processed at all.
- Nothing relevant to the UD bridge.
- Of the authors named in the reading request, the chapter relies on Pollard and Sag 1994 throughout, cites Sag 2012 and Mueller 2021 (chapter 32) only to set SBCG aside, and does not cite Bouma.
