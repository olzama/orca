# Chapter 15: Island phenomena and related matters (Rui P. Chaves)

Handbook pages 665-723 (text 665-701, references 701-723). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter surveys the classic island constraints on long-distance dependencies: the Coordinate Structure Constraint, the Complex NP Constraint, Subjacency, the Right Roof Constraint, Freezing, subject islands (including the Sentential Subject Constraint), adjunct islands, Superiority and whether-islands, the Left Branch Condition, and the Complementizer Constraint, plus island circumvention under ellipsis. Its thesis is that almost every island has robust, attested exceptions, so islands are unlikely to be a unitary syntactic constraint; they are attributed to a mix of semantic, pragmatic (topicality, informational focus, relevance to the main assertion), prosodic, and processing factors (memory decay, garden paths, "digging-in", parsing expectations). Only a few effects get a grammatical account in HPSG: the Conjunct Constraint (from the traceless analysis), the Left Branch Condition (determiners and genitives are not valents), part of Superiority (two lexical entries for wh-words), and possibly the Complementizer Constraint. The chapter says of itself that its focus is on the phenomena and not on HPSG analyses (p. 665), and that HPSG "remains relatively agnostic about many island types" (p. 700).

The chapter does not discuss any implemented grammar or parsing system. DELPH-IN, the ERG, LKB, ACE, TRALE, and Enju are not mentioned. The only contact points are (a) scope examples taken from the MRS paper, Copestake et al. 2005 (footnote 16, p. 674; footnote 75, p. 696), and (b) a pointer in the conclusion to Data-Oriented Parsing work with HPSG (Neumann and Flickinger 1999, 2002; p. 701). Every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is therefore "not discussed" unless stated otherwise. Overall the chapter bears on the parser less through specific analyses than through one general point: grammaticality, as defined by the grammar, and acceptability come apart for extraction.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. A grammar that hard-codes islands will reject attested sentences; a grammar that does not will license unacceptable ones

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (meaning of "strict" output)
- Reference: Section 2, p. 667; Section 3, pp. 670-672 (schema 11); Section 4, pp. 672-674 (schema 16); Section 6, pp. 678-680; Section 9, pp. 690-693; Section 14, pp. 700-701
- What the chapter says: "it would be rather trivial to impose the classic island constraints in the standard syntactic environments in which they arise" by constraining SLASH (or EXTRA) on a rule or lexical entry, for example identical SLASH on all conjuncts (11), [SLASH { }] on adnominal modifiers (16), [EXTRA < >] on dependents of S, or a SLASH constraint on adjuncts. Each such stipulation is then shown to have accepted counterexamples: non-ATB extraction from asymmetric coordination (12), extraction from relative clauses and noun-complement clauses (17-18), extraposition out of embedded clauses (26-27), extraction from subjects (32, 34, 35, 37, 46, 47), extraction from untensed and tensed adjuncts (52-53). "HPSG has generally refrained from assuming that islands are syntactic" (p. 667).
- Why it matters: replay success or failure is only as good as the grammar's choice here. If the grammar being replayed contains any of these constraints, good sentences of the listed kinds fail replay and drop to the relaxed output. If it contains none, replay succeeds on island violations too, so "strict" means "licensed by the grammar" and does not mean "acceptable". The chapter's position is that the remaining filtering is probabilistic, pragmatic, and processing-based, which in the planned architecture would have to come from the tagger.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter does not say which, if any, of these constraints implemented grammars impose.

### 2. One filler, several gaps; gaps that are summed into a plural referent

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics (MRS as a by-product) / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 2, p. 666 (1); Section 3, pp. 670-671 (13) and footnote 6; Section 8.2, p. 689 (48-49); Section 9, p. 692 (54-55)
- What the chapter says: SLASH is "a set-valued feature"; "by allowing SLASH sets to be identified (or unioned)" multiple gaps linked to one filler come for free, in coordination and outside it (subject plus object, complement plus adjunct, two complements). A further reason not to equate SLASH values is that multiple gaps are sometimes "cumulatively" combined into a "pluralic gap": in (13) the gaps are not co-indexed with each other, and the filler denotes a plural referent composed of the gap referents (What did Kim eat and drink at the party? Answer: pizza and beer). Both readings exist, and speakers differ by example.
- Why it matters: (a) The summed reading cannot be produced by plain structure sharing of the filler's index with each gap; the tree is the same for both readings, so the derivation tree does not determine the semantics there. The chapter gives no HPSG mechanism for the summed reading (it cites Chaves 2012a among others). (b) For the UD bridge, one filler token is an argument of two or more predicates, which a basic UD tree records only once. (c) Set union is not ordinary unification, so the feature-structure engine has to commit to some encoding of SLASH.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Extraposition is a second nonlocal dependency (EXTRA), and it is not bounded by NP, PP, or even the clause

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / tree-encoding (the dependency is concatenative, but the attachment site is far from the head it modifies)
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 677-678 (24); Section 6, pp. 678-680 (25-27); Section 8, p. 684 (36)
- What the chapter says: "When treated as a form of extraction, rightward movement has been predominantly accounted for via a feature EXTRA(POSED) (Keller 1994; 1995; Bouma 1996; Van Eynde 1996; Müller 1999: Section 13.2; Kim and Sag 2005), rather than by SLASH." Corpus examples show extraposed relative clauses and PPs whose host is embedded under several NP and PP nodes (24), extraposition from subjects (36), and extraposition out of embedded clauses across a matrix adverbial (26-27). "A detailed account of extraposition island phenomena does not exist in any framework" (p. 680); the suggested conclusion is that "there is no syntactic constraint on EXTRA".
- Why it matters: the derivation stays a continuous tree, which is compatible with the design, but the relation between an extraposed phrase and its host is carried by a feature passed through arbitrarily many nodes. The UD side gets a non-projective dependency whose head can be deeply embedded, and the HPSG-to-UD conversion has to trace EXTRA to find it. The opposite direction (UD to derivation) has to insert whatever rules introduce and discharge EXTRA. The phrase "when treated as a form of extraction" implies other treatments exist, but the chapter does not present them.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Sluicing and fragments: a unary construction whose content comes from the discourse context

- Severity: medium (for semantics), none for tree shape
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 13, p. 700 (73)
- What the chapter says: in HPSG the remnant wh-phrase is "coerced" into a proposition-denoting clause "via a unary branching construction that taps into contextual information" (Ginzburg and Sag 2000; Culicover and Jackendoff 2005; Jacobson 2008; Sag and Nykiel 2011). The antecedent "need not correspond to overt discourse" (What floor? What else?). There is no extraction and no island.
- Why it matters: the tree is a plain unary rule over a phrase, with no empty elements, which fits the encoding. But the propositional content is not composed from the daughters; it is taken from context. Replay of a single sentence cannot supply it, so the MRS for such a clause is necessarily incomplete or needs a placeholder.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. The traceless analysis moves extraction into the lexicon, including for adjuncts

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements / tree-encoding (lexical-rule chain in the tag)
- Reference: Section 3, p. 668 and footnote 3; Section 12, p. 699
- What the chapter says: accounts with traces (Pollard and Sag 1994; Levine and Hukari 2006) and traceless accounts (Sag and Fodor 1995; Bouma et al. 2001; Ginzburg and Sag 2000; Sag 2010) coexist. In the traceless accounts only valents (ARG-ST members) can appear in a head's SLASH, typed as gap-synsem. To let adverbials be extracted, Ginzburg and Sag (2000) make those adverbials members of ARG-ST. Adnominal adjectives cannot be extracted because they are on no ARG-ST. Adnominal PPs are unclear; extracted PPs can often be parsed as VP modifiers instead (footnote 3).
- Why it matters: the design's no-empty-elements assumption matches the traceless line, but the cost is that a gap is a property of the selecting word. The tag for a verb must say which of its arguments is missing, and for adjunct extraction the verb (not any visible modifier) must carry an added argument. This lengthens and diversifies the lexical-rule part of the tag, and the gap site is not a node of the tree, so it has to be reconstructed for the UD bridge. Footnote 3's attachment ambiguity (noun modifier or VP modifier) is a place where one string has two derivations with little to tell them apart.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Two independent fillers, with nested or crossing paths

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 7, pp. 680-681 (30)
- What the chapter says: "constructions with two independent gaps in close proximity are licit, but not trivial to process", including the case where "the extraction paths cross" (Fodor 1978): "Who can't you remember which papers you sent copies of _ to _?"
- Why it matters: a node can carry two different elements in SLASH at once. When a filler is attached, the rule name alone does not say which SLASH member it binds; with a set-valued SLASH and two fillers of the same category this is a choice point, so replay needs either a fixed discharge order in the grammar or an extra bit in the tree encoding. The chapter does not say how the pairing is fixed. (The choice-point reading is my inference from the chapter's set-valued SLASH; the chapter itself only discusses processing difficulty.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Wh-words have two lexical entries distinguished by accent

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (lexical-type choice not visible in text)
- Reference: Section 10, pp. 694-695 (63)
- What the chapter says: following Ginzburg and Sag (2000: 251 and Chapter 5), who has an ex situ entry (non-empty WH, optionally accented) and an "optionally ex situ" entry that must be accented and has empty WH; in a multiple wh-interrogative all wh-phrases except the first must be in focus. Wh-quantifiers are held in STORE.
- Why it matters: the tagger has to choose between two lexical types for the same written form, and the cue the theory names (pitch accent) is absent from text. Position (fronted or in situ) will usually decide, so the practical risk is small. Echo and reference questions (61-62) are the cases where position is not enough. The STORE-based semantics of (63) is not MRS.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Subject extraction and the complementizer: an adverb as a complement of "that"

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 12, pp. 698-699 (71-72)
- What the chapter says: in Bouma et al. (2001) and Ginzburg and Sag (2000: 181) an extracted subject leaves a gap-synsem on the SUBJ list, and "that" requires an S with empty SUBJ, which blocks "Who did Tom say that had bought the tickets?". To license the adverbial amelioration in (72), Ginzburg and Sag let the complementizer select an adverb and a subject-gap clause as its two arguments. The chapter calls this "ad-hoc", since the adverb would be expected to adjoin to the clause.
- Why it matters: a grammar following this analysis has a second lexical entry for "that" and a tree in which the adverb is a sister of the complementizer, which is not the attachment a UD treebank gives (adverb as modifier of the embedded verb). The conversion would need a special case.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Determiners and genitives as non-valents; left-branch extraction in other languages

- Severity: low for English, medium for the multilingual goal
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / continuity
- Reference: Section 11, pp. 696-698 (67-70)
- What the chapter says: the Left Branch Condition follows if determiners are not valents of the noun and select it via SELECT (68). Genitive phrases combine with the noun "via a dedicated construction" and are co-indexed with a role of the noun through X-ARG (Sag 2012: 133; Chaves and Putnam 2020: 197-198). The clitic "'s" must lean phonologically on the NP it selects. French "Combien a-t-il vendu de livres?" is only an apparent violation, because "de livres" is an independent post-verbal de-N' nominal (Abeillé et al. 2004). Polish, Russian, and Hungarian allow real-looking left-branch extraction (70); the chapter only says that these languages lack determiners and that "it is possible" the extracted phrase has an independent status like the French one.
- Why it matters: for English, the choice between specifier-as-valent and functor-style selection changes which rule names appear in the tree and where the genitive's semantics is assembled (a construction, not the lexicon). For other languages, a UD treebank attaches the fronted wh-determiner or possessor to the noun with a non-projective arc, while the HPSG analysis the chapter suggests makes the two parts separate dependents of the verb; the Slavic and Hungarian cases have no worked-out analysis here.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 10. Lexically obligatory or forbidden extraction

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (lexical type carries SLASH constraints)
- Reference: Section 2, p. 667 (2)
- What the chapter says: lexical items can constrain SLASH idiosyncratically. Prepositions in languages without stranding are [SLASH { }]. Some uses of "assure" require one complement to be in SLASH, so extraction is obligatory ("Who can you assure me _ to be the most competent?").
- Why it matters: a lexical type exists that can only occur in an extraction context. This is consistent with the design (the tag carries it) but is one more case where the lexical type depends on non-local context.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 11. Sentences with exactly one grammatical analysis that is locally dispreferred

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (tagging without search)
- Reference: Section 7, pp. 680-681; Section 8.1, pp. 685-687 (39-45) and footnote 57
- What the chapter says: in "Fat people eat accumulates" and in hard subject-island violations "the sentences have exactly one grammatical analysis, but that parse is preempted by a highly preferential alternative which ultimately cannot yield a complete analysis". Sentence-initial "that" is preferentially taken as a determiner or pronoun even where it must be a complementizer (Gibson 2006, footnote 57). A stranded "to" directly before a shifted NP invites the wrong attachment (29c).
- Why it matters: the chapter is describing human parsing, but the configuration is the one where a tagger with no search is most exposed: locally probable labels that do not add up to a licensed derivation. These are natural stress-test items for the fallback path.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- No empty elements is the mainstream modern HPSG position for extraction. The traceless analysis (Sag and Fodor 1995; Bouma et al. 2001; Ginzburg and Sag 2000; Sag 2010) is presented as the one that derives the Conjunct Constraint with no stipulation, since "there is simply nothing to conjoin"; the trace-based accounts must stipulate it (Section 3, p. 668).
- Filler-gap dependencies are feature passing, not movement, so the derivation remains a continuous tree. Nothing in the chapter uses discontinuous constituents or word order domains; even extraposition is handled by a feature (EXTRA; Section 6, p. 678).
- Semantic scope is not subject to islands: coordination (footnote 4, p. 669), relative clauses (footnote 16, p. 674, with an example from Copestake et al. 2005), left branches (footnote 75, p. 696, again from Copestake et al. 2005), and "that"-clauses (footnote 76, p. 698). This fits scope-underspecified MRS, where the syntax does not have to enforce scope islands.
- Ellipsis under sluicing is a unary-branching construction over the remnant, with no deleted or silent structure (Section 13, p. 700). Unary rules are exactly what the tree encoding provides for.
- The chapter's overall position is that the grammar should be permissive about extraction and that the fine-grained filtering is probabilistic: speakers "attend to probabilistic information about the syntactic distribution of filler-gap dependencies" (Section 4.2, p. 676), and subject-island effects are "at least in part probabilistic" (Section 8.2, p. 690). A division of labor between a statistical component that proposes and a grammar that licenses is in line with this. The conclusion names the open problem directly: "it is unclear how to combine probabilistic information with syntactic, semantic and pragmatic representations", and points to Data-Oriented Parsing with HPSG (Neumann and Flickinger 1999, 2002; Arnold and Linardaki 2007; Bod et al. 2003; Bod 2009) as one avenue (Section 14, p. 701).
- A permissive grammar also means fewer spurious replay failures: if the coordination rule, the head-modifier rule, and S nodes say nothing special about SLASH or EXTRA (the position the chapter argues for, pp. 671-672, 680), attested "island-violating" sentences replay without special treatment.
- Extraction constraints that are grammatical are stated on lexical entries (prepositions, "assure", "that", wh-words, determiners), which is where the tagger's label puts information (Section 2, p. 667; Section 10, p. 695; Section 11, p. 697; Section 12, p. 699).

## Theory strains

- Traces or no traces. Pollard and Sag (1994) and Levine and Hukari (2006) keep traces and must stipulate that conjuncts are not empty; the traceless accounts derive this. Levine's (2017) alternative, that each conjunct needs a stressed syllable, is questioned: why would no such constraint hold for preposition stranding? (Section 3, p. 668, footnote 2.)
- Adjunct extraction. Making extractable adverbials members of ARG-ST (Ginzburg and Sag 2000) is a device introduced "in order to allow certain adverbials to be extractable"; Levine and Hukari (2006) are cited for a different view. Adnominal PP extraction is empirically unclear (Section 3, p. 668, footnote 3).
- The coordination schema. Gazdar (1981), GPSG, Pollard and Sag (1994: 202), and Beavers and Sag (2004) identify SLASH across conjuncts and mother, once counted as a major empirical win over movement accounts. Chaves (2003) and Levine and Hukari (2006: 354) hold that the rule should not mention SLASH at all, because of asymmetric coordination and summed ("pluralic") gaps; the Element Constraint is then pragmatic (Section 3, pp. 669-672). The Coordinate Structure Constraint "does not receive a unitary account in modern HPSG" (p. 672).
- Complex NP Constraint. A [SLASH { }] stipulation on modifiers (16) is "tempting" but contradicted by counterexamples in English and many other languages; the proposed explanations are information structure and processing (Section 4, pp. 672-675).
- D-linking. No non-circular definition; counterexamples arise out of the blue; no experimental evidence for D-linking of bare wh-words. Competing explanation: memory decay and plausibility (Section 4.1, pp. 675-676; Section 10, pp. 693-694).
- Processing accounts are themselves disputed. Sprouse et al. (2012a) find no correlation between working-memory measures and island acceptability; the reply questions the measures; Phillips (2013b) dismisses the reply. The chapter concludes only that it is "unclear to what extent" processing contributes (Section 4.2, p. 676).
- Subjacency. Bounding nodes as a language-specific parameter require innate part-of-speech knowledge (Müller 2020; Newmeyer 2004), and the specific claims for English and German are refuted by corpus data (Müller 2004, 2007; Meurers and Müller 2009; Strunk and Snider 2013) (Section 5, pp. 677-678).
- Right Roof Constraint. Stipulating empty EXTRA at S would model it, but counterexamples exist. "A detailed account of extraposition island phenomena does not exist in any framework" (Section 6, pp. 678-680).
- Freezing. Explained by a combination of local ambiguity, crossing dependencies, prosody of stranded prepositions, and a pragmatic clash between two focused constituents; no grammatical analysis is offered (Section 7, pp. 680-681).
- Subject islands. Data are contested (footnote 36 on PP extraction; footnote 60 on NP-embedded clausal subjects; footnote 53 on sentential subjects). Experimental results are mixed: effects persist in some studies and fade with exposure in others. "The SI constraint is not due to a single factor" (Section 8, pp. 681-690).
- Parasitic gaps. Examples with both gaps inside islands (49, 55a) should be out and are fine; "the notion of parasitic gap is rather dubious" (Section 8.2, p. 689; Section 9, p. 692).
- Adjunct islands. Truswell's (2011) event-semantic account is not confirmed experimentally and wrongly excludes extraction from tensed adjuncts (Section 9, p. 691, footnote 69). "It is doubtful that any purely syntactic account can describe all the empirical facts" (p. 693).
- Superiority. Three competing accounts: Pesetsky's D-linking with unselective binding, a processing account (Arnon et al. 2007), and the Ginzburg and Sag account with two lexical entries per wh-word keyed to accent. The last one encodes a prosodic generalization as lexical ambiguity. Experimental support for amelioration by a third wh-phrase is lacking (footnote 71) (Section 10, pp. 693-696).
- Left Branch Condition. Derived only by taking determiners and genitives out of the noun's valence (SELECT, a dedicated genitive construction with X-ARG). Languages that violate it get a tentative remark, not an analysis (Section 11, pp. 696-698).
- Complementizer Constraint. The Ginzburg and Sag treatment of the adverb effect "seems ad-hoc", though it fits French que/qui. The prosodic alternative (Kandybowicz 2006, 2009) found no experimental support in Ritchart et al. (2016). "Further research is needed to determine the true nature" of the effect (Section 12, pp. 698-699).
- Ellipsis. Deletion after movement (Merchant 2001) wrongly predicts island sensitivity under sluicing; HPSG uses direct interpretation from context (Section 13, p. 700).
- The general strain. HPSG has no explicit, testable account of island effects: "it is yet unclear how these factors can be brought together", and little work connects HPSG to processing models even though the theory was meant to be compatible with them (Section 14, pp. 700-701).

## Not covered here

All items in this section are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter never says which island constraints the ERG or any other implemented grammar actually imposes (for example on SLASH in coordination, on extraction from adjuncts or subjects, on extraposition). Caveat 1 depends on this, so it needs to be checked directly in the ERG rules and against treebanked counterexamples.
- The mechanics of how SLASH is introduced, passed, and bound (lexical rules or unary extraction rules, head-filler rule, list or set encoding) are left to Chapter 13. In a grammar that introduces gaps with unary phrasal rules, the gap site is a node in the derivation tree, which changes caveats 5 and 6.
- No analysis is given for how a filler is paired with one of several SLASH elements, or for summed gaps; the chapter only cites the literature.
- Resumptive pronouns are mentioned only in passing ("intrusive" resumptives, p. 692); right-node raising and other non-constituent coordination are not treated here.
- Nothing is said about how extraction or extraposition is represented in dependency frameworks, so every UD-bridge implication above is my inference from the HPSG analyses described.
- The chapter's garden-path material concerns human sentence processing; applying it to a search-free tagger (caveat 11) is my extrapolation.
