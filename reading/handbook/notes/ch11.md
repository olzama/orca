# Chapter 11: Complex predicates (Danièle Godard and Pollet Samvelian)

Handbook pages 419-488 (text 419-480, references 480-488). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter defines a complex predicate, in the HPSG tradition, as a construction of two or more words in which the head attracts the syntactic arguments of its predicate complement (argument attraction, also called composition, inheritance, or sharing; Hinrichs and Nakazawa 1989, 1994). It works through three guises. In Romance languages (Sections 3-4), attraction shows up as clitic climbing and other bounded dependencies, and it coexists with three different phrase structures: a flat VP (French and Italian auxiliaries, Italian restructuring verbs), a verbal complex (Spanish, Romanian), and a phrasal, possibly unsaturated complement (the copula). In German and Korean (Section 5), attraction shows up as word order freedom and remote passive, with a binary head-cluster phrase building the verbal complex. In Persian (Section 6), light verb constructions are multi-word lexemes that feed derivational morphology and have a range of semantics from compositional to idiomatic.

The chapter is purely theoretical. It never mentions parsing or any implemented grammar or system (no DELPH-IN, LKB, ACE, TRALE, or Enju), so every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is "not discussed". English appears only in one footnote (fn. 26, p. 460), which says that English has no head-cluster schema and takes saturated complements of the copula. The bearing on an ERG-first parser is therefore small; the bearing on the multi-language goal (Romance, German, Dutch, Korean, Persian, Hindi) is substantial.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Flat, n-ary head-complement phrases

- Severity: medium (none for the ERG; real for Romance and Korean grammars built on these analyses)
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 4.2, pp. 436-441 (Figure 4, p. 437); Section 4.3, pp. 441-442 (schema 24, Figure 5); Section 5.2.3, p. 466 (schema 58)
- What the chapter says: The head-complements-phrase (24) realizes a whole non-empty sublist of COMPS as sister daughters in one step. In the Italian flat structure, the restructuring verb, the infinitive, and the complements attracted from the infinitive are all sisters (Figure 5). For Korean the chapter adopts a head-subject-complements-phrase (58) that "combines a head with its subject and its complements in one go", with no LP constraints, so that all permutations of the dependents are licensed. The chapter notes that (24) also admits binary structures (German, schema 38), and that for Korean a binary-branching alternative exists (Kim 2016) whose differences from the flat one are "irrelevant for the purpose of this chapter".
- Why it matters: the flat structure is argued for empirically. It is what separates Italian (adverbs and inverted subjects between the two verbs, example 20a, 21a) from Spanish (verbal complex, nothing intervenes). A tree encoding that only produces binary nodes has to binarize these phrases, and the binarization must not collapse the Italian flat VP into the Spanish V-V cluster, because the two are distinguished by the LIGHT values of mother and daughters (constraint 26 for Spanish says the flat VP violates it). Remark of mine: this is a design consequence I am drawing, the chapter does not discuss binarization.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 2. Free complement order by discharging an arbitrary COMPS element

- Severity: medium to high for German-type grammars; none for the ERG
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 5.1.2, pp. 456-457 (schema 38, Figure 11)
- What the chapter says: The German head-complement-phrase (38), from Müller (2021b), splits the head daughter's COMPS into three parts, 1 ⊕ ⟨3⟩ ⊕ 2, and discharges the middle element. "The presentation of the list as composed of three parts, with the relevant one in any position, allows for a free order." The subject of a finite verb is on COMPS as well (Pollard 1996), so one schema introduces subjects and complements.
- Why it matters: the rule name alone does not say which COMPS element the non-head daughter saturates. Replay has to resolve the split of the list. When the daughter is compatible with more than one element (for example two NPs with syncretic case), the same tree with the same rule names licenses more than one feature structure and more than one semantics, so the derivation tree is not a complete recipe. Either the rule label must carry the position of the discharged element, or replay must be allowed a small, bounded search.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Valence of the head is open until the complement is known (argument attraction)

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3, pp. 426-428 (entries 8b, Figure 1); Section 4.1, pp. 434-435 (lexical rules 18); Section 4.4, p. 449 (33); Section 5.1.2, p. 458 (41, 42); Section 5.1.3, p. 461 (47); Section 5.2.3, p. 467 (60)
- What the chapter says: The attracting head has an ARG-ST or COMPS list containing a list variable that is identified with the SUBJ and COMPS of its verbal (or predicative) complement. Romance heads attract from the complement's ARG-ST or COMPS as a lexical word; German heads always attract from COMPS, because of partial verb phrase fronting, where the fronted verb has already combined with a subset of its complements and "only the remaining complements are attracted" (p. 458). Attraction iterates through chains of verbs (Figures 12 and 14).
- Why it matters: the lexical type of an auxiliary, modal, copula, or restructuring verb does not fix how many complements it takes. With the binary head-cluster phrase this is harmless for replay, since the verbs combine first and the lists become instantiated bottom-up (Figures 12-14). In the flat Romance structure, and with the German entry order in which the verbal complement is the last element of the list, the number and identity of the remaining daughters is only known after the verbal complement is unified in, so the order in which replay unifies the daughters matters. For the tagger, the label of the head verb cannot encode a fixed valence frame; the frame is read off the complement.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Two lexical descriptions for the same verb with no semantic difference, and optional coherence

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (label ambiguity, treebank consistency) / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 2.1, pp. 422-423 (examples 4a, 4b); Section 4.1, pp. 431-436 (lexical rules 18); Section 5.1.1, p. 451; Section 5.1.2, p. 457; Section 7, p. 479
- What the chapter says: Restructuring verbs "have two possible descriptions", an ordinary verb with a VP complement and a complex predicate head, related by the Argument Attraction Lexical Rules (18). There is "no evident semantic distinction" between the two uses of Italian *volere* (p. 422-423). Membership in the class is lexical and varies between speakers (*sembrare*, p. 435; Spanish, p. 436). In German, "many verbs can be constructed either way" (p. 451), and with binary branching the verbal complex "only shows up structurally when there is a series of verbs" (p. 457).
- Why it matters: when no clitic, intervening adverb, scrambling, or passive disambiguates, the same string has two derivations with the same meaning. The tagger must learn a consistent choice, and the evidence for the choice (clitic position on another verb, an adverb elsewhere in the clause) is non-local to the token that carries the lexical-rule label. For the UD bridge, both derivations should map to the same dependency tree, so the HPSG-to-UD direction is many-to-one and the UD-to-HPSG direction needs a default.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Adverb scope inside a coherence field is not fixed by the adverb's position

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 5.1.2, pp. 455-456 (examples 37b-d, fn. 20); Section 5.1.3, pp. 459-460 (example 44)
- What the chapter says: "In a coherence field, an adverb can scope over any of the verbs that belong to it." In (37b) *oft* precedes *zu reparieren* while modifying *versucht*; (37c) and (44) are given two translations each, one per scope. The chapter uses this as a diagnostic for a single coherence field and does not give the mechanism that derives the two readings.
- Why it matters: if an adverb attached at one position can take scope over different verbs of the cluster, either the derivation tree must differ per reading (the tagger then has to choose a reading with no string evidence), or one tree corresponds to several semantic representations and MRS is no longer a deterministic by-product unless scope is left underspecified. The chapter does not say which.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Pronominal clitics are affixes; a climbed clitic is an argument of a different word

- Severity: medium for Romance, none for English
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / UD-bridge / semantics
- Reference: Section 3, pp. 427-430 (ARP 9, Figures 2 and 3); Section 4.3, pp. 443-445 (Figure 7); Section 4.4, pp. 449-451 (Figure 9)
- What the chapter says: Following Miller and Sag (1997), Romance clitics are affixes. An argument typed *aff* stays on ARG-ST, is absent from COMPS, and is realized by a morphological rule on a *reduced-verb*. In clitic climbing the affixal argument originates on the ARG-ST of the participle, infinitive, or adjective and is realized on the auxiliary, restructuring verb, or copula (*l'a lu*, *lo-vuole dare*, *leur-sera fidèle*). The leaves in the figures are *l'a*, *lo-vuole*, *leur-sera*.
- Why it matters: the HPSG leaf is the host verb with its clitics, and the clitic's semantic role belongs to a different leaf. If the token stream splits clitics from the verb, leaves no longer equal tokens and the tagger must label a host plus its clitics as one unit, with the reduced-verb rule in the unary chain. In the conversion to dependencies, the clitic's governor in the derivation (the host) differs from the predicate it is an argument of. Remark of mine: UD tokenization and attachment of clitics is not discussed in the chapter; the mismatch needs to be checked against the UD guidelines.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Domain union is still needed for some scrambling

- Severity: high for Korean-type grammars that follow this analysis; none for English
- Assumption affected: continuity
- Reference: Section 5.2.1, p. 464 (examples 53, 54); background in Section 5.1.2, pp. 453-455
- What the chapter says: Korean control verbs allow the same interleaving of matrix and embedded arguments as auxiliaries (53b) but fail the case-alternation and negative-polarity tests for argument attraction. The authors follow Kim (2016) in giving them VP complements, "and scrambling in (53) must be due to a different process (that is, domain union, as in Lee 2001; see Reape 1994)". In domain union "the domain for ordering is larger than the domain of constituency, so that the elements belonging to different phrases can be reordered and interleaved" (p. 455).
- Why it matters: this is a discontinuous VP. A concatenative derivation tree cannot represent (53b) under this analysis, so a Korean grammar would need either a different analysis of control-verb scrambling or a tree encoding that handles discontinuity.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Relational constraints beyond unification

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (engine expressiveness)
- Reference: Section 3, p. 427 (ARP 9 and fn. 5); Section 4.3, pp. 441, 444 (24, 26); Section 5.1.2, p. 456 (38); Section 5.2.3, p. 466 (58)
- What the chapter says: The analyses use append with unknown split points (1 ⊕ ⟨3⟩ ⊕ 2), "contained list difference" in the Argument Realization Principle (the COMPS list is ARG-ST minus the subject minus a list of non-canonical elements, and the relation is undefined when the removed list is not a sublist), the relational constraint synsem2sign that converts a list of synsems into a list of daughter signs, parametric list types such as list(non-canonical) and list([LIGHT 1]), and optional elements (a tag in place of a singleton list for a possibly absent subject).
- Why it matters: a small engine that only does typed unification over fixed-arity rules cannot state these directly. Each needs a compiled-out encoding (one rule or lexical type per split point or per realization pattern), which multiplies rule names and tagger labels.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Lexical rules whose applicability depends on inherited arguments (medio-passive, remote passive)

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 432-433 (rule 13, examples 12); Section 5.1.2, pp. 455-456 (examples 37a, b); Section 5.2.1, pp. 463-464 (example 52)
- What the chapter says: The Italian medio-passive lexical rule (13) requires an input with an accusative NP complement. The restructuring verb *potere* qualifies only because it inherits that NP from its infinitive. In the German remote passive, *zu reparieren versucht* "behaves like a passivized transitive verb" and the object of the embedded infinitive becomes the nominative subject of the passive auxiliary. Korean long passive is parallel.
- Why it matters: the unary rule sits on the attracting verb, but its input condition is satisfied by material that only becomes known when the verbal complement is combined. Replay cannot validate the lexical rule at the leaf; failure is detected late, higher in the tree. The tagger has to put a passive or medio-passive label on a verb whose own lexical entry has no object.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 10. Non-constituent coordination and coordination of bare participles

- Severity: low to medium (the chapter defers the analysis to Chapter 16)
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / continuity
- Reference: Section 4, fn. 6, p. 430; Section 4.2, pp. 439-441 (examples 22, 23)
- What the chapter says: Tense auxiliaries can take a coordination of participles (*a acheté et lu ce livre*), which "may be seen as raising a difficulty" because argument structure characterizes words; the authors find it "plausible" that such coordinations inherit an argument structure from the conjuncts. Italian (22a), a restructuring verb with a clitic scoping over a coordination of infinitive-plus-complements sequences, is treated as a possible non-constituent coordination, subject to a same-syntactic-level constraint on the parallel elements.
- Why it matters: a coordination node has to expose ARG-ST or COMPS for attraction to go through, which is an extra requirement on the coordination rules. Non-constituent coordination is a known problem for a plain concatenative tree, and the chapter gives no rule for it.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 11. Light verb constructions: several lexemes per light verb, selected by the noun, with non-compositional semantics

- Severity: medium for Persian-type languages (also Korean, Hindi); low for English
- Assumption affected: semantics / tree-encoding / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 6.1, pp. 469-472; Section 6.3, pp. 475-479 (entries 79, 81, 83, 85)
- What the chapter says: Persian has about 250 simplex verbs; most verbal meanings are noun (or adjective, particle, PP) plus light verb. The parts are separate words: they can be separated by an auxiliary or a PP, coordinated, extracted (67), and the noun can become a passive subject (68). The verb *zadan* 'hit' gets four lexemes in the chapter: one whose content is identified with the noun's content (79), one that adds a BACKGROUND condition with an existentially quantified instrument (81), one with an unpredictable 'pretend' relation over a class of nouns (83), and an idiomatic one that selects the noun through LID (85). The instances "do not share a common core meaning".
- Why it matters: the lexical type of the light verb is determined by which noun it combines with, possibly at a distance or through an extraction path, so the tagger faces a large verb-sense ambiguity keyed to another token. Semantics is assembled non-homomorphically: in (79) two tokens yield one relation, in (81) a relation and a quantified background condition appear with no token of their own, in (85) the noun's relation is an idiomatic one. The chapter's semantics is stated with CONT|NUCLEUS and BACKGROUND, not MRS; how the BACKGROUND set would be represented in MRS is left open. For the UD bridge, the syntactic head is the light verb while the predicate meaning sits largely with the noun.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 12. Word-internal structure with argument attraction (compounds built on complex predicates)

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / semantics
- Reference: Section 6.2, pp. 472-475 (representations 74-76, example 77)
- What the chapter says: Complex predicates feed derivation: *bāz-konande* 'opener' is a lexeme with two morphological daughters (M-DTRS, after Bonami and Crysmann 2018), an adjective and a verb standing in the same attraction and semantic relation as in the syntactic complex predicate, plus the suffix *-ande*. The simplex participle *\*konande* does not exist. The compound noun takes the NP expected by the verb as its complement (77).
- Why it matters: a single token contains two lexemes with their own valence and semantics. If such forms are to be analyzed productively, the per-token label needs a compound rule over two lexical entries, not a unary chain over one. The alternative is to list them.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 13. Smaller points

- Unary phrase that changes LIGHT (low; tree-encoding). Section 4.3 fn. 13, p. 442, and Section 4.4, p. 449, Figure 9: "the head-only phrase is non-light", and a lexical predicative complement of the copula is made [LIGHT−] by a unary-branching phrase. This is a string-vacuous unary rule the tagger must place. It fits the unary/binary assumption.
- Mention of traces (low; no-empty-elements). Fn. 22, p. 456: LIGHT is outside LOCAL "because an extracted constituent may differ from its trace as regards lightness" (Müller 1996, 2021b). The German analysis the chapter builds on assumes traces for extraction; the chapter defers to Chapter 13.
- Null pronouns as an option (low; no-empty-elements). Fn. 10, p. 435: "in a grammar with null pronouns", impersonal and unaccusative verbs could get a null pronoun subject, which would unify the two attraction lexical rules (Monachesi 1998). The chapter's own *null-pro* is a synsem subtype on ARG-ST (Figure 2, p. 428; entry 15, p. 433; *pro* in entry 83, p. 477) and is not a phrase-structure leaf.
- Unrealized and absent subjects (low; semantics / UD-bridge). German subjectless copular clauses (46, p. 460); Korean subjects "not always realized" (p. 467).
- Korean afterthought construction (low; continuity). Example (57a), p. 465, and p. 468: a post-verbal NP "requires an additional mechanism", not given.
- Gapping (low). Example (6), pp. 425-426, is used only as a headedness test; no analysis is given.

## Support for the design

- Argument attraction is preferred over domain union for the central cases. For German coherent constructions (Section 5.1.2, pp. 453-456) and Korean auxiliaries (Section 5.2.1, pp. 462-464) the chapter considers a linearization analysis (same tree as the incoherent construction, larger order domain) and rejects it because of remote passive, case alternation, and negative-polarity licensing. The adopted analysis keeps continuous constituents and moves the work into valence lists. This is the analysis type the design assumes.
- The verbal complex is binary. The head-cluster-phrase (25, p. 443; 40, p. 457; 61, p. 468) combines a light head with one light complement and yields a light phrase. Chains of verbs are nested binary nodes (Figures 10b, 12, 14).
- German clause structure in the adopted analysis is binary: one complement discharged per head-complement node (38, p. 456; Figure 11).
- The phenomenon is "lexically driven" (Section 7, p. 479): attraction is stated in lexical entries and lexical rules, structure in a small number of schemas. That matches a division of labor between a tag (lexical type plus unary lexical-rule chain) and a few binary rules.
- No empty elements in the chapter's own analyses. Clitics are affixes, null pronouns and gaps are synsem subtypes on ARG-ST (Figure 2, p. 428), and every tree in the chapter has only overt leaves.
- English is essentially outside the phenomenon. Fn. 26, p. 460 (after Müller 2009): the copula entry (47) "also works for English and other Germanic SVO languages"; since these "do not have a Head-Cluster Schema", the copula goes through the Head-Complement Schema with saturated complements, so the attracted list is empty. The chapter gives no English complex predicate.
- The same three ingredients (attraction in the lexicon, a head-cluster schema, a LIGHT feature) are reused across Romance, German, Korean, with language-specific constraints added (26 for Spanish; AUX + for Korean). This favors a shared core of coarse schemas across languages, which is also what the "relaxed" fallback relies on.
- Syntax and semantics are explicitly decoupled: "complex predicates show that syntax and semantics are not always isomorphic" (p. 423), and attraction "does not induce a specific semantic combination" (p. 479). The semantics is specified in the lexical entries, so it remains recoverable from the derivation as long as the right entry is chosen (see caveat 11).

## Theory strains

- No agreed definition of "complex predicate" (Section 2.1, pp. 420-423). Syntactic definition (argument attraction) versus semantic definition (one situation; Butt 2010) versus word-internal decomposition (Hale and Keyser 1997). The authors set the semantic definition aside. Serial verb constructions are excluded because they "do not share a grammatical mechanism" (p. 423). Japanese causatives are excluded as lexical (Manning et al. 1999), against Gunji's (1999) dual representation (fn. 2, p. 422).
- Phrase structure of Romance complex predicates (Sections 4.2-4.3, pp. 436-441; fn. 7, p. 430). Flat VP versus verbal complex versus VP complement. Andrews and Manning (1999, LFG) assume a VP complement throughout and use the coordination data (22a) as support; the authors answer with a non-constituent coordination analysis and an extra same-level constraint, and object that the LFG account cannot separate Italian from Spanish. Within HPSG, Aguila-Multner and Crysmann (2020) reanalyze French auxiliaries as periphrasis with a VP complement.
- Argument attraction versus domain union (Section 5.1.2, pp. 453-456; Section 5.2.1, pp. 462-464). The chapter argues for attraction but ends up needing both mechanisms for Korean: attraction for auxiliaries, domain union for control verbs (p. 464). Chung (1998) treats control verbs as complex predicate heads; Kim (2016) does not (fn. 27, p. 461).
- Lexical versus phrasal source of attraction in Korean (fn. 31, p. 467). Kim (2016) uses a Head-LEX construction; the chapter adopts Chung's (1998) lexical analysis "for reasons of space and to make a comparison ... easier".
- Flat versus binary clause structure (Section 5.2.3, p. 466; schema 24 versus 38, fn. 21, p. 456). The choice for Korean is declared irrelevant to the chapter. German subjects are complements (Pollard 1996), Romance subjects are not (p. 456).
- The LIGHT / LEX / WEIGHT feature (Section 4.3, p. 442; fn. 14, p. 443; fn. 22-23, pp. 456-457). Three names for one device; "the properties of light elements may differ depending on the language". Authors disagree on where it is specified: the chapter states LIGHT on the mother and both daughters of the head-cluster phrase, following Hinrichs and Nakazawa (1994) and De Kuthy and Meurers (2001), "contrary to Müller", who leaves the non-head daughter, head daughter, and mother unspecified in grammars of German so that auxiliary flip with non-verbal material is possible. LIGHT is placed outside LOCAL for extraction reasons.
- Language-particular stipulations that carry the Italian/Spanish contrast (Section 4.3, pp. 443-444). Italian simply "lacks the head-cluster-phrase"; Spanish gets an extra biconditional constraint (26) tying a phrase's LIGHT value to that of its non-head daughters. Speaker variation in Spanish and Italian is acknowledged but not modeled (pp. 435, 436, 438).
- Source list for attraction differs by language (Section 5.1.2, p. 458). Romance entries attract from ARG-ST or COMPS of a basic verb; German "always attracts ... from the COMPS list", forced by partial verb phrase fronting.
- Doubling of lexical entries (Section 4.1, pp. 434-435; Section 7, p. 479). Restructuring verbs need two descriptions linked by lexical rules, and separate rules for control and raising verbs to cover subjectless complements; a null-pronoun subject would allow one rule (fn. 10). The copula by contrast gets one underspecified entry.
- Basic-verb versus reduced-verb (Section 3, pp. 428-430). The distinction is carried as a HEAD value and is needed to stop the participle from hosting the clitic. That French participles never host clitics is "assume[d] to be a morphological property"; Italian participles can host clitics except under the auxiliary, handled by the auxiliary selecting a basic verb.
- Coordination of words with argument structure (fn. 6, p. 430). Acknowledged as a difficulty for attraction from ARG-ST; the answer is given as "plausible".
- Persian complex predicates between morphology and syntax (Sections 6.1-6.2, pp. 469-475). Syntactically separable words that jointly behave as a lexeme and feed derivation. The chapter's compound analysis for *-ande* nominalization differs from Müller (2010) (fn. 32, p. 473), whose paper argues against inheritance-based accounts.
- Light verb semantics (Section 6.3, pp. 475-479). No common core meaning for *zadan*; four lexemes related by "family resemblance", with the generalizations said to be factored out in an inheritance hierarchy whose "details cannot be discussed here". Idioms need the LID device (Sag 2007, 2012).

## Not covered here

All items in this section are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter says nothing about implemented grammars. Whether the ERG, the DELPH-IN grammars for German, Spanish, Korean, or Japanese, or TRALE grammars use argument composition, a head-cluster rule, or binary versus flat structures has to be checked in the grammars themselves.
- English constructions that could be treated as complex predicates (auxiliary chains, light verbs such as *take a walk*, particle verbs, resultatives) are not analyzed. German particle verbs and resultatives are named as coherent constructions (p. 451) with a pointer to Müller (2002) only.
- Dutch cross-serial dependencies are cited (Rentier 1994; Bouma and van Noord 1998) but not presented; they are the classic challenge for continuous trees in verb clusters.
- German verb-second and verb-initial clauses, and how the verbal complex interacts with verb movement or its trace-less alternatives, are outside the chapter (all German examples are verb-final embedded clauses or simple main clauses without analysis).
- German auxiliary flip and the "third construction" are mentioned only in fn. 14 and in reference titles.
- Romance causative and perception verbs are set aside (p. 430, fn. 9), as are Hindi aspectual complex predicates beyond one paragraph (p. 423).
- No MRS: the semantics is given in the *soa* / NUCLEUS format with BACKGROUND. How argument attraction interacts with MRS composition (for example, which handle or index an attracted argument is linked to, and how adverb scope in a cluster is represented) is not addressed.
- Universal Dependencies conventions for auxiliaries, copulas, clitics, and light verb constructions are not in the chapter; the UD-bridge remarks under caveats 4, 6, and 11 need to be verified against the UD guidelines.
- Computational consequences of argument composition (lists of unknown length during parsing) are not mentioned.
