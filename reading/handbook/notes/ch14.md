# Chapter 14: Relative clauses in HPSG (Doug Arnold and Danièle Godard)

Handbook pages 595-663 (text 595-649, references from 649). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter surveys HPSG analyses of relative clauses and related constructions. Section 2 sets out the shared core (a filler-gap dependency handled by SLASH, pied-piping handled by a second nonlocal feature REL, and co-indexation of the antecedent with the relativized constituent through MOD) and then the two main approaches: the lexical analysis of Pollard and Sag (1994), which uses a phonologically empty relativizer R, and the constructional analysis of Sag (1997), which uses cross-classified phrasal types and no empty elements. Section 3 covers non-finite wh-relatives, complementizer relatives (Arabic, English *that*, French *dont*), bare relatives (Japanese, Korean, English), reduced relatives, and non-restrictive relatives. Section 4 covers extraposition, relative-like clauses that are complements (clefts, superlatives, French predicative relatives), Korean dependent-noun and pseudo-relative constructions, internally headed relatives, free relatives, pseudo-clefts, and transparent free relatives. The authors conclude that relative clauses "are not a natural kind" (p. 648).

The chapter is theoretical. Its body does not mention DELPH-IN, the ERG, the LKB, ACE, TRALE, or Enju. The only remark on processing is footnote 54 (p. 639) on Müller (2004). Implementation-related titles appear in the reference list only (Crysmann 2005; Haddar et al. 2009; Zalila and Haddar 2011; Kim and Yang 2003) and are cited in the text for their linguistic analyses. Every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is therefore "not discussed", with the nearest relevant statement added where there is one. Bearing on the parser is high: relative clauses are frequent in English, and the chapter shows exactly where the design's assumptions (no empty elements, unary and binary rules, continuity, deterministic semantics) are supported and where they are bought at the price of extra constructions.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. The lexical analysis needs an empty relativizer; the trace-free alternative needs dedicated constructions

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements / leaves-equal-tokens
- Reference: Section 2.2, pp. 607-609 (entry (23), Figure 3); Section 3.3.2, pp. 630, 632 (Figure 9); Section 3.4, p. 634; footnote 31, p. 619; footnote 34, p. 620
- What the chapter says: Pollard and Sag (1994: Chapter 5), building on Borsley (1989), give relative clauses a phonologically empty head R that takes the wh-phrase and a slashed S as arguments, carries the MOD value, and builds the noun-modifying content. A one-argument variant of R licenses English bare relatives. Other analyses in the same style: Holler (2003) for German clause-adjoined non-restrictive relatives, a phonetically null complementizer for Arabic relatives with indefinite antecedents (Alqurashi and Borsley 2012), and phonologically null relative pronouns for Irish (Assmann et al. 2010). The chapter states the trade-off directly: there is an "impossibility of having an entirely lexical and non-constructional account of bare relatives that does not employ empty elements" (p. 632), because a lexical verb entry with MOD and SLASH (57) cannot stop the SLASH value from being inherited further up, which would license a spurious extra filler (58).
- Why it matters: any grammar following the lexical analysis has a leaf with no token. The design is safe only for grammars that take the constructional route, and that route moves the work into rule names that the tagger must predict without an overt cue (caveat 2).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter says Sag's (1997) constructional analysis "completely dispenses with phonologically empty elements" and that this style "has dominated work in HPSG in the last two decades" (pp. 596, 609).

### 2. Bare relatives are licensed by a phrasal unary construction with no overt marker

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3.3.2, pp. 629-632 (constraint (55), Figure 10, footnote 44); Section 2.3, p. 610 (Figure 4)
- What the chapter says: In Sag (1997), "the cakes Kim bought" contains a unary-branching construction non-wh-rel-cl: a clause whose head daughter has an NP in SLASH becomes a noun-modifying clause with empty SLASH, co-indexed with the modified nominal. A subtype (simp-inf-rel-cl) covers "book (for Sam) to read". The slashed daughter is a VP, not an S, which encodes that the gap cannot be the subject (56).
- Why it matters: this unary node sits above a whole clause, far from any lexical rule chain, and nothing in the string marks it. The tree encoding must be able to place phrasal unary nodes at arbitrary heights, and the tagger must predict them from context alone. Replay will catch a missing unary node (SLASH is not bound, MOD is absent) but cannot repair it.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Extraposed relative clauses: three analyses, each straining a different assumption

- Severity: high
- Assumption affected: continuity / UD-bridge / deterministic-replay / semantics
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 637-639 (examples (67)-(69), footnote 54)
- What the chapter says: Relative clauses can be separated from their antecedent in English ("Someone might win who does not deserve it"). Three approaches exist. (a) Order domains (Nerbonne 1994; Kathol and Pollard 1995): the clause combines with the nominal as usual but stays a separate DOMAIN element, so its phonology is "contributed discontinuously". (b) A nonlocal feature EXTRA (Keller 1995; Bouma 1996; Müller 1999a, 2004; Crysmann 2005, 2013): the clause makes its semantic contribution "downstairs" as a modifier but is not realized there; its LOCAL value is put on EXTRA, inherited upward, and discharged by a daughter that "contributes its phonology in the normal way, but makes no semantic contribution". The bottom of the dependency needs "a family of lexical items derived by lexical rule, or a non-branching construction", and the top needs a special construction. (c) Kiss (2005), adopted by Crysmann (2004) and Walker (2017): the clause is an ordinary daughter in its surface position, and modification is generalized so that its index "may be identified with that of any suitable constituent within the sister", through a set-valued ANCHORS feature inherited upward; this relies on MRS or LRS. The chapter judges that "there seems to be little to choose between these alternatives" on theoretical grounds.
- Why it matters: (a) breaks continuity. (b) keeps continuous trees and fits replay, but adds an unmarked lexical rule or unary rule on the antecedent nominal that the tagger must predict, and the modified noun is not the sister of the clause, so the UD head of the clause must be read from the feature structure. (c) keeps the tree simple, but, as the chapter describes it, the tree does not say which nominal inside the sister is modified; if the grammar leaves that open, the same derivation yields several semantic results and the MRS is no longer a deterministic by-product (this last inference is ours).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. Footnote 54: Müller (2004) compared "computer processability" of linearization-based grammars and continuous grammars with EXTRA and preferred linearization "for computational reasons", but later gave it up for theoretical reasons concerning German clause structure. Crysmann (2005), cited for the EXTRA approach, has "efficient and portable implementation" in its title; the chapter does not describe it.

### 4. Restrictive versus non-restrictive relatives: same syntax, different semantics

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics / deterministic-replay / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 3.4, pp. 633-636 (examples (61)-(66), footnotes 51-52)
- What the chapter says: Non-restrictive relatives take wide scope "much like independent utterances" and accept non-nominal antecedents (clauses, VPs). Arnold (2004) assumes "a uniform syntax" for both kinds and two ways of combining the clause with its head: restrictively, or non-restrictively with the content contributed at the level of the root clause. Non-restrictive combination requires a finite wh-relative, and [MOD noun] becomes a requirement of restrictive semantics only. Lee-Goldman (2012) uses a multidimensional CONTENT inspired by Potts (2005). In (64) the antecedent is not syntactically realized in the sentence; Arnold and Borsley (2008) handle this with the ellipsis and fragment analysis of Ginzburg and Sag (2000). The chapter notes that non-restrictive relatives "are often set off intonationally".
- Why it matters: for a finite wh-relative on a noun, both combinations are licensed, so strings such as (61a) and (61b) differ only in which combining rule is named. The tagger must choose from commas or context, and replay cannot detect the wrong choice. Wide-scope content needs a place in the MRS outside the normal restriction. For clausal or VP antecedents the UD attachment site is not a noun. Antecedents supplied by the discourse are outside what a sentence-level replay can compute.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Order domains for preposed supplemental relatives in English

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: continuity
- Reference: Section 3.4, p. 636 (example (66))
- What the chapter says: In "It may happen now, or — which would be worse — it may happen later", the relative precedes its clausal antecedent and follows the coordinator. Lee-Goldman (2012) combines the *which* clause with a clause whose DOMAIN begins with a coordinator and places the relative's domain after the coordinator, using Reape-style domains; a separate special construction handles "What is worse, ...".
- Why it matters: an English construction analyzed with discontinuous combination. It is rare, and a continuous analysis (the relative attaching to the clause after the coordinator) is conceivable, but the chapter gives only the domain analysis.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Set union, disjoint union, default constraints, and TO-BIND in the nonlocal machinery

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: other (the feature-structure engine: replay by unification alone)
- Reference: Section 2.2, p. 609; Section 2.3, pp. 611-613 (constraints (26), (27), footnotes 23, 25, 26); footnote 33, p. 620; Section 3.2.3, pp. 625-626 (rule (47))
- What the chapter says: SLASH and REL are set-valued. In Pollard and Sag (1994) the mother's value is "the union of the values on the daughters, less any indicated as being discharged" through TO-BIND. Sag (1997) uses disjoint union in the head-filler constraint and for RESTR; the authors replace the latter with ordinary union and follow Copestake et al. (2005) in treating RESTR as multisets. Propagation of REL (and, following Ginzburg and Sag 2000, of all nonlocal features) is stated on words as a default: a word's REL value is "by default" the union of its arguments' REL values, and "since these are only default principles, they can be overridden", for example by the requirement that clauses have empty REL. Alqurashi and Borsley (2012) likewise govern SLASH inheritance by a default principle.
- Why it matters: set union, set difference, and overridable defaults are not plain unification. A replay engine needs these compiled into list or difference-list operations and into hard constraints, with every override spelled out as a separate type or rule.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Where the noun-modifying semantics is built differs between analyses, and so does the rule that joins clause and noun

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: semantics / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 2.1.2, pp. 605-607; Section 2.3, pp. 613-615 (constraint (27), Figure 6, footnotes 22, 26, 27)
- What the chapter says: A relative clause has propositional "internal" content but must contribute a restriction on the noun's index. Pollard and Sag (1994) put the conversion in the empty relativizer. Sag (1997) puts it in a dedicated head-relative-phrase, whose semantics is "not the normal semantics associated with head-adjunct phrases". Sag (2010: 522), Müller (1999a: 95), and Müller and Machicao y Priemer (2019) put it in the relative clause construction itself, so that the combination with the noun is an ordinary head-adjunct phrase. In addition, Sag (1997) attaches wh-relatives to NP, not N (footnote 22), supported by Kiss (2005) with "hydras" ("The boy and the girl who dated each other"); this "requires a different approach to semantic composition", for example MRS (Chaves 2007) or LRS (Walker 2017).
- Why it matters: rule inventories and tree shapes for the same string differ by analysis (special head-relative rule or generic head-adjunct rule; attachment below or above the determiner). The design is unaffected as long as one grammar is fixed, but derivations are not comparable across grammars, and NP-level attachment only gives the right restriction under MRS-style composition. For hydras the antecedent is a coordination with a sum index, which a dependency tree has no single head for.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter names MRS as one of the semantic frameworks that make NP attachment workable.

### 8. Free relatives: no consistent head, exocentric schema, case syncretism

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / UD-bridge / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 4.2.3, pp. 643-646 (examples (74)-(80), footnotes 64-66)
- What the chapter says: "She ate what I suggested" has the distribution of an NP, and the wh-phrase must fit both the gap inside the clause and the position outside it (the matching effect). Treating the wh-phrase as head (Kubota 2003) is "inconsistent with" its being the filler; treating the clause as head leaves the distribution unexplained. German shows case matching that is satisfied by syncretic forms ("was" for nominative and accusative, (79c)) and also genuinely mismatching free relatives; Müller (1999a: 96) uses "a schema that explicitly does not project the category of the relative phrase but a related category", and footnote 66 states that "no account assuming any of the material in the relative clause to be the head can account for the data". Agreement splits as well: in (80a) the clause-internal verb agrees with "whoever's dogs" while the free relative as a whole is singular like "whoever".
- Why it matters: the mother category is computed by a special, non-headed rule, so the "head" that a UD conversion would pick is not given by the rule's head daughter. A form that must satisfy two case requirements at once needs case values that unify with both; with a naive atomic case value, strict replay fails on grammatical input.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Filler-gap identity is too strict for some grammatical English relatives

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (strict output failing on grammatical input)
- Reference: Section 3.2.2, pp. 620-622 (examples (39)-(40), footnote 36); Section 3.4, pp. 635-636 (example (65))
- What the chapter says: SLASH inheritance "ensures identity between the LOCAL values of filler and gap (including, of course CASE)". If "that" or "who" is nominative (the explanation offered by Pollard and Sag and by Sag for "*to who I spoke"), then "the person who I spoke to" has a nominative filler for a non-nominative gap; the chapter can only "hope that a treatment of other filler-gap mismatches will provide an account" (footnote 36). In "Kim has ridden a camel, which Sam never would" an NP filler corresponds to a position where an NP is impossible; Arnold and Borsley (2010) let the elided VP argument of an auxiliary carry an NP in SLASH.
- Why it matters: a grammar that enforces full LOCAL identity rejects ordinary sentences unless it contains extra lexical entries or relaxed constraints for these cases. Each such fix is a further unmarked lexical variant for the tagger.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 10. Category of *that* and of reduced relatives decides the tree and the dependency label

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3.2.2, pp. 620-622 (footnotes 35, 43); Section 3.3.2, pp. 632-633 (examples (59), entry (60)); footnote 9, p. 602
- What the chapter says: Relative "that" has been analyzed as a marker in some clauses and a relative pronoun in others (Pollard and Sag 1994), as a relative pronoun throughout (Sag 1997), and as a complementizer heading a head-complement structure (the option the chapter leans toward). Reduced relatives ("a person standing by the door", "a person fond of children") are a clausal construction with a PRO subject in Sag (1997) and plain lexical modifiers in the authors' preferred account. "The time when ...", "the reason why ..." are "more plausibly analysed as complements of nouns".
- Why it matters: under the pronoun analysis "that" is a filler in a head-filler phrase and bears a grammatical function inside the clause; under the complementizer analysis it is a head taking the clause as complement. The bridge to UD must map whichever analysis the grammar chose onto one fixed UD convention, and the presence or absence of a clausal node over a participle changes the tree to encode. (The chapter does not discuss UD.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 11. Relative-like clauses in complement position, and clefts without an analysis

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / deterministic-replay (coverage)
- Reference: Section 4.2.1, pp. 640-641 (examples (70)-(71)); Section 4.2.4, pp. 646-647 (examples (81)-(82))
- What the chapter says: Clauses with relative-clause internal structure occur as complements: of superlatives ("the most interesting that we have heard"), of German "diejenige" determiners, in it-clefts, and as French predicative relatives after perception verbs (Koenig and Lambrecht 1999: REL-marked clauses with both an internal and an external subject). It-clefts are analyzed, following Pollard and Sag (1994: 260-262), through a lexical entry for "be" with an "it" subject, an XP, and an S with an XP gap. That entry does not extend to "It was Sam on whom she particularly focused her attention", and "it is not obvious how this problem should be dealt with". For pseudo-clefts it is "not obvious" whether the wh-clause is an interrogative (Yoo 2003) or a free relative (Gerbl 2007). Transparent free relatives take their category from an internal "nucleus" ("what anyone would consider belligerent" is an AP).
- Why it matters: the same clause-internal structure maps to different external relations, so the UD label cannot be read from the clause's own rule name. Constructions with no settled analysis are places where strict replay is likely to fail and the relaxed output must take over.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. Yoo (2008) and Kim (2011) are said to handle transparent free relatives "using the existing apparatus with only minor adjustments".

### 12. Zero-marked lexical rules that redirect dependencies

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (size and predictability of the label set)
- Reference: Section 3.2.3, pp. 622-626 (entries (43), (47), footnote 39); footnote 18, p. 609; Section 3.3.1, p. 627
- What the chapter says: French "dont" relatives with a resumptive pronoun are licensed by a lexical rule on propositional attitude and communication predicates (Abeillé and Godard 2007): the verb binds a pronominal SLASH element coming from its CP complement and introduces a co-indexed PP[de] gap in its own SLASH. The rule has no morphological reflex. Pollard and Sag's Subject Extraction Lexical Rule and the lexical-rule alternative for Japanese adnominal verbs (after Müller 2002) are of the same kind. Coordination of a gap conjunct with a resumptive conjunct needs "a formal complication" left aside in the chapter.
- Why it matters: the label of an ordinary verb such as "est certain" or "déclare" must carry an invisible rule whose trigger lies elsewhere in the sentence (a "dont" above, a pronoun below). The sequence labeler can learn this, but the dependency of the label on distant material is the kind of case where it will make errors that replay then rejects.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 13. Constructions where the grammar leaves the antecedent link to pragmatics or to an unspecified choice

- Severity: medium for Japanese, Korean, and languages with internally headed relatives; none for English
- Assumption affected: semantics / deterministic-replay / leaves-equal-tokens
- Reference: Section 3.3.1, pp. 627-628 (example (51)); Section 4.2.2, pp. 641-643 (examples (72)-(73), footnote 60)
- What the chapter says: Sirai and Gunji (1998) analyze local Japanese relativization without SLASH, through tense affixes that are syntactic heads combining with the verb by predicate composition; nonlocal relativization involves null pronominals, with co-indexation left to "a pragmatic condition that requires the relative clause be 'about' the modified noun". Korean pseudo-relatives ("the smell of rubber burning") have no gap at all. For internally headed relatives (Lakhota, Dogon, Quechua), Pollard and Sag (1994: 232-236) assume an exocentric NP whose nominal "consists of just a clause"; the REL index is inherited downward and "identified with the index of one of the NPs" inside.
- Why it matters: with affixes as syntactic heads, leaves are smaller than tokens. With pragmatic co-indexation the MRS from replay lacks the link between noun and clause. With downward REL inheritance to "one of the NPs", the derivation tree does not determine which NP is the semantic head, so replay needs extra information or must choose.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. Kim and Yang (2003), cited for the Korean constructional analysis, has an LKB implementation in its title; the chapter does not describe it.

### 14. Pied-piping constraints are not settled, so a strict grammar will be wrong in one direction

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (strict output failing on grammatical input)
- Reference: Section 2.1.1, pp. 601-605 (examples (12)-(18), footnote 11)
- What the chapter says: Sag (1997: 455) requires the preposed phrase to be headed by a noun or a preposition, which the attested AP, AdvP, and non-finite VP fillers ("compared to whom ...", "faster than which ...", "to be admitted to one of which ...") show to be "over-restrictive". The Clausal REL Prohibition of Pollard and Sag (1994: 220) allows some examples "that should be excluded". The restrictions "are poorly understood".
- Why it matters: a grammar that implements the stricter constraint rejects rare but attested sentences, which then fall to the relaxed output. This is a coverage issue, not an architectural one.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- The dominant analysis has no empty elements. Sag (1997) "is constructional and completely dispenses with phonologically empty elements" (Section 2.3, p. 609), and this approach "has dominated work in HPSG in the last two decades" (p. 596). The reservations about the empty relativizer (a novel part of speech, no independent evidence) are what led Sag to it (p. 609).
- The structures are unary and binary and continuous. A wh-relative is a binary head-filler phrase (Figure 5, p. 612); a bare relative is a unary construction over a slashed clause (constraint (55), Figure 10, pp. 630-631); the relative clause combines with its noun in a binary head-adjunct structure (Figure 6, p. 614). In Section 2 and Section 3, only the preposed supplemental relatives of Lee-Goldman (p. 636) use discontinuity.
- Unbounded dependencies are local feature passing. The filler-gap dependency and pied-piping are handled by inheriting SLASH and REL between mother and daughters (Section 2.1.1, pp. 598-600). This is what a replay engine does once the tree is given; no search is involved.
- Unexpressed subjects are not daughters. PRO is "a pronominal that is not syntactically expressed as a syntactic daughter"; it is an element of the SUBJ list (Section 3.1, p. 617). Non-finite relatives therefore add no tokenless leaves.
- The semantic link between antecedent and gap comes out of unification. The index of the antecedent reaches the relative pronoun through MOD and REL, and the gap through SLASH (Figure 1, p. 601; Figures 5-6). The role of the head noun inside the relative clause is therefore present in the feature structure after replay, which is what both the MRS and an enhanced-dependency style UD conversion need.
- Non-derivational, single-level analysis. The raising and matching analyses are rejected because they are "fundamentally derivational in nature, presupposing at least two levels of syntactic structure" (Section 2.4, pp. 615-616). The adjunction-plus-co-indexation analysis is shared by all HPSG variants.
- Fully lexical analyses exist for complementizer relatives. The Arabic and French "dont" analyses use ordinary head-complement structures and a lexical entry that binds SLASH itself; "no constructional apparatus is used at all" (Section 3.2.1, p. 620; Section 3.2.3, p. 623). A complementizer analysis of English "that" would work the same way (p. 622).
- Reduced relatives can be purely lexical: participial, PP, and AP postmodifiers "involve neither relative pronouns nor genuine gaps", so no construction and no empty element is needed (Section 3.3.2, p. 633).
- Lexical rules are the accepted carrier of meaning changes that inheritance cannot express. Footnote 44 (p. 631): Müller (2006, 2007, 2010) argues that embedding content under a modal operator "cannot be handled by inheritance mechanisms", "so that a lexical rule approach is required". This matches labels that carry lexical rule chains.
- The EXTRA analysis of extraposition is continuous and uses "only the more commonplace apparatus of nonlocal features", with a lexical rule or "a non-branching construction" at the bottom (Section 4.1, pp. 638-639). Several authors argue it is the superior analysis (Crysmann 2013; Borsley and Crysmann 2021).
- MRS is named as a semantic framework that makes the more flexible analyses work: NP-level attachment of relatives (footnote 22, p. 611, with Chaves 2007 giving an MRS analysis of coordination and relative clauses) and Kiss's (2005) analysis of extraposition (p. 638). RESTR values are treated as multisets following Copestake et al. (2005) (footnote 26, p. 613).
- Non-restrictive relatives are syntactically integrated (Arnold 2004, 2007; Arnold and Borsley 2008): they are ordinary parts of one tree, and the claim that they need antecedents outside syntax is argued to be incorrect (Section 3.4, p. 635).
- Simplification is possible: the special head-relative-phrase "is not strictly necessary" (p. 614), and Sag (2010) no longer distinguishes subject from non-subject finite relatives (footnote 20, p. 610). Both reduce the rule inventory.
- Cross-classified construction types (Figure 4, p. 610) resolve to a small set of maximal types, each of which can serve as one rule name in a derivation tree.

## Theory strains

1. Empty relativizer versus constructions (Sections 2.2-2.4, pp. 607-615). The lexical analysis needs "a novel part of speech (R)" and empty elements "for which there is no independent evidence"; the constructional analysis needs three constructions and a type hierarchy. The chapter adds that a lexical account of bare relatives without either device is impossible (p. 632).
2. Status of REL (footnotes 2 and 6, pp. 599-600). Van Eynde (2004) argues pied-piping is a local dependency, against the nonlocal REL feature. REL is a set of indices in Pollard and Sag (1994) and Sag (1997), a set of parameters in Ginzburg and Sag (2000). REL is discharged at RP in one analysis and required to be empty on all clauses in the other (footnote 24, p. 612).
3. Constraints on pied-piping (Section 2.1.1, pp. 601-605). The data are "confusing and contradictory"; restricting the preposed phrase (Sag 1997) undergenerates, restricting the inheritance path (Clausal REL Prohibition) overgenerates, and "it is quite possible that both approaches are necessary". REL and QUE obey different restrictions (18), and there is "no morphological or syntactic unity underlying the concept of an English wh-expression" (Sag 2010: 493).
4. The semantic type mismatch (Sections 2.1.2 and 2.3, pp. 606-615). Three places to convert propositional content into a nominal restriction (empty head, head-relative-phrase, the relative clause construction). The authors concede that neither argument for the separate construction is "compelling". Sag's disjoint union for RESTR "does not work as a general operation" (footnote 26). Head-adjunct phrases end up with two different semantic principles (footnote 27).
5. N versus NP attachment (footnote 22, p. 611; Section 3.2.2, p. 621). Sag attaches wh-relatives to NP to derive ordering facts; this forces "that" relatives to be wh-relatives because of coordination, and requires a different semantics. Hydras support NP attachment.
6. The category of "that" (Section 3.2.2, pp. 620-622). Marker, relative pronoun, or complementizer; Pollard and Sag split it across two analyses (footnote 35), which the authors call less appealing than a uniform treatment. The nominative-case explanation for the lack of pied-piping is "not very convincing", since a nominative filler should not match a non-nominative gap; the same problem for "who" is left open. The complementizer analysis raises unanswered questions about the type hierarchy. Arnold (2004) wrongly predicts supplemental "that" relatives unless "that" is reanalyzed (footnote 51).
7. Subject relatives (footnotes 18 and 20, pp. 609-610). Pollard and Sag need the Subject Extraction Lexical Rule because of the Trace Principle; Sag (1997) keeps the distinction, Sag (2010) drops it, and "it is not clear how important this is". Related: "to" as complementizer (Sag 1997) or auxiliary (footnote 30, p. 617).
8. Stipulated restrictions (Section 3.1, pp. 617-618). Non-finite wh-relatives require a PP filler, an "apparently idiosyncratic restriction" stated directly in the construction (34b).
9. Bare relatives across languages (Section 3.3, pp. 627-632). Japanese: a SLASH-less affix-based analysis plus pragmatics for the nonlocal cases. Korean: a slashed S and a construction that empties SLASH on the mother. English: a slashed VP and a unary construction. Where SLASH is terminated differs in each. The modal meaning of infinitival relatives is addressed by none of the analyses (footnote 44).
10. Reduced relatives (Section 3.3.2, pp. 632-633). Clausal construction with PRO (Sag 1997) or lexical modifier; "it is not obvious to us what is gained" by the former, and whether the difference has empirical consequences is "not clear".
11. Non-restrictive relatives (Section 3.4, pp. 633-636). Empty relativizer with discourse antecedent (Holler 2003), uniform syntax with two semantic modes (Arnold 2004), multidimensional content (Lee-Goldman 2012); integration versus conceptual-structure accounts (Blakemore 2006). Auxiliary-stranding relatives have three candidate analyses.
12. Extraposition (Section 4.1, pp. 637-639). Domains, EXTRA, or generalized modification through ANCHORS; "little to choose between these alternatives" in theoretical cost, with the deciding factor possibly being integration with other extraposition.
13. It-clefts (Section 4.2.1, pp. 640-641). The standard entry for "be" does not cover clefts with a relative phrase filler; no solution is offered.
14. Free relatives (Section 4.2.3, pp. 643-646). "The central analytic problem" is that the wh-phrase behaves as head and as filler; German mismatching free relatives rule out every analysis that takes material inside the clause as head, including "certain HPSG analyses" and the Minimalist accounts of Donati, Ott, and Chomsky. Welsh differs from English in which element determines the reference (footnote 65).
15. Pseudo-clefts and transparent free relatives (Section 4.2.4, pp. 646-647). Interrogative or free relative; connectivity effects; outside HPSG, "grafts" (van Riemsdijk 2006).
16. Internally headed and pseudo-relatives (Section 4.2.2, pp. 641-643). Korean "kes" clauses as complements versus relatives; pseudo-relatives as "a special construction"; an exocentric NP for Dogon whose summary "ignores a number of technical and empirical issues" about REL.
17. French "dont" (Section 3.2.3, pp. 622-626). A lexical rule that turns a resumptive dependency into a gap dependency partway up the tree, with distinct LOCAL subtypes (prl, nprl) for pronouns and gaps and an additional complication for coordination. Arabic: lexical (Alqurashi and Borsley 2012) versus constructional (Hahn 2012).
18. Defaults (footnotes 25 and 33). Nonlocal feature propagation is stated with default constraints that are "overridden" where needed, a device outside the monotonic core of the formalism.
19. No natural kind (Section 5, p. 648). "There is nothing one can say that will be true of everything that has been described as a 'relative clause'"; some are head-filler, some head-complement, some have REL, some lack even SLASH. The authors present this as a virtue; for a revised theory it means the construction-specific types do not reduce to a common schema.
20. Comparison with other frameworks. Raising and matching analyses are derivational and face empirical problems with case and with treating wh-words as determiners (Section 2.4, footnote 28, pp. 615-616; Webelhuth et al. 2019). Categorial Grammar treats the relative word as functor and has difficulty with pied-piping (footnote 3, p. 599; Pollard 1988).

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter does not say how the ERG or any other implemented grammar analyzes relative clauses, bare relatives, "that", free relatives, or extraposition. Which of the options in caveats 1-3 and 8-10 the ERG takes has to be checked in the grammar itself.
- The chapter never states whether the gap at the bottom of a SLASH dependency is a trace or is introduced without one; Figure 1 shows a slashed V with no trace daughter, and footnote 18 mentions the Trace Principle of Pollard and Sag (1994) only in passing. This is deferred to Chapter 13 and is decisive for the no-empty-elements assumption.
- UD is not mentioned. All statements above about the UD bridge are my reading of how the described analyses would interact with a dependency conversion (for example, the treatment of "that", the head of a free relative, the attachment of extraposed and non-restrictive relatives).
- The statement in caveat 3 that Kiss-style modification leaves the choice of modified nominal open to the replay engine is my inference from the chapter's wording; Kiss (2005) and Crysmann (2004) need to be read to see how the choice is constrained or underspecified.
- Relative-correlative constructions (Hindi, Marathi) and amount relatives are mentioned only in footnotes 53 and 55 as omissions.
- The chapter does not discuss ambiguity of relative clause attachment (which of several preceding nouns is modified), which is a practical issue for the tagger.
- Punctuation and intonation as cues for the restrictive/non-restrictive distinction are mentioned only as "set off intonationally"; how a grammar that sees commas as tokens or as punctuation marks on words treats them is not addressed.
- Nothing is said about processing cost of set-valued nonlocal features or of default constraints.
