# Chapter 25: Computational linguistics and grammar engineering (Emily M. Bender and Guy Emerson)

Handbook pages 1105-1153 (text pp. 1105-1130, then references). Section and page references below are to the handbook pagination.

## Summary

The chapter surveys computational work in HPSG: why the theory suits implementation (stable formalism separate from theory, interest in "peripheral" phenomena, type hierarchy), how parsing stays tractable although the formalism is Turing-complete, how ambiguity is handled by statistical parse ranking and supertagging, and how MRS is reduced to dependency graphs (EDS, DMRS, DM). It then describes the main grammar projects (CoreGram on TRALE, the DELPH-IN Consortium with the ERG, LKB, PET, ACE and the Grammar Matrix, and Alpino, Enju and Babel), their uses in hypothesis testing, treebanking (Redwoods, WikiWoods, Alpino/Lassy and its UD mapping) and downstream applications, and closes with linguistic insights from grammar engineering (scale of ambiguity, long-tail phenomena, analysis-order effects). This chapter bears directly on the parser design. Most of what it says supports the design for DELPH-IN grammars; the caveats concern tokenization and multiword expressions, ambiguity, coverage, and the fact that HPSG implementations outside DELPH-IN (TRALE/CoreGram, Babel) use devices the design excludes.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Leaves are not always single tokens: multiword lexical entries and external preprocessing

- Severity: high
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens (and tree-encoding)
- Reference: Section 2.2.2, p. 1112; Section 3.2, pp. 1117-1118
- What the chapter says: for multiword expressions such as *by and large*, "the grammar assigns a single lexical entry to multiple tokens". Dridan (2013) therefore extends supertagging to "ubertagging", which "jointly predicts both a segmentation of the input and supertags for those segments". Real-world text further requires unknown-word handling (Adolphs et al. 2008), handling of extra-grammatical markup (Flickinger et al. 2010), and integration of external morphological analyzers and named entity recognizers (p. 1118).
- Why it matters: a tagger that assigns one label per input token has to reproduce the grammar's own segmentation, which is not the input tokenization. The label scheme needs a way to say "this token is part of a multi-token leaf", and the UD bridge has to align UD words with grammar leaves.
- DELPH-IN stance: the problem is solved inside DELPH-IN by ubertagging (Dridan 2013), which made parsing four times faster and more accurate (p. 1112). The chapter gives no detail on the token-mapping machinery itself.

### 2. Scale of ambiguity; disambiguation is outside the grammar

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (and tree-encoding)
- Reference: Section 2.2.2, pp. 1110-1112; Section 5.1, pp. 1128-1129
- What the chapter says: the 2018 ERG gives 21 readings for a five-word sentence and 35,094 for a newspaper sentence (p. 1110). Ranking "is not usually determined by the grammar itself"; a statistical model is trained on a treebank (p. 1111). In Redwoods roughly half the ambiguity is lexical and half syntactic, and resolving it needs several kinds of information combined, semantic as well as syntactic (p. 1129). A supertagger "will predict the wrong lexical entry" some of the time (p. 1111).
- Why it matters: in the planned design the tagger alone carries the whole disambiguation burden; lexical-type labels resolve only about half of it, so the tree-structure bits must resolve the rest. In a chart parser a wrong supertag narrows the search; under search-free replay a wrong label gives a failed replay or a licensed but unintended reading. (The second sentence is my inference from the chapter's figures.)
- DELPH-IN stance: supertagging and ubertagging prune the search space before chart parsing, and a separate ranking model picks among the remaining parses (Toutanova et al. 2002, 2005; Matsuzaki et al. 2007; Dridan 2013). Hand-written rule weights were tried and were "extremely brittle" and "next to impossible to maintain" (footnote 11, p. 1111).

### 3. Coverage gap: the grammar does not analyze every sentence, and grammar-based treebanks contain only analyzed sentences

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (strict output); other (training data)
- Reference: Section 4.1.4, p. 1124; Section 4.2.2, p. 1126; Section 4.2.3, p. 1127
- What the chapter says: "only items for which the grammar finds a reasonable parse can be included in the treebank" (p. 1124). Systems that use ERG output have high precision and low recall, one reason being "that the grammar does not cover all sentences in natural text" (p. 1126; also Flickinger et al. 2012, p. 1127).
- Why it matters: for uncovered sentences no label sequence replays successfully, so the relaxed output is needed for a real share of text. The training data for the tagger (Redwoods-style treebanks) contains no examples of uncovered sentences, so the tagger is trained on a biased sample.
- DELPH-IN stance: robust processing returns analyses for sentences the grammar rejects (Zhang & Krieger 2011; Buys & Blunsom 2017; Chen et al. 2018; pp. 1117-1118, 1127). Treebanks also allow one-off lexical entries for misspellings in the Alpino workflow (p. 1123).

### 4. Empty elements, relational constraints and cyclic structures in non-DELPH-IN grammars

- Severity: medium (low for the ERG; matters for the multilingual goal)
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements (and deterministic-replay)
- Reference: Section 3.1, p. 1115; Section 2.2.1, p. 1109; Section 3.2, p. 1117
- What the chapter says: CoreGram grammars (German, Danish, Persian, Maltese, Mandarin; smaller ones for English, Yiddish, Spanish, French, Hindi) are implemented in TRALE, "which accommodates a wide range of technical devices proposed in the literature, including phonologically empty elements, relational constraints, implications with complex antecedents, and cyclic feature structures", plus macros and an expressive morphological component. Melnik (2007) observes that this lets grammar engineers implement a wider range of theoretical proposals directly than the LKB does.
- Why it matters: a replay engine built for the DELPH-IN formalism cannot run CoreGram grammars, and derivations with empty leaves have no token to carry a label.
- DELPH-IN stance: the boundedness argument for DELPH-IN grammars assumes "a system without relational constraints" (p. 1109). The chapter does not state outright that DELPH-IN grammars have no empty elements; it only contrasts TRALE with the LKB.

### 5. Discontinuous constituents

- Severity: medium (for languages beyond English)
- Assumption affected: continuity
- Reference: Section 3.3.3, pp. 1119-1120; Section 2.1, p. 1106
- What the chapter says: Babel, a broad-coverage grammar of German, "makes extensive use of discontinuous constituents" (Müller 2004a). Worst-case parsing complexity is much worse, but "parsing speed doesn't seem to suffer in practice". The shuffle operator (Reape 1994) is named as a formal device whose necessity is argued for and against (p. 1106).
- Why it matters: a derivation with discontinuous constituents is not a concatenative tree over the token sequence, so the tree encoding does not apply as designed.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed for DELPH-IN. The chapter describes the standard chart algorithm for grammars with "a context-free backbone" (pp. 1108-1109).

### 6. Unbounded structures: recursive unary rules, valence and SLASH lists

- Severity: low (for the ERG); medium for some other languages
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding; deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 2.2.1, pp. 1108-1109, footnotes 8-9
- What the chapter says: the formalism is Turing-complete (Johnson 1988). "In principle, a grammar may allow an infinite number of possible feature structures, for example if it includes recursive unary rules." In DELPH-IN grammars the number is bounded: only valence lists constrain grammaticality, and they are never longer than four or five; in many DELPH-IN grammars, including the ERG, SLASH has at most one element. Unbounded valence or SLASH lists may be required for cross-serial dependencies (Rentier 1994), in which case the bound depends on sentence length (footnote 9).
- Why it matters: a label that encodes the chain of unary and lexical rules has a closed inventory only if unary chains are bounded in practice. (This link to the label inventory is my inference; the chapter speaks only of the number of feature structures.) The one-element SLASH limit means some multiple-extraction analyses are not available in the ERG.
- DELPH-IN stance: bounded by grammar design, in part because DELPH-IN "does not adopt proposals like the DEPS list of Bouma, Malouf & Sag (2001)" (footnote 9).

### 7. MRS contains material that is not tied to tokens; the dependency reductions lose information

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics (and UD-bridge)
- Reference: Section 2.2.3, pp. 1112-1114
- What the chapter says: the MRS for *The cherry tree blossomed* contains abstract predicates with no lexical item (`udef_q`, `compound`). EDS discards some scope information; DMRS is fully interconvertible with MRS only under conditions on intrinsic arguments (footnote 15); DM expresses predicate-argument structure "purely in terms of the surface tokens", dropping some abstract predicates, turning others into edges, and dropping some scope information. The example MRS in the chapter omits ICONS and difference lists "for simplicity".
- Why it matters: MRS as a by-product of replay is fine, but any evaluation or UD comparison that goes through token-level graphs (DM) is lossy, and semantic dependencies are not syntactic dependencies (footnote 14).
- DELPH-IN stance: all four representations are DELPH-IN standards with software support (PyDelphin and others, p. 1114).

### 8. The UD bridge has only partial precedent, and treebank conversion needs construction-specific rewriting

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 3.3.1, pp. 1118-1119; Section 3.3.2, p. 1119; Section 4.1.4, p. 1123
- What the chapter says: the only HPSG-to-UD mapping mentioned is for Alpino (Bouma & van Noord 2017), and it starts from Alpino's syntactic dependency graphs, which are built directly in the feature structures (each lexical entry encodes a partial dependency graph), not from derivation trees. In the other direction, the nearest case is Enju: Penn Treebank trees were binarized, head information was percolated, and analyses of subject-control verbs, auxiliary verbs, coordination and extracted arguments had to be converted before lexical entries could be induced with a small set of hand-written schemata (p. 1119).
- Why it matters: the chapter offers no precedent for converting ERG derivations to UD or UD trees to ERG-style derivations. The Enju experience indicates which constructions need special treatment in a treebank-to-HPSG conversion.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed for DELPH-IN grammars.

### 9. Derivations are tied to a grammar version

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding; other (training data maintenance)
- Reference: Section 2.2.2, p. 1112; Section 4.1.4, p. 1124; Section 5.3, pp. 1129-1130
- What the chapter says: when the grammar is updated, the corpus is reparsed and the stored discriminant decisions are replayed, "leaving only a small amount of further annotation work"; Redwoods and WikiWoods are updated with each ERG release.
- Why it matters: rule names, lexical types and therefore the tagger's label inventory change with each grammar release; the tagger has to be retrained on the matching treebank release.
- DELPH-IN stance: discriminant-based treebanking makes annotation reusable across grammar versions (Oepen et al. 2004; Flickinger et al. 2017).

### 10. Mal-rules: a successful derivation does not always mean a grammatical sentence

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (meaning of "strict")
- Reference: Section 4.2.1, pp. 1124-1125
- What the chapter says: a mal-rule "is like a normal rule, in that it licenses a construction, and can be treated the same during parsing"; its presence in a parse signals an error to report (Bender et al. 2004; Flickinger & Yu 2013).
- Why it matters: if mal-rules are loaded, "replay succeeded" has to be qualified by which rules were used. The device is also an existing grammar-internal form of relaxation.
- DELPH-IN stance: used with the ERG in a large educational system (Suppes et al. 2014).

## Support for the design

- Context-free backbone. The standard chart algorithm applies to grammars where "every analysis can be expressed as a phrase-structure tree plus constraints between mother and daughter nodes" (pp. 1108-1109). This is the property that tree encoding plus replay relies on.
- Bounded feature structures in DELPH-IN grammars: no relational constraints assumed, short valence lists, SLASH of at most one element in the ERG, no DEPS (p. 1109 and footnote 9).
- Supertagging is established practice for HPSG (Matsuzaki et al. 2007) because HPSG is lexicalized: "choosing the correct lexical entry for each token brings in rich information that can be exploited to rule out many possible parses" (p. 1111). Ubertagging adds segmentation and gave a fourfold speedup with better accuracy (p. 1112). The planned tagger extends this line from lexical entries to whole derivations.
- Standardized formalism and file formats let several engines (LKB, PET, ACE, Agree) process the same grammars (p. 1117). A new replay engine targeting the same formalism (Copestake 2002a) fits this ecosystem.
- Training data exists: Redwoods stores "the final full HPSG analysis that was selected" (p. 1124); WikiWoods has 55 million automatically parsed and ranked sentences, less reliable but large (p. 1124).
- Neural or statistical systems trained on grammar output already match grammar-based parsers on semantic graphs: Buys & Blunsom (2017) nearly as accurate, full coverage, 70 times faster; Chen et al. (2018) slightly more accurate, and they gain by using the phrase-structure (derivation) trees, which the chapter says lets the system "learn compositional rules mirroring composition in the grammar" (p. 1127). This supports predicting derivations and not only graphs.
- A strict/relaxed split has precedent in DELPH-IN robust processing (Zhang & Krieger 2011, a PCFG approximation of an HPSG; p. 1117).
- Precision: DELPH-IN grammars "model grammaticality", have lower ambiguity than treebank-trained grammars, and are bidirectional (pp. 1107, 1117). A successful replay is therefore meaningful evidence of well-formedness.
- Multilingual goal and coarse universal schemas: the Grammar Matrix core and libraries (pp. 1116, 1121), CoreGram's shared constraint sets (pp. 1115, 1120), and Enju's "small set of hand-written HPSG schemata" (p. 1119) are existing inventories of cross-linguistic or coarse schemas. AGGREGATION derives Matrix grammar specifications from interlinear glossed text (p. 1122).
- Inspectable grammars: each constraint stated once on one type makes types a target for documentation and exploration (p. 1107).
- UD: an HPSG-inspired grammar's output has been mapped to UD to build a UD treebank (Bouma & van Noord 2017; pp. 1119, 1123).

## Theory strains

- Expressive formalism versus restricted formalism (Section 2.2.1, p. 1108). The HPSG formalism is Turing-complete; CCG and TAG build complexity limits into the formalism as a claim about human language. Müller (2015: 64) argues for analyses first, efficiency second. The chapter presents these as different schools of thought.
- Disputed formal devices and the gap between "paper" HPSG and DELPH-IN (pp. 1106, 1109, 1115, 1117). The necessity of the shuffle operator (Reape 1994) is argued both ways. TRALE supports empty elements, relational constraints, complex-antecedent implications and cyclic structures; the LKB does not directly support this range (Melnik 2007). DELPH-IN does not adopt the DEPS list of Bouma, Malouf & Sag (2001) and often limits SLASH to one element. The theory as published and the theory as implemented at scale are therefore not the same object.
- Feature structures versus descriptions (footnote 4, p. 1107). Pollard & Sag (1994) distinguish fully resolved feature structures from underspecified descriptions; computational work operates entirely with partial feature structures and drops the distinction.
- Analyses chosen for parser efficiency (Section 2.2.1, pp. 1109-1110). A grammar writer facing several analyses "may opt for the analysis that is more efficient for a particular parsing algorithm" (Flickinger 2000). Some ERG analyses are engineering choices, which is relevant when reading the ERG as theory.
- Analysis-order effects (Section 5.3, pp. 1129-1130). The form of an implemented grammar is partly an artifact of the order in which phenomena were implemented (Fokkens 2014); the chapter says this is probably true of non-computational theory too. Fokkens's CLIMB keeps competing analyses alive in a meta-grammar.
- Disambiguation lies outside the grammar (Section 2.2.2, pp. 1110-1111; Section 4.1.4, p. 1124). The grammar licenses tens of thousands of readings and has nothing to say about preference; hand-written weights failed. Treebanking also exposes "spurious ambiguity".
- What is universal (Sections 3.1, 4.1.1, 4.1.2; pp. 1115, 1120-1121). CoreGram ("bottom-up with cheating") and the Grammar Matrix (core plus libraries, independent copies) are two different methods for stating cross-linguistic constraints, and both treat the core as a revisable hypothesis; the Matrix core is often refined when new languages are added. CoreGram's general constraints allow V2 but rule out verb-penultimate order.
- Long tail (Section 5.2, p. 1129). The ERG has over 60 raising and control types, "many of which are not otherwise discussed in the syntactic literature" (for example *incumbent*). Theory based on core cases underdescribes the lexicon.
- Cross-serial dependencies (footnote 9, p. 1109; Section 3.3.1, p. 1118) may need unbounded valence or SLASH lists (Rentier 1994; Bouma & van Noord 1998), in tension with the bounded lists of DELPH-IN grammars.
- Semantic representation (Section 2.2.3, pp. 1112-1114; Section 3.2, p. 1118). Several competing reductions of MRS exist, each dropping different information; the chapter notes that external application requirements shape the form of semantic analyses.

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter never states that DELPH-IN grammars use only unary and binary rules, nor that they have no empty elements; it gives the complexity bound for a general maximum rule arity and contrasts TRALE's devices with the LKB. The design assumptions on these two points need another source (for example Copestake 2002b or the ERG itself).
- Nothing on the DELPH-IN token-mapping and lexical-filtering machinery, on how derivation trees are stored, or on whether a derivation tree alone determines the feature structure and MRS. The claim "the derivation is a complete, deterministic recipe" is not addressed.
- Nothing on lexical rules versus inflectional rules in derivations, orthographic rules, or punctuation handling, all of which affect what the per-token label must encode.
- No published account of ERG-derivation-to-UD or UD-to-ERG conversion is cited.
- Parsing-as-tagging approaches to full constituency or derivation trees postdate or lie outside the chapter; the only tagging discussed is lexical (supertagging, ubertagging).
- The chapter does not discuss Sag's Sign-Based Construction Grammar, Ginzburg & Sag 2000, or Kathol's linearization work; of the "paper" HPSG literature it touches only Pollard & Sag 1994, Reape 1994 and Bouma, Malouf & Sag 2001, each in one sentence or footnote.
