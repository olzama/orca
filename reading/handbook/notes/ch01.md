# Chapter 1: Basic properties and elements (Anne Abeillé and Robert D. Borsley)

Handbook pages 3-45 (text ends on p. 35; references follow). Page numbers below are handbook page numbers. "DELPH-IN stance" reports only what this chapter says; the chapter says very little about implemented grammars (one sentence on p. 7 and footnote 3 on p. 5).

## Summary

The chapter introduces HPSG as a declarative, constraint-based ("model-theoretic"), monostratal grammar in which each expression has one relatively simple constituent structure, branching is not required to be binary, and phonologically empty elements are used sparingly. It presents the building blocks (types, features, implicational constraints, structure sharing), the organization of the lexicon (cross-classified lexeme hierarchy, ARG-ST versus SUBJ/COMPS, the Argument Realisation Principle, lexical rules), and the phrase-type hierarchy of Constructional HPSG (head-complement, head-subject, head-filler, head-adjunct, head-specifier, head-marker phrases) with the Head Feature Principle, the Valence Principle and the default Generalised Head Feature Principle. Constituent order is treated as a relation between PHON values of mother and daughters, with order domains (Section 7.1) as an alternative in which word order is decoupled from constituent structure. The chapter closes with a short comparison to Sign-Based Construction Grammar. It distinguishes three versions of the framework (early HPSG of Pollard and Sag 1987/1994, Constructional HPSG of Sag 1997 and Ginzburg and Sag 2000, and SBCG of Sag 2012) and concentrates on the second.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Order domains: word order decoupled from constituent structure

- Severity: high
- Assumption affected: continuity
- Reference: Section 7.1, pp. 32-34; also footnote 35, p. 27; shuffle operator in footnote 21, p. 17 and footnote 36, p. 28
- What the chapter says: "Much work in HPSG has proposed that the observed order is a reflection not of the constituent structure of an expression but of a separate system of order domains" (Reape 1994; Müller 1996; Kathol 2000). Ordering rules may then order non-sister elements. Example (46): *A man came into the room who looked like Churchill* has two daughters (`a man who looked like Churchill`, `came into the room`) but three domain elements, so the NP daughter is discontinuous in the string. Kathol (2000: 81) analyzes German interrogatives this way. Domains are called "most plausible" for nonconfigurational languages such as Warlpiri (Donohue and Sag 1999), but "may well have a role to play in more familiar languages" (Bonami et al. 1999; Chaves 2014). "Free" word order can be stated by leaving the PHON concatenation unspecified via shuffle (p. 28).
- Consequence for the design: under such analyses the derivation tree's yield is not the token string, so a tag sequence that decodes to a continuous tree cannot represent the analysis. For English the chapter gives an alternative with continuous structures (extraposition as a different structure, Kiss 2005), so the threat is mainly to the multi-language goal.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter cites Müller (1996), an implemented German fragment, among the order-domain works, but says nothing about how any implementation handles domains.

### 2. Branching is not limited to binary

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules
- Reference: Section 2, p. 5; Section 5.1, pp. 22-23 (constraint (32), Figure 6) and footnote 30, p. 23; Section 5.2, p. 29 and footnote 38
- What the chapter says: "Unlike Minimalism, HPSG does not assume that all branching is binary." The head-complement constraint (32) takes a word head and all its complements as sisters (*give some money to charity* is ternary in Figure 6). Subject-auxiliary inversion is "standardly seen as ternary branching" with subject and complement both sisters of the auxiliary (Pollard and Sag 1994: 40). Footnote 30: "binary branching has been assumed in HPSG grammars for a number of languages" (pointer to Müller 2021b, Chapter 10).
- Consequence for the design: the textbook analyses are flat; a binary-plus-unary tree encoding corresponds to a particular family of grammars, and gold analyses from the theoretical literature will not map one to one onto its trees. The UD bridge is also affected, since head-complement attachment order inside a binarized VP is a grammar-internal choice.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed by name; only the general remark in footnote 30 that grammars for a number of languages assume binary branching.

### 3. Empty elements are restricted, not excluded

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 5-7, including footnote 6, p. 7
- What the chapter says: HPSG "makes limited use of phonologically empty elements"; they "are only assumed if there is compelling evidence for them". Much work assumes no empty element in unbounded dependencies, but "There is, however, some debate here" (Sag and Fodor 1995; Müller 2004; Borsley and Crysmann 2021). Footnote 6: Borsley (2009) argues that Welsh has phonologically empty pronouns; general discussion in Müller (2016: Chapter 19.2).
- Consequence for the design: the no-empty-elements assumption matches the majority position described here but is not a theorem of the framework; some analyses (traces, Welsh empty pronouns) have leaves without tokens.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Arguments with no constituent: gaps, null subjects, pronominal affixes

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge (also deterministic-replay and leaves-equal-tokens)
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 16-17 (lexical items (17), (19); ARP (20); footnotes 21-22)
- What the chapter says: null subjects are on ARG-ST but not on SUBJ (Manning and Sag 1999); extracted objects are on ARG-ST but not on COMPS; arguments "realised as pronominal affixes (traditionally known as clitics in Romance languages) are absent from COMPS lists" (Miller and Sag 1997; Monachesi 2005). The Argument Realisation Principle (20) is a constraint on `word` that removes non-canonical arguments from COMPS. "There are complex issues here, and the principle will probably take a different form in different languages."
- Consequence for the design: (a) in this formulation the choice between a canonical and a gapped or affixal realization is made inside the word by a constraint, with no rule node in the tree; the tag for the token must carry that choice (as a lexical type or a unary rule) for replay to be deterministic. (b) Romance pronominal clitics are affixes on this analysis, so they are part of the verb leaf, not leaves of their own. (c) The semantic argument exists without a syntactic dependent, so the dependency has to be recovered from the filler or from the semantics when converting to dependencies.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Default (non-monotonic) principles

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 5.1, pp. 23-24 (Valence Principle (33); Generalised Head Feature Principle (34))
- What the chapter says: the Valence Principle is stated as "Unless some constraint says otherwise, the mother's values for the valence features are identical to those of the head daughter" (cf. Sag and Wasow 1999: 86). Ginzburg and Sag (2000: 33) replace it and the Head Feature Principle with the GHFP, a default constraint in the sense of Lascarides and Copestake (1999): mother and head daughter share SYNSEM "unless some other constraint requires something different".
- Consequence for the design: a replay engine that only does monotonic unification cannot run Constructional HPSG as written; defaults must be compiled out into each rule beforehand.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Sets, list difference, shuffle and other relational constraints

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 3, p. 13; Section 4.1, pp. 15-17 (append in (14), contained list difference and shuffle in (20) and footnote 21); Section 5.1, p. 24 (SLASH as a set, set union in (36), footnote 32)
- What the chapter says: features may have sets as values; SLASH is a set of local objects and the head-filler constraint uses set union; the ARP uses list concatenation and a "contained list difference" relation interdefinable with the sequence union (shuffle) operator of Reape (1994) and Kathol (1995). Footnote 32: the mother of a head-filler phrase need not have empty SLASH, allowing multiple extraction (*Paul, who could we talk to about?*).
- Consequence for the design: these relations are not plain unification and can be nondeterministic (shuffle, set membership). An engine with "no search" needs list-only encodings of them.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Constraints that are not attached to a named rule

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 4-5; Section 3, pp. 8, 12-14 (constraint schema (10), footnote 12)
- What the chapter says: the grammar is a set of constraints that every object must satisfy; "There are no procedures constructing representations". Linguistic objects are fully specified; only descriptions are underspecified. The antecedent of a constraint may be a feature description, not only a type, which is "necessary, for example, in the constraints that constitute Binding Theory". Footnote 12: AVMs "used to be combined by unification" (Pollard and Sag 1987), with a pointer to Richter (Chapter 3) on the term.
- Consequence for the design: in the theory, a tree of rule names is not automatically a complete recipe; constraints with complex antecedents (Binding Theory) apply to any object matching a description and are outside the rule inventory. Replay "by unification in the order the tree gives" is an implementation view that the chapter treats as a matter of performance.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed (the chapter notes only that implementations exist, p. 7).

### 8. Headedness is not uniform: unheaded phrases, syntactic versus semantic head, functors

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 3, footnote 9, p. 9; Section 5.1, p. 21 and footnote 34, p. 27; Section 6.1, p. 30; Section 6.2, pp. 31-32
- What the chapter says: not all phrases are headed (coordination is "the most important type of non-headed phrase"); the head daughter may itself be a phrase, including an unheaded one. In head-adjunct phrases "the adjunct is not a syntactic head, but may well be the semantic head". Determiners are specifiers in some work and markers/functors selecting the head via SELECT in other work; complementizers are markers in some work and heads in other work ("There is no consensus here"). Adverbial PPs are adjuncts in some work and optional complements in other work.
- Consequence for the design: the head choice needed for dependency conversion is analysis-dependent for determiners, complementizers and coordination, and the adjunct/complement status of adverbial PPs is not fixed by the theory. Conversion rules must be stated per grammar and per rule, not per "HPSG".
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Semantics is not uniformly MRS

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 3, p. 13; Section 4.1, p. 19; Section 5.1, pp. 26-27
- What the chapter says: content has been modeled with Situation Semantics, MRS (Copestake et al. 2005), Lexical Resource Semantics (Richter and Sailer 2004), Montague-style semantics (Sag 2010) and Frame Semantics (SBCG). The MRS composition principles are given only "Leaving aside quantification and other complex matters".
- Consequence for the design: MRS as a by-product is tied to grammars built on MRS; analyses from the wider literature may assume another semantics. Lexical rules such as extraposition and passive leave CONTENT unchanged, so syntactically different derivations "may have the same semantic representation" (p. 21), which matters when evaluating on semantics.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed beyond citing MRS.

### 10. Language-specific grammars; even basic constraints do not transfer

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: other (relaxed fallback from universal schemas; multi-language goal)
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 7-8; Section 3, footnote 17, p. 14
- What the chapter says: HPSG rejects assuming an element or property in one language because other languages have it; "Grammars should be motivated on a language-specific basis" (Müller 2015: 25). Footnote 17: the constraint `phrase => [COMPS <>]` "is plausible for English, but it is too strong for some languages", in particular with complex predicates or partial VPs and for SOV languages analyzed with binary branching.
- Consequence for the design: a set of "coarse universal schemas" for the relaxed fallback has no theoretical standing in the chapter's picture; the schemas listed in Sections 5-6 are shared across much work but their constraints vary by language.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. Müller (2015) (CoreGram) is cited as the source of the principle.

### 11. Relaxed output lies outside the theory's notion of well-formedness

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (strict versus relaxed output)
- Reference: Section 2, p. 5 and footnote 3
- What the chapter says: an object "is well-formed if and only if it conforms to all relevant constraints"; "In most HPSG work, all constraints are equal", with no violable constraints as in Optimality Theory.
- DELPH-IN stance: footnote 3 points to Müller and Kasper (2000) and Oepen et al. (2004) "for an HPSG parser with probabilities or weighted constraints". This is the only place where the chapter mentions parsers.

### 12. Lexical rules have more than one formal status

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3, p. 10; Section 4.2, pp. 20-21, footnotes 25-26
- What the chapter says: lexical rules can be seen "as a generative device, or alternatively, as a set of well-formedness conditions on the lexicon" (Meurers 2001); they can be written with INPUT and OUTPUT features "or with the left hand side as a daughter".
- Consequence for the design: only the daughter-based (unary rule) reading yields a node in the derivation tree; the design depends on that reading.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed (Briscoe and Copestake 1999 is cited for the daughter-based view, p. 10).

## Support for the design

- Single constituent structure, no movement: HPSG is monostratal, with "a single constituent structure"; relations attributed to movement are captured by feature identity (Section 2, p. 5). One tree per analysis is what the tree encoding assumes.
- Structures are simple and flat compared with Minimalism, and there are no functional heads such as T or Num (Section 2, p. 5; Section 4.1, p. 14). Trees are shallow, and leaves are words.
- Limited use of empty elements; no empty complementizers, determiners or null subjects in the constituent structure; much work has no traces (Section 2, pp. 5-6). Gaps are handled in the lexical item of the selecting head (Section 4.1, p. 16).
- Realizational morphology: "there are no morphemes, just lexemes and the words that realise them" (Section 4.1, p. 14; also Section 3, p. 10). Leaves are words, with no sublexical syntactic nodes.
- Lexical rules as unary structures: "it is widely assumed that lexemes and words that are derived through a lexical rule have the lexeme from which they are derived as a daughter" (Briscoe and Copestake 1999; Meurers 2001), so "the DTRS feature is relevant to words as well as phrases" (Section 3, p. 10). In SBCG lexical rules are lexical constructions (footnote 42, p. 34). This matches the chain of unary/lexical rules in the tag.
- Phrase-type constraints "are rather like phrase structure rules", and Ginzburg and Sag (2000: 33) use a rule-like notation for them (Section 5.1, pp. 25-26). A tree whose nodes are phrase-type (rule) names is a natural object in Constructional HPSG.
- A word can be the head daughter of a head-subject or head-filler phrase without a unary phrasal projection (footnotes 31 and 33, pp. 24-25), which keeps unary chains short.
- In the default treatment of order, the PHON of a phrase is the concatenation of its daughters' PHON values (Section 5.2, pp. 27-28), which is the continuity assumption.
- Semantics is built at every node by two simple principles in the MRS style: INDEX shared with the head daughter, and RELS of the mother as the concatenation of the daughters' RELS (Section 5.1, pp. 26-27). "At each node, all kinds of information are available" (Section 3, p. 10). This supports MRS as a by-product of replay.
- Cross-linguistic sharing of schemas: English and Welsh adjective-noun phrases, or English prepositional and Japanese postpositional phrases, "might have exactly the same analysis" apart from PHON order (Section 5.2, p. 28). This is some support for coarse shared schemas with language-specific order.
- Lexical information is mostly inherited from a cross-classified type hierarchy, so "very little information needs to be listed for each specific lexeme" (Section 4.1, p. 18). A lexical type is a compact and informative tag.
- Implementation is valued by the theory itself: "one can only be fully confident that a complex analysis works if it is incorporated into a computer implementation" (Section 2, p. 7).
- Binary branching is in use in HPSG grammars for a number of languages (footnote 30, p. 23).

## Theory strains

- Traces versus traceless extraction: "some debate" (Section 2, p. 6; Sag and Fodor 1995; Müller 2004).
- Gaps in SUBJ lists: allowed by Ginzburg and Sag (2000: 177-183), "but this is controversial" (footnote 22, p. 17).
- Valence features: SUBJ and COMPS; SPR instead of or in addition to SUBJ; a single SUBCAT (Pollard and Sag 1987); a single VALENCE in SBCG, plus X-ARG. "Obviously, there are some important issues here" (Section 4.1, p. 17; footnote 23; Section 6.2, p. 31).
- Form of the Argument Realisation Principle: "complex issues", probably language-specific; the authors decline to fix it (Section 4.1, p. 17).
- Head Feature Principle plus Valence Principle versus the default GHFP; both the informal Valence Principle and the GHFP rely on "unless some constraint says otherwise" (Section 5.1, pp. 23-24).
- Which daughters features: DTRS including the head versus HEAD-DAUGHTER plus NON-HEAD-DAUGHTERS (footnote 9, p. 9).
- Flat versus binary branching (footnote 30, p. 23; footnote 17, p. 14).
- Word versus "light" phrase as the head of a head-complement phrase, to allow coordinated lexical heads (footnote 29, p. 22).
- Subject-auxiliary inversion: a dedicated ternary phrase type (Pollard and Sag 1994; `sai-phrase` in Ginzburg and Sag 2000) versus auxiliaries with two complements and no subject (Sag et al. 2003), with a possible third, order-domain analysis that nobody has proposed for English (footnote 38, p. 29; Section 7.1, p. 33).
- The AUX feature: a property of auxiliary verbs versus a property of positions, with auxiliaries unspecified (footnote 14, p. 13).
- Adverbial PPs as adjuncts versus optional complements (Abeillé and Godard 1997; Bouma et al. 2001; Ginzburg and Sag 2000 versus Levine 2003; Levine and Hukari 2006; Chaves 2009): "There is an unresolved issue here" (Section 6.1, p. 30; footnote 39).
- Determiners as specifiers versus markers/functors; MOD versus SELECT for adjuncts (Section 6.2, pp. 31-32; footnote 40).
- Complementizers as markers versus heads: "There is no consensus here" (Section 6.2, pp. 31-32; footnote 26, p. 20).
- Extraposition: different constituent structure (Kiss 2005) versus same structure with a different order domain (Kathol and Pollard 1995) (Section 7.1, p. 32).
- How much work order domains should do: "an unresolved matter"; whether HPSG with domains is still monostratal: "We will not take a stand on this" (Section 7.1, pp. 33-34).
- Constructional HPSG versus SBCG: SBCG adds constructions and the Sign Principle ("Essentially, then, they are complications") and removes DTRS, HD-DTR and SYNSEM; simplicity "do[es] not obviously favour or disfavour the approach". SYNSEM exists in earlier HPSG only to block selection of a complement's internal structure (Section 7.2, pp. 34-35).
- No agreed semantics: Situation Semantics, MRS, LRS, Montague-style, Frame Semantics (Section 3, p. 13).
- Status of lexical rules: generative device versus well-formedness conditions on the lexicon (footnote 25, p. 20); lexical versus phrasal-constructional accounts (footnote 19, p. 14, against Goldberg 1995; 2006).
- Formal foundations: Pollard and Sag (1987) and (1994) "have rather different formal foundations" (footnote 1, p. 4); the term "unification" is itself flagged (footnote 12, p. 12).
- Competition with other frameworks: against Minimalism on binary branching, empty elements, functional heads, core/periphery and the restriction of "head" to words (Sections 2, 4.1, 5.1); against LFG on distributing information over several structures (footnote 10, p. 10); against Optimality Theory on violable constraints (footnote 3, p. 5).

## Not covered here

All items in this section are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter does not describe any implemented system (LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju) or say how DELPH-IN grammars resolve the choices above (binary rules, no defaults at run time, list encodings of sets, lexical rules as unary rules). Chapter 25 is the place to check.
- Nothing on tokenization, multiword expressions, punctuation or the mapping between tokens and leaves. The remark in caveat 4 that affixal clitics conflict with a treebank's tokenization is my inference from the chapter's analysis; the chapter does not mention UD or dependency treebanks.
- Nothing on ambiguity, parse selection or the size of the rule and lexical-type inventories, which determine the tag vocabulary.
- Quantifier scope and the underspecified parts of MRS are explicitly set aside (p. 26); Chapter 22 is the place to check whether a derivation determines the semantics.
- Coordination, ellipsis, idioms, complex predicates and partial VPs are only pointed to (Chapters 16, 17, 11), although they are likely to matter for continuity and for the dependency conversion.
- The consequences I attach to each caveat ("Consequence for the design") are my reading of what the chapter's statements imply for the parser; the chapter itself does not discuss parsing architectures.
