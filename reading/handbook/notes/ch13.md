# Chapter 13: Unbounded dependencies (Robert D. Borsley and Berthold Crysmann)

Handbook pages 537-594 (text to p. 584, then references). Notes taken strictly from the chapter; my own remarks are marked as such.

## Summary

The chapter surveys the HPSG treatment of unbounded dependencies (UDs): wh-interrogatives, relatives, topicalization, tough constructions, clefts, free relatives, resumption, pied piping, wh in situ, extraposition, and filler-gap mismatches. The common core is the set-valued NONLOCAL feature SLASH (Section 2, pp. 539-543), and the discussion is organized by the bottom of the dependency (what a gap is, Section 3), the middle (how SLASH is passed up, Section 4), and the top (head-filler phrases and their subtypes, lexical binding of SLASH, Section 5). Sections 6-9 cover resumptive pronouns (Welsh, Hausa, Hebrew, Arabic, Persian, Irish), further properties of wh-interrogatives (QUE/WH and REL, Cooper storage for in situ wh, multiple fronting), extraposition (EXTRA feature, order domains, anaphoric "generalized modification"), and "dishonest gaps". The conclusion (p. 582) states that two foundational questions are unresolved: whether gaps are empty categories, and whether SLASH passing is head-driven. An appendix (pp. 582-584) contrasts Sign-Based Construction Grammar (GAP as a list of signs).

Bearing on the parser: high. This is the chapter where the no-empty-elements assumption is an open theoretical dispute, where the feature machinery goes beyond plain unification (set union, default constraints), and where several analyses leave part of the semantics undetermined by the tree (in situ wh scope, the antecedent of an extraposed relative clause). The chapter says nothing about any implemented grammar or platform: LKB, ACE, the ERG, the Grammar Matrix, TRALE, and Enju are never mentioned. All "DELPH-IN stance" entries below are therefore "not discussed", sometimes with a note on what the chapter says about the theoretical mainstream.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Gaps as empty categories (traces) are a live option in the theory

- Severity: high (for any grammar that adopts traces); none for a traceless grammar
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements (also leaves-equal-tokens)
- Reference: Section 2, p. 540; Section 3, pp. 543-545 (Figure 3), pp. 548-549; Section 10, p. 582
- What the chapter says: "Both positions have been developed within HPSG, but probably the view that there is nothing at all in constituent structure is the more widely assumed position" (p. 540). Traces were "a feature of early HPSG work, notably Pollard & Sag (1994: Chapter 4)" and are assumed in "more recent work, e.g. Levine & Hukari (2006), Borsley (2009), Borsley (2013), and Müller (2004c)" (p. 544). "It is not easy to choose between these two approaches" (p. 544). Levine and Hukari use an empty category for complement, subject, and adjunct gaps alike (p. 548). Pollard and Sag (1994) have traces in complement position but not in subject position (p. 548). The conclusion lists the question as unresolved (p. 582).
- Consequence for us: with a trace, Figure 3 has a leaf with no token. A tagger that emits one label per token has nowhere to put it unless the trace is folded into the label of a neighboring token.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter only reports that the traceless view is "probably the most widely assumed position" (p. 544; Sag 1997, Bouma et al. 2001, Ginzburg and Sag 2000, Sag 2010).

### 2. Other empty elements: empty relativizer, null complementizer, empty copula

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 3, p. 545 fn. 4; Section 5, pp. 557-558 and fn. 19
- What the chapter says: "Various empty categories have been proposed in the HPSG literature, most prominently the empty relativiser of Pollard & Sag (1994: Chapter 5)"; Sag et al. (2003) propose a phonologically empty copula for African American Vernacular English (fn. 4). For zero relatives (`the man you talked to`), the options are Sag's (1997) special `head-relative-phrase`, "a special unary branching phrase type (Müller 1999b: Section 10.3.2)", or "a phonologically null counterpart of relative that" (p. 558), the last being the analysis of Alqurashi and Borsley (2012) for Modern Standard Arabic (fn. 19).
- Consequence for us: the same construction has an empty-element analysis and a unary-rule analysis. Only the second fits the design. A grammar ported from the literature may come with the first.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. In the traceless analysis the gap is introduced inside the word, with no rule shown that does it

- Severity: high
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (also tree-encoding)
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 540-541 (11); Section 3, p. 544 (18), pp. 546-547; Appendix, p. 584 (99-100)
- What the chapter says: gaps "are only represented on argument structure". The verb `put` in `What did you put on the table?` "has a gap in its ARG-ST list and therefore only a PP in its COMPS list and in constituent structure" (pp. 540-541). The chapter presents this as a property of the word (18) and does not name a rule or lexical rule that produces it. In SBCG likewise "a member of the ARG-ST list of a lexical head [may] appear not as a member of the word's VALENCE list, but as a member of its GAP list" (p. 584). The chapter also notes that "nonlocal realisation corresponds to lexical valence reduction" (p. 546), and that case, agreement, and linking constraints stated on valence lists must then "hold for lexemes, not words" (p. 547).
- Consequence for us: which argument of a word is the gap is a free choice in the ARG-ST-to-valence mapping. If the derivation tree names only phrase rules and lexical rules, and the gap is not introduced by a named rule, the tree does not say which argument is missing, and replay is no longer search-free. The tagger label of the verb must carry this choice (as a lexical-rule step or a distinct lexical type). The lexeme/word distinction also matters for where the engine checks case and agreement.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. (For extraposition, the chapter does show named lexical rules, Keller's (77) and (78), p. 573-574; see caveat 12.)

### 4. Adjunct gaps: nothing is missing, and three analyses place the gap differently

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (also UD-bridge)
- Reference: Section 3, pp. 548-549 (25-27)
- What the chapter says: in `When did you talk to Lee?` "it is not obvious that there is a gap ... because no obligatory constituent is missing" (p. 548), yet extraction path effects show there is a filler-gap dependency. Analyses: (a) Bouma et al. (2001) and Ginzburg and Sag (2000): verbal adjuncts "are optional extra complements", so the gap is a complement gap; (b) Levine and Hukari (2006): adjuncts modify VP and the gap is an empty category; (c) Chaves (2009): adjuncts modify VP, no empty category, and coordination is relaxed so that the mother's SLASH can hold a group formed from the daughters' SLASH members.
- Consequence for us: the place where the adjunct gap is introduced (which verb, in a multi-clause sentence) has no surface cue; the label of that verb must mark it. Under (a) adjuncts are syntactically complements, which blurs the argument/adjunct distinction that the UD conversion needs. Under (c) the SLASH value of a coordination is computed (group formation), not unified.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Subject gaps and subject wh-questions have competing tree shapes

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (also UD-bridge)
- Reference: Section 3, pp. 547-548 (22-24); Section 5, p. 552 fn. 16
- What the chapter says: Pollard and Sag (1994) stipulate that gaps cannot be subjects and let verbs like `think` "take a VP complement and have a non-empty value for SLASH". Ginzburg and Sag (2000) put subject gaps on both ARG-ST and SUBJ. Levine and Hukari (2006) use a trace. For `Who knows the answer?`, "on some analyses ... who is just a subject and not a filler", for other work it is a filler (fn. 16).
- Consequence for us: the same string gets a head-subject tree or a head-filler tree depending on the grammar, and `who do you think saw Kim` has either a clausal or a VP complement. The tree encoding and the UD conversion rules for subjects must be written per grammar; under the Pollard and Sag analysis the extracted subject of the lower verb is introduced by the higher verb's lexical entry.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Set-valued SLASH with set union is not plain unification

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (engine expressiveness)
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 540, 542-543 (9, 14-16); Section 4, p. 549 (28), p. 551; Section 5, p. 556 (46-47), p. 557 (50); Section 7.1, p. 568; Section 10, p. 582
- What the chapter says: SLASH is `set(local)` (9). The standard Head-Filler Schema (15) splits the head daughter's SLASH as `2 ∪ 3`. SLASH Amalgamation (28) makes a word's SLASH the union of its arguments' SLASH values. The Nonlocal Feature Principle defines each nonlocal feature of the mother as "the set union of the nonlocal features of the daughters" (p. 568). Tough adjectives and relative `that` subtract one element from the complement's SLASH (46, 50). Several SLASH members can be present at once (16, 47). The conclusion says SLASH is "a set- or list-valued feature" (p. 582).
- Consequence for us: union, and "remove the member that matches the filler", are relational constraints. With more than one SLASH member, picking the member to bind is a choice the rule name alone does not fix, unless the grammar uses lists with a fixed discipline. The engine needs either set operations or a grammar that encodes SLASH as (difference) lists.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter mentions the list-valued option only in passing (p. 582) and in SBCG, where GAP "takes as its value a list of signs" (p. 583).

### 7. Default constraints carry the middle of the dependency

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (engine expressiveness)
- Reference: Section 4, pp. 549-550 (28); Section 5, p. 556, p. 560
- What the chapter says: SLASH Amalgamation "is a default constraint, as indicated by the '/'" (p. 550). It is overridden lexically by tough adjectives (46) and relative `that` (50): "We can override the principle in the present case" (p. 556). The Generalised Head Feature Principle of Ginzburg and Sag (2000) says "a headed phrase and its head daughter have the same SYNSEM values unless some other constraint requires something different" (p. 550), and is invoked again to let free relatives differ in HEAD or CASE from their head daughter (p. 560).
- Consequence for us: default unification is not monotonic unification. A replay engine restricted to plain unification can only run such a grammar if the defaults have been compiled out ahead of time into the types of rules and lexical entries.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The non-default alternative mentioned is the Slash Inheritance Principle, which "stipulates that a phrase and its head have the same value for SLASH except at the top of a dependency" (Bouma et al. 2001; p. 550).

### 8. One filler, several gaps; several fillers, crossing dependencies

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 2, p. 543 (16); Section 4, pp. 551-552 (Figure 4, 32); Section 3, p. 549 (27); Section 5, p. 556 (47)
- What the chapter says: both approaches to SLASH passing allow several daughters to share one SLASH value, so across-the-board extraction and parasitic gaps are expected, and "for HPSG, all these gaps have essentially the same status" (p. 552). Chaves's (27) has one plural filler distributed over different gaps in the two conjuncts (`how many sharks_{i+j} ... _i died ... _j were killed`). (16) and (47) have two simultaneous dependencies.
- Consequence for us: the derivation stays an ordinary tree and replay is unaffected, but the filler is an argument of two or more predicates. A UD basic tree gives it one head, so the conversion loses information in one direction and must reconstruct reentrancy in the other. In (27) the filler's index is not identical to either gap's index.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Tops without a filler: the dependency ends inside a lexical entry

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge (also semantics)
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 555-558 (43-51)
- What the chapter says: in tough constructions the subject "cannot share a local feature structure with the gap" because of the case conflict; it is only coindexed with the SLASH member of the infinitival complement (45-46). "Lexical binding of SLASH" also covers `too`/`enough` and relative `that` as complementizer (50). Zero relatives raise "an issue ... about semantics": the clause has clausal semantics, not modifier semantics, and Sag (1997) adds a special `head-relative-phrase`; "One might well wonder how satisfactory this approach is" (p. 558).
- Consequence for us: no head-filler node marks the top, so a converter that looks for a filler rule finds nothing; the link between `the professor` and the gap exists only as coindexation inside the adjective's entry. For zero relatives, the semantics of the relative clause depends on a construction-specific rule.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 10. Free relatives: the initial phrase is both filler and head, and the mother can differ from its head

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge (also deterministic-replay through defaults)
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 559-560 (56-58, Figure 8, fn. 20-21)
- What the chapter says: the wh-phrase "behaves like a filler" and "also behaves like a head". The proposed `free-relative` type makes the first daughter the head. In German, the case of the wh-element can differ from the case the context requires, handled as "specific overrides enforced by the free-relative rule" under the Generalised Head Feature Principle (p. 560). Müller (1999a) has the opposite analysis, in which the initial constituent is not a head (fn. 21).
- Consequence for us: head choice for the dependency conversion flips between analyses, and the proposed analysis needs a rule whose mother overrides head features (see caveat 7).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 11. Resumptive pronouns: a dependency whose bottom is an ordinary overt pronoun

- Severity: medium (multilingual goal); none for English
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (also UD-bridge)
- Reference: Section 6, pp. 560-567 (59-63, Figures 9-11)
- What the chapter says: resumption is treated as a SLASH dependency (since Vaillette 2001). In Borsley's (2010) Welsh analysis the selecting head has "a pronominal argument coindexed with its SLASH value", and Slash Amalgamation is extended "to optionally include" such an element (p. 562); the pronoun is the ordinary pronoun. In Hausa both a gap and a resumptive are possible in the same position (61), and resumptive dependencies escape strong islands (62). Crysmann (2012, 2016) distinguishes `full-local` and `weak-local` SLASH members and `gap`/`resump` synsem types. The alternatives are disjunctive amalgamation (Borsley 2010; Alotaibi and Borsley 2013) or the type system (Crysmann 2016). Persian seems to need "percolation of constructional information" through SLASH, which "does not play nicely with basic assumptions of locality" (p. 566). The Irish hybrid-chain analysis of Assmann et al. (2010) needs "a great deal of disjunction" (p. 567).
- Consequence for us: whether a pronoun is resumptive is decided on the selecting head, optionally, and no rule name in the chapter records the decision. For replay to be deterministic the choice must be visible in the head's label. For UD, the role is filled by an overt pronoun token and also related to a fronted phrase or antecedent.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 12. Extraposition (a): EXTRA feature, lexical rules on the source word, an n-ary schema, and spurious ambiguity

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (also binary-unary-rules)
- Reference: Section 8.1, pp. 573-574 (77-79, fn. 39)
- What the chapter says: Keller (1995) and Müller (1999b) use a separate nonlocal feature EXTRA. Two lexical rules introduce the dependency: one removes a complement from COMPS (77), one adds a modifier to the EXTRA set of a noun or verb (78). The Head-Extra Schema (79) "will bind all extraposition dependencies, which are realised as extraposed daughters", with non-head daughters `1 ... n` ordered by linear precedence statements. Footnote 39 mentions a PERIPHERY feature "introduced to control for spurious ambiguity that could arise from string-vacuous extraposition".
- Consequence for us: the tree stays continuous, which is good. Three costs: (i) the adjunct extraposition lexical rule applies to the antecedent noun, so that noun's label must include a rule whose only evidence is a relative clause or PP far to the right; (ii) schema (79) as given is n-ary with free daughter order; (iii) string-vacuous extraposition yields several derivations for one string and one meaning, so training trees need a canonical choice.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. On (iii) the chapter points to Crysmann (2005b) "for an alternative solution" (fn. 39).

### 13. Extraposition (b): relative clause extraposition as anaphora; the tree does not fix the antecedent

- Severity: high
- Assumption affected: semantics (also deterministic-replay, UD-bridge)
- Reference: Section 8.3, pp. 575-579 (80-86, Figure 12, fn. 46-47)
- What the chapter says: Kiss (2005) proposes a set-valued ANCHOR feature "that indiscriminately percolates up the tree the index (and handle) of any nominal expression. In situ and extraposed relative clauses then semantically bind one of the INDEX/HANDLE pairs contained in the ANCHOR set of the head they syntactically adjoin to" (p. 577). The approach is stated in Minimal Recursion Semantics (fn. 46). Relative clause extraposition "may give rise to split antecedents" (p. 576). Crysmann (2013) keeps the anaphoric analysis for adjuncts and the extraction-like analysis for complements, both through EXTRA with `weak-local` versus `full-local` members.
- Consequence for us: in Figure 12 the relative clause attaches to a verbal projection whose ANCS set holds two indices; the rule application is the same whichever one is bound. A derivation tree of rule names therefore does not determine the MRS; the antecedent choice is extra information that the tree encoding must carry or a later step must resolve. In UD the relative clause depends on its antecedent noun, so the conversion has to read the antecedent off the semantics, and a split antecedent has no single head.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed as such. The chapter says Crysmann (2005b) "proposes to synthesise the approach by Kiss (2005) with that of Keller (1995), using a two-step percolation mechanism that effectively controls for spurious ambiguity" (fn. 47); the reference list gives that paper's title as "Relative clause extraposition in German: An efficient and portable implementation".

### 14. Extraposition (c) and multiple wh-fronting: analyses with word order domains

- Severity: medium (high for a grammar that adopts them; the chapter itself doubts the approach for English extraposition)
- Assumption affected: continuity
- Reference: Section 8.2, p. 575 and fn. 40; Section 7.2, pp. 569-570 (69)
- What the chapter says: Kathol (1995, 2000) and Kathol and Pollard (1995) model German extraposition with Reape-style order domains; extraposition from NP "already necessitates special mechanisms, such as partial compaction". For stricter-order languages such as English "a scrambling approach to extraposition becomes highly questionable" (p. 575). Separately, in Serbo-Croatian multiple fronted wh-phrases behave as one unit for second-position clitics, and Penn (1999) "proposes a topological analysis based on extended word order domains" (p. 570).
- Consequence for us: in these analyses the yield of a node is not a contiguous token span, so the derivation tree is not a bracketing of the string and the tagging-based tree encoding does not apply directly.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 15. Scope of in situ wh-phrases is not fixed by the tree

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 7.2, p. 569 (68); Section 7.3, pp. 570-571 (70-72)
- What the chapter says: Ginzburg and Sag (2000) follow Pollard and Sag (1994) "in adopting a Cooper storage, which enables them to have the in situ wh-quantifier in (68) retrieved either as a parameter of the embedded interrogative clause, or as a parameter of the matrix question" (p. 569). WH fixes the scope of fronted wh-phrases only; "In situ wh-quantifiers ... are permitted to take arbitrarily wide scope". Where in situ is the norm (Iraqi Arabic, Coptic), the interrogative dependency runs through QUE percolation, with language-specific locality (Johnson and Lappin 1997; Crysmann and Reintges 2014).
- Consequence for us: with storage and retrieval, one derivation tree corresponds to several readings unless the retrieval site is recorded in the tree or the semantics is left underspecified. "The tree is a complete recipe for the semantics" holds only in the second sense.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 16. Pied piping adds a second nonlocal dependency inside the filler

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (engine expressiveness)
- Reference: Section 7.1, pp. 567-569 (64-67)
- What the chapter says: QUE/WH and REL "establish dependencies within the filler itself", in addition to SLASH; in relatives REL shares the relative pronoun's index with the antecedent noun at arbitrary depth. Passing is by the Nonlocal Feature Principle (set union over all daughters) or head-driven (Sag 1997; Ginzburg and Sag 2000). Ginzburg and Sag restrict WH amalgamation "to the least oblique element on ARG-ST" (p. 568).
- Consequence for us: no effect on tree shape. The engine needs the same set machinery as for SLASH (caveat 6), for three or four nonlocal features. The antecedent-to-relative-pronoun link is carried only by index sharing, which the UD conversion can use.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 17. Long-distance facts show up in the lexical labels of words along the path

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3, pp. 545-546 (20-21); Section 4, pp. 550-551 (30); Section 6, pp. 566-567
- What the chapter says: Hausa and Mauritian verbs take a different form when the direct object is extracted, the same form as intransitives. Irish particles `aL`/`goN` mark every clause between filler and gap. Irish complementizers also register gap versus resumptive at the bottom.
- Consequence for us: the correct label (lexical type or inflectional rule) of a verb or particle depends on a dependency whose ends may be far away. The tagger sees the whole sentence, and replay checks the choice, so this is a learnability point and a useful consistency check, not a structural problem.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 18. Filler-gap mismatches ("dishonest gaps") and tops that are not head-filler phrases

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge (also other: the assumption that filler and gap share LOCAL)
- Reference: Section 1, p. 539 (8); Section 5, p. 558 (52-53); Section 9, pp. 580-582 (87-95, Figure 13)
- What the chapter says: in `Kim will sing, which Lee won't` the filler is nominal and the gap a VP; Arnold and Borsley (2010) let an auxiliary's unrealized complement optionally carry a nominal in SLASH. In `That he might be wrong, he didn't think of`, Bouma et al. (2001) use an NP gap with an S in SLASH; Webelhuth (2012) instead combines a clause with `S[SLASH {NP}]` in a construction that is not a head-filler phrase. In clefts (`It's me that likes beer`) the focused phrase and the gap cannot share an index; "There are important challenges here" (p. 558).
- Consequence for us: a converter cannot assume that the filler has the category, or even the index, of the role it fills. The first analysis also ties relatives to auxiliary complement ellipsis, where a complement is unrealized with an empty SLASH.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 19. Figures show a ternary clause; SBCG changes the feature geometry

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules; other (cross-grammar tooling)
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 541-542 (Figures 1-2); Appendix, pp. 583-584 (96-100)
- What the chapter says: the inverted clause `did you put on the table` is drawn as one node with daughters V, NP, VP. In SBCG, signs have no DTRS, there are no `synsem` or `local` objects, VALENCE replaces SUBJ and COMPS, ARG-ST and GAP are lists of signs, and filler-gap identity is stipulated as identity "except for" WH and REL (Sag 2012: 166).
- Consequence for us: the theoretical literature assumes flat rules where convenient; and tools that walk feature paths (inspection, conversion) cannot assume one geometry across HPSG variants. The "identical except for some features" constraint is again not plain unification.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- The traceless analysis is "probably the more widely assumed position" (p. 540) and "probably the most widely assumed position" (p. 544), with empirical arguments for it: a gap cannot be a conjunct (19), and Hausa and Mauritian morphology treats extraction like valence reduction (pp. 545-546). In SBCG too, gaps "are just ordinary signs which appear in a GAP list and not in a VALENCE list" (p. 584).
- Everything about a UD is passed through local trees by a feature (pp. 540-542, Figures 1-2). The trees are continuous and the leaves are the words. No movement, no second structure.
- "It is generally accepted" that all UD types (with or without filler, gap or resumptive, with or without mismatch) use the one feature SLASH/GAP (p. 582). One mechanism in the engine covers the whole family.
- The top of each construction is a distinct phrase type in a hierarchy (`wh-interr-cl`, `wh-rel-cl`, `top-cl`, `the-cl`, `fin-wh-rel-cl`, `inf-wh-rel-cl`; Figures 5-7, pp. 554-555). A derivation node labeled with such a type identifies the construction directly, which helps both the tree encoding and the UD conversion.
- Zero relatives can be handled by "a special unary branching phrase type" (Müller 1999b; Sag 2010: 531; p. 558), which is exactly a unary rule in the tree.
- Under the head-driven approach, extraction path effects are "just a contrast between a verb which is [SLASH { }] and a verb which is [SLASH {[ ]}]" (p. 551): locally checkable on the word during replay.
- The trend in the resumption literature is from lexical ambiguity and disjunction toward underspecification in a type hierarchy (`local` > `full-local`, `weak-local`; `slashed` > `gap`, `resump`; Figures 10-11, pp. 564-566). That is ordinary typed unification. Resumptives are "always the ordinary pronouns" (McCloskey's generalization, p. 563), so no special lexical entries for the pronoun tokens.
- For extraposition, the nonlocal-feature analysis (EXTRA) keeps continuous trees, and the chapter judges the order-domain alternative "highly questionable" for languages like English (p. 575).
- Lexical rules (77-78) are the stated mechanism for introducing extraposition dependencies (p. 573), which matches a unary-chain component in the token label.
- Spurious ambiguity is recognized as a problem to be designed out (fn. 39, fn. 47), in line with wanting one derivation per analysis.
- The chapter treats the Complex NP example (16) and similar degraded sentences as grammatical but hard to process (p. 543), so the grammar licenses them; no extra grammatical filter is expected.

## Theory strains

- Traces versus no traces (Section 2, p. 540; Section 3, pp. 543-549; Section 10, p. 582). Listed by the authors as unresolved. Pollard and Sag (1994) mix the two (complement traces, no subject traces); Levine and Hukari (2006) use traces throughout; the mainstream uses none. A third option, gaps on ARG-ST and valence lists but not in the tree, "has rarely been considered" (p. 543).
- Head-driven versus non-head-driven SLASH passing (Section 4, pp. 549-551; Section 10, p. 582). The head-driven approach (Sag 1997; Bouma et al. 2001; Ginzburg and Sag 2000) handles extraction path effects but "says nothing about" non-headed phrases such as asymmetric coordination (31). The Nonlocal Feature Principle (Pollard and Sag 1994; Levine and Hukari 2006; Chaves 2012) handles those but needs "some extra element" for path effects. "It is not easy to choose" (p. 551).
- Within the head-driven camp: Slash Inheritance Principle (a stipulation with an exception at the top) versus the Generalised Head Feature Principle (a default) (p. 550). SLASH Amalgamation is itself a default overridden lexically (pp. 550, 556).
- Consequences of the traceless analysis for the rest of the lexicon (Section 3, pp. 546-547): case, agreement, and ergative linking must be stated on lexemes; Borsley (2016) argues from Archi agreement for constituent-structure constraints, "which will favour a trace-based perspective" (fn. 10).
- Subject extraction and the that-trace effect (pp. 547-548): Pollard and Sag (1994) stipulate no subject gaps and add VP-complement entries for `think`; Ginzburg and Sag (2000) attribute the effect to `that` not combining with a non-empty SUBJ; Levine and Hukari (2006) argue the Ginzburg and Sag account of the adverb effect (24) is "unsatisfactory".
- Adjuncts as complements versus VP modifiers (pp. 548-549): Bouma et al. (2001) versus Levine (2003) and Levine and Hukari (2006), with the cumulative-scope example (26); Chaves (2009) offers a third route through relaxed coordination.
- Is the tough construction unbounded at all (p. 550 fn. 12)? Grover (1995) proposes a local account for English; German and French counterparts are local.
- Relative `that`: complementizer (lexical binding) or relative pronoun and filler (Sag 1997) (p. 557).
- Zero relatives (pp. 557-558): Sag's (1997) `head-relative-phrase` is openly doubted ("One might well wonder how satisfactory this approach is"); alternatives are a unary phrase or a null complementizer.
- The clause-type hierarchy is admitted to be "complex" and needs ad hoc types such as `standard-head-filler-phrase` solely to allow `that` in the-clauses (pp. 554-555).
- Clefts (p. 558): the focused phrase and the gap cannot share an index (`It's me that likes beer`); cross-linguistic variation in which index features agree (German versus French, pp. 558-559). No analysis is offered.
- Free relatives (pp. 559-560): filler, head, or both; the both-analysis needs overrides for German case mismatches; Müller (1999a) takes the non-head view and then needs a mechanism for the matching effects (fn. 21).
- Resumption (Section 6, pp. 560-567): separate RESUMP feature (Vaillette 2001), "cumbersome" for ATB, versus a single SLASH; Borsley (Welsh) versus Crysmann (Hausa) on how gaps and resumptives differ; ambiguity versus underspecification; Alotaibi and Borsley's (2013) "stipulative nominative case assignment" versus Crysmann's (2017) weight-based account. Open: Persian (top-of-dependency information passed through SLASH violates locality) and Irish hybrid chains (heavy disjunction).
- English-specific stipulations in Ginzburg and Sag (2000): singleton WH set and non-recursive head-filler structures (p. 570); the ban on QUE percolation from a right daughter is "a parochial constraint of English" (p. 571); locality of QUE percolation differs between Iraqi Arabic and Coptic (p. 571).
- Extraposition (Section 8, pp. 572-579): three competing approaches (EXTRA as rightward extraction; order domains; anaphoric ANCHOR percolation). Kiss (2005) separates complements from adjuncts; Müller (1999b, 2004a) unifies them under EXTRA with counterexamples to Kiss's locality claim; Crysmann (2013) says the unified analysis "severely overgenerates" and proposes a hybrid. Order domains need "partial compaction", motivated only by extraposition (p. 575). ANCHOR percolation is "indiscriminate".
- Dishonest gaps (Section 9, pp. 580-582): once LOCAL and the SLASH member may differ, the core identity that defines a gap (11) is given up case by case. Bouma et al. (2001) versus Webelhuth (2012) on clausal "fillers".
- HPSG versus SBCG (Appendix, pp. 582-584): set of `local` objects versus list of signs; SBCG must stipulate identity "except for" WH and REL, and "would need to be extended to account for some of the phenomena considered here".
- Relation to other frameworks: the SLASH idea is inherited from GPSG (fn. 1); optional wh-fronting in Iraqi Arabic is cited as a problem for transformational models (p. 570); the chapter claims the approach "seems to compare quite favourably" with other frameworks (p. 582) without argument. Island constraints are deferred to Chapter 15 (Chaves).

## Not covered here

All items in this section are my own remarks, not claims of the chapter.

- The chapter never mentions the ERG, the LKB, ACE, the Grammar Matrix, TRALE, or Enju, and does not say how any implemented grammar introduces gaps, passes SLASH, or binds it. In particular it does not say whether implemented grammars use extraction rules (unary phrase rules or lexical rules that move an argument to SLASH), lists or difference lists in place of sets, or compiled-out defaults. Caveats 3, 6, and 7 need to be checked against the ERG source before they are counted as real obstacles; I expect the ERG's own choices to remove most of them, but that expectation comes from outside this chapter.
- Nothing on parsing complexity or processing of UDs, apart from the remark that some multiple-gap sentences are hard to process (p. 543).
- Island constraints are deferred to Chapter 15; relative clauses in detail to Chapter 14; order domains to Chapter 10; binding to Chapter 20; MRS to Chapter 22.
- Semantics is touched only through Cooper storage (p. 569) and Kiss's use of MRS handles (p. 577). The chapter does not discuss underspecified scope representations as an answer to caveat 15; whether the MRS produced by replay leaves in situ wh scope underspecified is something to check elsewhere.
- Nothing on UD or dependency conversion; all UD-bridge consequences above are my inferences from the analyses described.
- Right node raising, gapping, and other ellipsis that interacts with extraction are not covered here (auxiliary complement ellipsis is mentioned only in connection with `which Lee won't`, pp. 580-581).
- English extraposition (`a man came in who ...`) is not analyzed; all extraposition data are German, and the chapter only says that a scrambling-style account is implausible for English.
- The SLASH member is drawn as the whole `local` object of the gap; the chapter does not discuss how much of it implemented grammars actually share.
