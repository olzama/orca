# Chapter 28: HPSG and Minimalism (Robert D. Borsley and Stefan Müller)

Handbook pages 1253-1329 (text 1253-1306, references 1307-1329). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter compares HPSG with the Minimalist Program and concludes that HPSG is the more promising framework. Section 2 contrasts the two in formalization, data quality, and the use of invisible entities. Section 3 contrasts the views of grammar: declarative constraints, underspecification, and a rich hierarchy of types and phrase types in HPSG, against a few procedural operations (Merge, Agree, Move), labeling, and feature deletion in Minimalism. Section 4 argues that Minimalist structures are too complex (empty functional heads, movement, lexical decomposition, cartography) and at the same time too simple (only binary, only headed, one mechanism for local and nonlocal dependencies), using flat structures, coordination, the NPN construction, passive, scrambling, fillerless gaps, and resumptive pronouns as test cases. Section 5 covers processing, acquisition, and restrictiveness.

Bearing on the parser: moderate and mostly indirect. The chapter is a framework comparison, so it describes HPSG analyses only as far as needed to contrast them with Minimalist ones. It never mentions DELPH-IN, the ERG, LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, derivation trees, or dependency grammar and UD. Its contacts with implementation are: theoretical work paired with implementations in the CoreGram project and in Müller's German textbook (Section 2.1, p. 1256); the citation of Copestake, Flickinger, Pollard and Sag (2005) for scope underspecification (Section 3.2, p. 1264); the citation of Flickinger (2008) for unary type-shifting rules (footnote 27, p. 1284); and the statement that Torr's Minimalist Grammar parser is equivalent to an HPSG analysis with Reape-style discontinuous constituents and SLASH passing (Section 2.1, p. 1258). Every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is therefore "not discussed" unless stated. The chapter gives more support for the design than threats to it; the threats concern the multilingual goal and the UD bridge more than English and the ERG.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Flat (ternary and wider) structures are widely accepted in HPSG

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules, tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3.6, p. 1271; Section 4.1.4, p. 1276; Section 4.2.1, pp. 1284-1288 (Figures 11, 13, 14; footnote 29); Section 5.3, pp. 1304, 1306
- What the chapter says: The restriction to binary branching "excludes various analyses that have been assumed within HPSG". Examples: the ternary VP of Pollard and Sag (1994: 36) "and much other work" for "gave a book to Lee"; a flat structure with subject and complements as sisters of the verb for the auxiliary-initial clause "Will Kim be here?"; a flat structure for Welsh finite verb-initial clauses (Borsley 2006a) and for Archi (Borsley 2016). The chapter calls the binary restriction "dubious" (p. 1304) and says the one remaining universal would be that linguistic objects are combined, "without the restriction to binarity" (p. 1306). It also says that binary branching structures are "a viable option in HPSG": Müller (2015a; 2021d) argues for binary structures obtained by parametrizing the Head-Complement Schema for head-initial, head-final, and free-order languages (p. 1286).
- Why it matters: The assumption of unary and binary rules is a choice inside HPSG, and it holds for some grammars only. A grammar written in the flat tradition (theoretical HPSG grammars of English, Welsh, Archi) would need n-ary rules in the tree encoding and in replay, or a systematic binarization. (This consequence is my inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter reports only that both flat and binary analyses exist in HPSG and that Müller favors binary ones.

### 2. HPSG has "a minimum" of empty elements, which is more than zero; German uses the analogue of head movement

- Severity: medium (for the multilingual goal; low for English)
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements, leaves-equal-tokens
- Reference: Section 4.1, p. 1273; Section 4.2.1, p. 1288; footnote 27, p. 1284; Section 5.1, p. 1299
- What the chapter says: HPSG expressions have "a single relatively simple constituent structure with a minimum of phonologically empty elements" (pp. 1273, 1299). The chapter does not say "none". On verb-initial clauses: "The analogue to head-movement analyses is standard among HPSG grammarians of German and there is data from apparent multiple frontings that makes an analysis which is the HPSG analogue of head-movement unavoidable (Müller 2003a; 2005b)" (p. 1288). Footnote 27 says that the effect of unary projections can be modeled by empty heads and points to Müller (2016: Sections 19.2 and 19.5) for the general relation between empty elements, unary projections, and lexical rules.
- Why it matters: A German grammar in the mainstream theoretical tradition has a leaf without a token (the verb trace), or an equivalent device. The footnote suggests the way out that fits the design: recode the empty element as a unary rule, which the tag can carry. (The recoding is my inference from footnote 27; the chapter states only the equivalence of effects.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Non-headed structures: coordination and the NPN construction

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge, tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3.6, p. 1271; Section 4.2.2, pp. 1288-1290 (examples 36-40, footnote 32); Section 4.2.3, pp. 1290-1291 (examples 41-42)
- What the chapter says: "For HPSG, as for many other approaches, there are headed expressions and non-headed expressions." Coordinate structures are the most important non-headed case. A coordination is not equivalent to its first conjunct ("Kim and Sandy" is plural); conjuncts may differ in category ("a linguist and proud of it", "angry and in pain"), which led to analyses where the coordinate structure has whatever properties the conjuncts share (Sag 2003); and conjuncts may be words ("Kim [criticized and insulted] his boss"), so the coordination inherits selectional information and then combines with a complement. The position that conjuncts are always phrases "seems untenable" (footnote 32; Abeillé 2006; Müller 2018). In the NPN construction ("student after student", "day after day after day", "miserable day after miserable day") no element can be identified as the head; the chapter endorses a phrasal construction (Jackendoff 2008; Bargmann 2015) that also covers repeated PN sequences.
- Why it matters: A conversion to UD has to pick a head for every phrase. For non-headed rules the choice is a convention of the conversion and cannot be read off the grammar. Word-level coordination means that the shared object attaches to a coordinated lexical node, so the HPSG tree and the UD tree (object attached to the first verb) differ in shape. NPN needs a special rule in the derivation inventory. (The UD consequences are my inference; the chapter does not mention dependencies.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Unbounded dependencies without a filler, without a gap, or with several gaps

- Severity: medium (UD bridge), low (tree encoding and replay)
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge, tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 4.2.5, pp. 1296-1298 (examples 51-52, footnote 36); Section 2.1, p. 1257 footnote 5 and p. 1258
- What the chapter says: In the SLASH-based approach "there is no assumption that there should always be a filler at the top of an unbounded dependency" (bare relatives, too/enough complements, tough complements; Pollard and Sag 1994: Chapter 4), and "no reason to think that there will always be a gap at the bottom": Welsh resumptive pronouns involve the same mechanism as gaps (Willis 2011; Borsley 2010; 2013), with "slightly different approaches" in Vaillette (2002), Taghvaipour (2010), Borsley (2013), and Crysmann (2012; 2016). Examples with more than one gap ("Who does Kim like _ and Lee hate _?", "Which book did you criticize _ without reading _?") are "expected within the SLASH-based approach" (Levine and Sag 2003; Pollard and Sag 1994: Section 4.6).
- Why it matters: The extraction path is carried by feature values, so the derivation tree stays continuous and has no trace leaf; this supports the design. The cost is on the UD side: a dependency whose "filler" is absent or is a pronoun in situ, and a filler linked to two gaps, cannot be read off tree shape and is not a single-head dependency. The bridge needs to recover these from the replayed feature structure or from rule names. (My inference; the chapter does not discuss dependency conversion.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter says only that SLASH passing is "standardly used in HPSG" (p. 1257, footnote 5).

### 5. Order domains and discontinuous constituents exist as an alternative in HPSG

- Severity: low to medium (multilingual goal)
- Assumption affected: continuity
- Reference: Section 2.1, p. 1258; Section 4.1.6, p. 1283 with footnote 26
- What the chapter says: Müller (2020: 177-180) shows that the derivations of Torr's wide-coverage Minimalist Grammar are "equivalent to an HPSG analysis assuming Reape-style discontinuous constituents (Reape 1994) and a SLASH passing mechanism". Footnote 26 names Reape-style order domains, added to the head-adjunct schema, as one way to impose order constraints on adjuncts that are not sisters; the other way named is Engelkamp, Erbach and Uszkoreit (1992). The chapter refers to Chapter 10 (Müller 2021b: Section 6) for order domains.
- Why it matters: Discontinuous constituents are an accepted HPSG device. A grammar that uses them does not give a concatenative derivation tree. The chapter does not say how common such grammars are.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Free constituent order is handled by combining arguments in any order

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding, UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 4.2.4.1, pp. 1292-1293; Section 4.2.4.2, pp. 1293-1296 (examples 46-50, Figure 17)
- What the chapter says: The analysis "standardly used in HPSG grammars of German" (Gunji 1986 for Japanese; Hinrichs and Nakazawa 1989; Kiss 1995; Meurers 1999; Müller 2005a; 2021c) lets arguments combine with the head in any order, with no movement and no trace. In SOV languages like German and Dutch the subject is treated like the other arguments and has no designated position. In passives the nominative argument stays where the accusative object would be; only the case differs.
- Why it matters: The derivation stays continuous and binary, which supports the design. Two costs: the grammatical function of an argument is not visible in the tree shape or rule name and has to come from the feature structure (valence list, case) after replay; and the tagger has no positional cue for which argument a head-argument node saturates. (My inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Large inventory of phrase types and "peripheral" constructions

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 2.1, p. 1257 (example 2); Section 3.3, p. 1267; Section 4.1.5, pp. 1276-1280 (examples 25-29)
- What the chapter says: Early HPSG (Pollard and Sag 1987; 1994) had a few general phrase types. Work in the 1990s concluded "that this is too simple and that a more complex system of phrase types is needed". Sag (1997), Ginzburg and Sag (2000), and Sag (2010) use many types: for example fin-wh-fill-rel-cl and inf-wh-fill-rel-cl, and two types for finite wh-interrogatives (subject and non-subject), the latter tying the features IC and INV. Each of the further unbounded dependency constructions (exclamatives, topicalization, comparatives, tough and too complements, the-clauses) needs its own treatment. A satisfactory grammar also has to cover constructions such as nominal extraposition, the comparative correlative, and the X's Way construction.
- Why it matters: The set of rule names that the tree encoding has to predict is large and has a long tail of rare construction-specific rules. The relaxed fallback from "coarse universal schemas" corresponds to the early-HPSG inventory, which the chapter says is too simple for the full complexity of syntax. (My inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Lexical rules and unary syntactic rules carry much of the analysis

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding
- Reference: footnote 27, p. 1284; Section 4.2.4.1, p. 1293; Section 4.1.3, p. 1276
- What the chapter says: HPSG uses unary branching structures in syntax and in the lexicon ("lexical rules basically are unary branching structures"). Unary syntactic rules are used for semantic type shifting (Flickinger 2008: 91-92; Gerbl 2007; Müller 2009). Passive is a lexical rule that maps a verb stem to a participle with a reduced argument structure list (Pollard and Sag 1987: 215; Müller 2003b; Müller and Ørsnes 2013). Morphology is realizational: affixes are phonology realizing properties of words or lexemes, with no syntactic heads for them (Crysmann 2021).
- Why it matters: This matches the design (unary chains are part of the tag), and it confirms that the unary chain is where voice, type shifting, and inflection are decided. The tag space grows with the product of lexical types and chains. (My inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed as such. Flickinger (2008) is cited for the unary type-shifting analysis without mention of the ERG.

### 9. Underspecification: the result of applying the constraints need not be fully resolved

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics, deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 3.2, pp. 1264-1265; Section 4.2.4.2, pp. 1294-1295
- What the chapter says: "HPSG: Everything that is not ruled out works." Types can be underspecified in lexical items and in schemata (Sag 1997: verbal as a supertype of verb and comp). Quantifier scope can be left unspecified (Copestake, Flickinger, Pollard and Sag 2005; Richter and Sailer 1999), with several scopings possible when no constraint decides. The scrambling discussion shows that the available scopings in German depend on argument order: the base order is unambiguous and a reordered sentence has two readings (Frey 1993), and trace-based analyses predict readings that do not exist (Kiss 2001; Fanselow 2001).
- Why it matters: The semantics obtained by replay is an underspecified description, which fits MRS. A derivation determines one such description; it does not pick a scoping. The German data imply that scope constraints depend on word order, so they would have to be stated in the grammar or in a later resolution step. The chapter does not show how the HPSG base-generation analysis derives the two readings.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed beyond the citation of Copestake et al. (2005).

### 10. Graded acceptability: weighted and violable constraints

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (strict versus relaxed output)
- Reference: Section 3.1, pp. 1263-1264 (example 8); Section 5.3, p. 1305
- What the chapter says: Because HPSG grammars are feature-value pairs with equalities, "it is possible to weigh constraints, admit constraint violations, and work with structures with violated constraints" (Sorace and Keller 2005; Pullum and Scholz 2001). Example (8) shows sentences with a growing number of violations (agreement, case, possessive form, determiner order, preposition order). Several island constraints "should not be modelled by hard constraints in competence grammars" (p. 1305).
- Why it matters: A strict replay is all-or-nothing, while the chapter's view of the theory is graded. The relaxed path could record which unification failed and continue, giving a graded result between "strict" and "universal schemas". (This design suggestion is my inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 11. Little is assumed to be universal

- Severity: medium (relaxed output and multilingual goal), none for English
- Assumption affected: other (coarse universal schemas for the relaxed output)
- Reference: Section 2.3, p. 1262; Section 5.3, pp. 1305-1306
- What the chapter says: "Grammars should be motivated on a language-specific basis" (Müller 2015c: 25). HPSG researchers develop language-internal fragments and generalize over them, which "leaves the option open that languages can have very little in common as far as syntax is concerned". Koenig and Michelson (2012) argue that Oneida has no syntactic valence; if so, "not even central concepts like valence and argument structure would be universal. The only remaining universal would be that we combine linguistic objects."
- Why it matters: Universal schemas such as head-complement and head-subject presuppose valence. By the chapter's lights they are generalizations over many grammars and may not apply to every language.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The CoreGram project (Müller 2015c) is mentioned on p. 1256 as theoretical work paired with implementations.

### 12. The formalism has Turing power

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 5.3, p. 1305
- What the chapter says: "The basic formalism of HPSG has Turing power" (Pollard 1999); the formalism should not be the constraining factor (Pollard 1997); theories of individual languages are much more restrictive (Müller 2016: Chapter 17).
- Why it matters: Replay with no search is bounded by the tree as long as each rule application is a finite unification. A grammar that uses relational constraints of unrestricted power would not give that guarantee. (My inference; the chapter does not discuss which devices particular grammars use.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- One simple surface structure. HPSG expressions have "a single relatively simple constituent structure with a minimum of phonologically empty elements" (Section 4.1, p. 1273; Section 5.1, p. 1299). HPSG grammars are "surface-oriented" and "the syntactic labels correspond for the most part to classical part of speech categorizations" (Section 4.1.6, pp. 1280-1282). HPSG structures are "quite closely related to the observable data" (Section 5.2, p. 1303). This favors both the tree encoding and a UD bridge.
- No movement. Passive, raising, unaccusatives, and scrambling are analyzed without any constituent occupying more than one position (Section 4.1.1, pp. 1273-1274; Section 4.2.4, pp. 1291-1296). Unbounded dependencies use SLASH, with no requirement of a filler or a trace-like gap (Section 4.2.5, pp. 1296-1297).
- Constraints are local. "All constraints are purely local and never affect anything larger than the immediate tree consisting of an expression and its daughters" (Section 5.1, p. 1299). Subject-verb agreement is a chain of local relations (Section 3.3, p. 1266). This is what lets replay apply one rule at one node at a time.
- Constraints are declarative and unordered (Section 3.1, p. 1263; Section 5.1, pp. 1299-1301). The result of replay does not depend on the order in which the nodes of the tree are visited.
- Unification does not destroy information. Saturated valence items are not projected, but "objects with a certain structure and with certain features are not modified"; a case-marked NP keeps its case (Section 3.5, p. 1271). The replayed feature structure therefore keeps the information needed later, for example the case of a free relative pronoun.
- Semantics is built alongside syntax, with no covert movement. "Syntactic analyses are complemented by separate semantic and morphological analyses" (Section 3.1, p. 1262); scope is underspecified (Section 3.2, pp. 1264-1265); adverbs attach to verbal projections and yield "the same valence but augmented semantics" (Section 4.1.6, p. 1282); information structure is represented in its own part of the feature description (p. 1282).
- Unary rules and lexical rules are standard devices (footnote 27, p. 1284), and empty heads and unary projections can do the same work.
- Adjunction is recursive and category-preserving; head features such as verb form are projected through adjunction, so a higher head can select them locally (Section 4.1.6, pp. 1282-1283, Figure 10).
- Binding refers to the ARG-ST list, not to tree configuration (Pollard and Sag 1992; Section 4.2.1, p. 1286). The tree shape is then free to be chosen on other grounds, including binary branching.
- Binary branching is "a viable option in HPSG" (Müller 2015a; 2021d; Section 4.2.1, p. 1286).
- Type hierarchies let lexical items and schemata be underspecified, which reduces the number of entries and rules (Section 3.2, p. 1264) and captures generalizations over classes (Section 4.1.5, p. 1280; Section 5.2, p. 1303).
- Formalization and implementation are treated as a strength of HPSG: implementations "show that the analyses are consistent and complete" (Section 2.1, p. 1256). This supports the goal of inspectable grammars.
- The Minimalist parsers that work at scale converge on HPSG devices: Lin's PrinciPar and MiniPar and Torr's parser use SLASH passing (Section 2.1, pp. 1257-1258).

## Theory strains

- Flat versus binary structure inside HPSG (Section 4.2.1, pp. 1284-1288). Pollard and Sag (1994: 36) and "much other work" use a ternary VP; Borsley (2006a; 2016) argues for flat clauses in Welsh and Archi; Müller (2015a; 2021d) argues for binary branching with a parametrized Head-Complement Schema. The chapter, written by one proponent of each, declares both viable and gives no criterion for choosing.
- Empty elements (Sections 2.3, 3.3, 4.1, 4.2.1, 4.2.5; pp. 1260-1262, 1267, 1273, 1288, 1293, 1296). The chapter criticizes invisible heads, empty operators, and silent expletives in Minimalism as unlearnable or ad hoc, and at the same time reports that the analogue of head movement is standard and "unavoidable" in HPSG grammars of German. HPSG is described as having "a minimum" of empty elements, with no principle given for when one is justified. Footnote 27 (p. 1284) concedes that empty heads, unary projections, and lexical rules can do the same work.
- Few schemas versus many phrase types (Section 3.3, p. 1267; Section 4.1.5, pp. 1276-1280). Early HPSG resembled Minimalism in having a few general phrase types; this was judged too simple. The chapter also grants that a hierarchical classification of empty complementizers "would be mimicking the HPSG approach with its hierarchy of phrase types" (p. 1280), which means the phrasal and the lexical encoding of the same generalizations are close to interchangeable.
- Adjuncts and adverb order (Section 4.1.6, pp. 1282-1284, footnote 26). Adjuncts are usually not sisters in a local tree, "but see Kasper 1994 and Bouma and van Noord 1998". Order constraints among non-sister adjuncts need extra machinery (Engelkamp et al. 1992, or Reape-style order domains). Actual analyses of adverb order are few (Koenig and Muansuwan 2005; Abeillé and Godard 2004).
- Coordination (Section 4.2.2, pp. 1288-1290). Non-headed; the category of unlike-category coordinations is whatever the conjuncts share (Sag 2003); whether conjuncts can be words is disputed (Kayne 1994 and Bruening 2018 against Abeillé 2006 and Müller 2018). The chapter criticizes Minimalist ConjP analyses in detail and refers to Chapter 16 for the HPSG analyses.
- NPN and other idiosyncratic constructions (Section 2.1, p. 1257; Section 4.2.3, pp. 1290-1291). Handled by dedicated phrasal constructions. The chapter presents this as a strength; it is also a point where the analysis is stipulated per construction.
- Subject wh-interrogatives (Section 4.1.5, p. 1278). The wh-subject "is followed by what looks like a VP, although it may be a clause with a gap in subject position". Ginzburg and Sag (2000) need separate types for subject and non-subject wh-interrogatives and a constraint identifying IC and INV values.
- Resumptive pronouns (Section 4.2.5.2, p. 1297). Four HPSG proposals with "slightly different approaches" (Vaillette 2002; Taghvaipour 2010; Borsley 2013; Crysmann 2012; 2016).
- Free relatives (Section 3.4, pp. 1268-1270; Section 3.5, pp. 1270-1271). Pied-piping and non-matching free relatives in German, and the double role of the case of the relative pronoun, are used against Minimalist labeling and feature deletion. For the HPSG side the chapter only points to Müller (1999a).
- What belongs in the grammar (Section 3.1, pp. 1263-1264; Section 5.3, p. 1305). Weighted, violable constraints are presented as possible in HPSG without being standard practice. Subjacency "does not hold in the form stated", several island constraints should not be hard constraints, and unattested patterns such as string reversal are attributed to performance.
- Restrictiveness (Section 5.3, pp. 1303-1306). The formalism has Turing power, above the mildly context-sensitive level usually assumed for natural languages. The chapter accepts this, following Pollard (1997), and places all restrictiveness in the individual grammars.
- Universals (Section 2.3, p. 1262; Section 5.3, pp. 1305-1306). HPSG researchers usually do not believe in Universal Grammar. If Koenig and Michelson (2012) are right about Oneida, valence and argument structure are not universal.
- Processing and acquisition (Section 5, pp. 1298-1303). HPSG "has shown less interest" in acquisition and relies on Construction Grammar work. The processing argument is about fit in principle; left-to-right incremental processing is stated as a fact about performance, with no incremental HPSG model given in this chapter.
- Competition with Minimalism itself. On passive (movement versus lexical rule; German unmarked order as counterevidence, Section 4.2.4.1), scrambling (movement versus base generation; Fanselow 2001 adopts the HPSG-style analysis within Minimalism, Section 4.2.4.2), UTAH and lexical decomposition (Sections 4.1.1-4.1.2), morphology in syntax (Section 4.1.3), and cartography (Section 4.1.6).

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims of the chapter.

- The chapter does not mention DELPH-IN, the ERG, LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, or any treebank, so it gives no information on how implemented grammars resolve the flat-versus-binary and empty-element questions. Chapter 25 is the place to look.
- Dependency grammar and UD are not mentioned. Every UD consequence above is my inference.
- MRS is cited once, for scope underspecification. Semantic composition is not described.
- The HPSG analyses themselves (schemas, SLASH amalgamation, the coordination schema, the verb-movement analogue with its trace or unary rule) are not given; the chapter points to other chapters.
- The chapter does not show how the base-generation analysis of scrambling accounts for the scope facts it uses against trace-based analyses.
- Phenomena that usually motivate discontinuity or empty elements in HPSG (extraposition, ellipsis and gapping, partial VP fronting beyond the one remnant-movement example on pp. 1304-1305, argument drop) are not discussed here.
- Tokenization issues (clitics, contractions, multiword expressions) are not discussed; the only related remark is that some Minimalist parsers require pre-segmented input (p. 1257).
