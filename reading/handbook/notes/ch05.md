# Chapter 5: HPSG in understudied languages (Douglas L. Ball)

Handbook pages 177-216. Section and page references below are to the handbook pagination.

## Summary

The chapter surveys HPSG work on typologically diverse, small languages through three phenomena: argument indexing (affixal "pronouns" or agreement on the verb, with or without a co-occurring noun phrase), non-accusative alignment (mainly ergative case), and VSO constituent order. For each it presents the analyses that exist or could exist, and it stresses repeatedly that several analyses are viable and that the choice is unsettled. The common core across all three is the trio of lists SUBJ, COMPS and ARG-ST, which the author proposes as candidates for (near-)universality. The chapter has no bearing on English specifically and says almost nothing about implemented grammars (one footnote on the Grammar Matrix and CoreGram, one pointer to Drellishak 2009); its relevance is to the long-term multilingual goal, to the "relaxed" universal schemas, and to the UD bridge.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Flat, n-ary clause structure (Head-All-Valents Schema)

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules (and tree-encoding)
- Reference: Section 4.2, pp. 197-200; schema (14) p. 198; Figure 4 p. 199; footnote 29 p. 197
- What the chapter says: the "seemingly most common" HPSG analysis of VSO languages makes the verb, the subject and all complements sisters in one constituent, licensed by a schema whose non-head-daughters list has n members. The same schema is said to have "a long pedigree" for English polar questions: Schema 3 of Pollard & Sag (1994: 40), sai-ph of Ginzburg & Sag (2000: 36), aux-initial-cxt of Sag (2012: 188). Footnote 29 lists variants (head-subject-complements; all arguments as complements; a single VAL list).
- Why it matters: a tree encoding built for unary and binary nodes does not represent this analysis directly. A flat analysis also carries free post-verbal order by linear precedence constraints inside the one constituent (p. 199), so the order of the daughters is not fixed by the rule name.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter does give a binary alternative (caveat 2), and says that where there is no evidence for a verb-subject constituent, either analysis "would be possible, all else being equal" (p. 202).

### 2. Binary head-initial analysis: fixed order of combination, verb-subject constituent

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge (and tree-encoding)
- Reference: Section 4.3, pp. 200-202; schema (17) p. 200; Figure 5 p. 201; footnote 30 p. 200
- What the chapter says: a single binary Head-Valent Schema combines the head with the first element of its VAL list, so the verb combines with the subject first and the object last. The verb and subject form a constituent, and the structural prominence of subject and object is the reverse of the familiar one. With SUBJ and COMPS instead of one VAL list, the same configuration needs two rules.
- Why it matters: this analysis fits the binary assumption and is deterministic, but the constituent structure differs from the English-style one (verb-object constituent). A conversion to UD cannot read subject and object off the tree configuration; it has to use valence or ARG-ST information. The chapter makes the same point in its own terms: binding and subject-object asymmetries are computed on ARG-ST, not on tree configuration (pp. 199, 202).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Verb-movement analogue with a trace (DSL)

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 4.1, p. 196
- What the chapter says: one HPSG analogue of verb movement uses the DOUBLESLASH (DSL) feature to pass information about the initial verb down to "a trace, with semantic and syntactic structure, though no phonological realization". This has been explored for Germanic (English verb-initial clauses and all Germanic V2 languages, per Mueller 2021b, 2021c). The author knows of no serious application to any verb-initial language.
- Why it matters: this analysis needs a phonologically empty leaf, which the design excludes. It matters for German-style grammars more than for the VSO languages discussed here.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed in this chapter (the chapter refers to chapter 10, Mueller 2021b, Section 5.1).

### 4. Verb-initial order by order domains (word order domains / linearization)

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: continuity
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 196-197; also footnote 8 p. 182 (shuffle operator of Reape 1994 and Kathol 1995)
- What the chapter says: the second verb-movement-like analysis combines the verb with its complements at a low level and constrains it to be clause-initial at the clausal level, through constituent order domains. It was worked out for Welsh by Borsley (1989, 1995, 2009), who "time and again" ends up preferring a flat-structure analysis for basic Welsh clause structure. HPSG researchers have found no compelling evidence for a VP in particular VSO languages (Borsley 2006 for Welsh; Ball 2008 for Tongan).
- Why it matters: under this analysis the derivation tree is not a continuous tree over the token string. The chapter reports that the surface-oriented analyses (caveats 1 and 2) are preferred, which limits the threat.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Cross-indexes resolved by syntactic context (underspecification analysis)

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (and tree-encoding, UD-bridge)
- Reference: Section 2.2.1, pp. 185-186; descriptions (6)-(8)
- What the chapter says: a verb with an argument-indexing affix has one underspecified lexical description; the indexed ARG-ST member resolves to non-canonical when no co-occurring nominal is present (the argument is then realized by the affix alone) and to canonical when one is present. The author states that this part of the analysis "is not lexically mandated" and depends on the expressions in the clause. Haspelmath (2013) criticizes this "dual-nature" treatment.
- Why it matters: replay stays deterministic, since the tree fixes whether a head-argument rule applies. The lexical label by itself does not say whether the argument is expressed by a separate token. For the UD bridge, the same verb form sometimes has a nominal dependent for that argument and sometimes has none, while the HPSG analysis has the argument on ARG-ST in both cases.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed for implementations. Footnote 6 (p. 180) points to Saleem (2010), an argument-optionality library for the Grammar Matrix, as "similar, though not identical".

### 6. Arguments realized only as affixes (pro-indexes): arguments without tokens

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens (and semantics, UD-bridge)
- Reference: Section 2.1, pp. 180-184; Argument Realization Principle (2) p. 182; Principle of Canonicality (3) p. 183; lexical item (4) p. 183
- What the chapter says: following Miller & Sag (1997), affixal arguments are non-canonical members of ARG-ST that are not mapped to SUBJ or COMPS. A single word such as Macushi ikoneka'piuya "I made it" has empty valence lists and is a full clause; its two arguments exist only on ARG-ST and in the semantics.
- Why it matters: no empty element is needed, which fits the design. The consequences are (a) a single leaf can carry a complete predication, so the lexical-rule chain in the tag must encode all the argument affixes, and (b) the MRS contains arguments that have no token and no UD node. Point (a) is my inference from the analysis, not a statement of the chapter.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. "Direct syntax" analysis of Oneida: nominals not combined through valence

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: other (relaxed universal schemas), UD-bridge, semantics
- Reference: Section 2.2.2, pp. 186-188; Figure 2 p. 188; footnote 31 p. 202
- What the chapter says: Koenig & Michelson (2015) argue that in Oneida all arguments are (at best) non-canonical ARG-ST members, so "there is never any head-argument combinations in the syntax". A co-occurring nominal is licensed by index sharing with a member of a NONLOCAL feature DISLOC, as if it attached to an already saturated clause. Oneida is said not to require SUBJ and COMPS lists at all. Whether this is limited to the most polysynthetic languages is "an open question".
- Why it matters: a fallback inventory of coarse universal schemas built on head-subject and head-complement combination has nothing to apply in such a language, and a UD subject or object relation corresponds to a dislocation-like structure in the HPSG derivation.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Loosening the one-to-one use of valence ("resource sensitivity")

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics (and continuity)
- Reference: Section 2.2.3, pp. 188-189; lexical rule (11) p. 188; footnote 14 p. 185; footnote 17 p. 189
- What the chapter says: two unexplored routes for cross-indexes. One is a lexical rule that adds a canonical COMPS element sharing its index with a non-canonical ARG-ST member, so that one semantic argument corresponds to two syntactico-semantic elements; the author warns of possible "undesirable consequences" and of a needed revision of the Argument Realization Principle. The other is non-cancellation of arguments in the syntax, following Bender (2008) on Wambaya (proposed there for apparent discontinuous constituency), with like-minded proposals in Meurers (1999) and Mueller (2008).
- Why it matters: if one semantic argument is realized by two elements, the semantic composition must not introduce it twice. Non-cancellation changes what a head-argument rule does to the valence lists, which a replay engine and any universal schema set would have to follow.
- DELPH-IN stance: the chapter cites Bender (2008) only as a proposal for discontinuous constituency without shuffle operators; it does not say how it is implemented.

### 9. Morphology on one word that belongs to the next constituent (Coast Tsimshian)

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens (and UD-bridge)
- Reference: Section 4.2, pp. 199-200; example (16)
- What the chapter says: in Coast Tsimshian, case-marking connectives attach to one syntactic item but relate to the following constituent (the marker for "cat" is on the verb, the marker for "fish" is on "cat"). The author says this is much easier to analyze when the interacting elements are sisters in a flat structure (Ball 2011).
- Why it matters: the token boundaries and the syntactic grouping do not coincide, which complicates both the token-to-leaf mapping and the alignment with UD tokens. It also ties this phenomenon to the flat analysis of caveat 1.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 10. Case constraints that need morphological information (split ergativity)

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (lexical type inventory)
- Reference: Section 3.2, pp. 193-194; constraint (13) p. 193; footnote 24 p. 194
- What the chapter says: the ergative-absolutive pattern can be stated as a constraint on a transitive verb lexeme type tied to an actor-undergoer relation. In split systems the constraint "would almost assuredly need to reference information from the morphology", for example tense. The author says there is probably no one-size-fits-all solution for case linking.
- Why it matters: case frames are fixed in the lexical type plus inflection, which fits the design; the label space must then distinguish case frames that depend on the inflected form.
- DELPH-IN stance: the chapter points to Drellishak (2009) for analyses of each non-accusative alignment, including the hierarchical type (p. 190); that work extends the Grammar Matrix (title in the reference list).

## Support for the design

- Surface orientation. HPSG analysts mostly take VSO order "as is" and do not derive it from a covert SVO order (Section 4, p. 196; Section 4.1, p. 197). Both main VSO analyses are continuous trees without empty elements.
- No traces for affixal arguments. Non-canonical ARG-ST members handle affixal arguments without any empty leaf (Section 2.1, pp. 181-184). Footnote 10 (p. 183) adds that gaps on SUBJ lists are never combined by the Head-Subject Schema.
- Case licensing is plain unification, and "the geometry of clauses would, all else being equal, not differ based on alignment alone" (Section 3.1, pp. 191-193). Ergative languages need no special tree shapes.
- The binary Head-Valent Schema (Section 4.3, p. 200) is a single binary rule with a fixed order of combination, which suits both tagging and replay.
- The dependency-oriented core (SUBJ, COMPS, ARG-ST) is useful across all three phenomena and is proposed as a candidate for universality, with Oneida as the exception for SUBJ and COMPS (Section 5, p. 202, footnote 31). This supports a small set of coarse universal schemas and a dependency bridge.
- Grammatical relations and binding are read from ARG-ST, not from tree configuration (pp. 199, 202), so a UD conversion based on valence and ARG-ST is in line with the theory.
- Implemented multilingual grammar projects exist: the Grammar Matrix (Bender et al. 2010) starts from a common core and adds language-specific elements; CoreGram (Mueller 2015) tries to use the same data structures across languages; both produce computer-processable grammars (footnote 32, p. 203). Implemented grammars are also cited in Section 1 (p. 177): Spanish DELPH-IN grammar (Marimon 2013), Jacy (Siegel, Bender & Bond 2016), and TRALE grammars for Persian and Mandarin (reference list).

## Theory strains

- Cross-indexes (pro-drop) have no standard HPSG analysis (Section 2.2, p. 185). Competing options: underspecification (2.2.1), direct syntax (2.2.2), a doubling lexical rule, non-cancellation of arguments (2.2.3). Footnote 14 (p. 185) attributes the gap to the resource-sensitive way HPSG cancels valence. The author notes the last two options are not fully explored, "which might raise some questions as to their viability" (p. 188).
- The underspecification analysis treats one affix as two structural types (pro-index and agreement marker); Haspelmath (2013) criticizes this, and the author defends it only mildly (Section 2.2.1, p. 186). LFG uses a similar analysis.
- canonical/non-canonical typing differs across versions: non-canonical is a subtype of synsem in Ginzburg & Sag (2000) but is renamed covert and made a subtype of sign in SBCG (Sag 2012) (footnote 7, p. 182). The Argument Realization Principle as stated lets non-canonical elements appear on SUBJ and COMPS, and the Principle of Canonicality is needed to make that harmless (pp. 182-183).
- Valence feature geometry is unsettled: SUBJ plus COMPS, a single VAL list (Ball 2008, 2017), subjects as complements for finite verbs in Welsh (Borsley 1989, 1995) and German (Pollard 1996); SUBJ lists with more than one member for Danish (Mueller & Oersnes 2013) (footnote 9 p. 182; footnote 29 p. 197; footnote 30 p. 200).
- VSO clause structure: four analyses compete (DSL verb movement with a trace, order domains, flat structure, binary head-initial), and Borsley suggests different languages use different flat variants (Section 4, pp. 196-202). The author lists unanswered questions: why flat structures are licensed here and not elsewhere, and why the binary analysis must combine from least to most oblique (p. 202). Both are stipulations at present.
- Existence of VP in VSO languages: evidence is negative but not decisive ("it is difficult to argue that anything is clearly absent"), and Welsh has non-finite VPs while lacking evidence for finite ones (Section 4.1, p. 197, footnote 28).
- Case linking: no one-size-fits-all solution; one combined ergative-absolutive constraint versus separate constraints per case (Ball 2008) (Section 3.2, pp. 193-194, footnote 24). "There is a lot still to be done."
- Universality of SUBJ and COMPS is challenged by Oneida (footnote 31, p. 202).
- Framework-level: HPSG is less explored for typologically diverse languages than LFG (Section 1, p. 178). The author presents the multiplicity of viable analyses as an advantage (p. 179); for our purposes it means the theory does not determine one derivation shape per phenomenon.

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not statements of the chapter.

- The chapter does not say how any implemented grammar (ERG, Grammar Matrix grammars, TRALE grammars) actually analyzes VSO order, pro-drop or English subject-auxiliary inversion; whether those use binary or flat rules has to be checked in the grammars themselves.
- Semantic composition is not discussed. The feature structures use a RELS list and a KEY relation, but nothing is said about MRS for dropped or affixal arguments.
- Phenomena the chapter names but skips (p. 179): noun incorporation, serial verbs, clause chaining, evidentiality, object-initial order, applicatives. Noun incorporation and serial verbs seem the most relevant to the leaves-equal-tokens assumption and the UD bridge.
- Tokenization and morphological segmentation for polysynthetic languages, and the size of the resulting label space, are not addressed.
- Discontinuous constituents in non-configurational languages are mentioned only in passing (Bender 2008 on Wambaya; Donohue & Sag 1999 on Warlpiri in footnote 3).
