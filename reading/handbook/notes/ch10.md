# Chapter 10: Constituent order (Stefan Müller)

Handbook pages 369-417 (text 369-404, references 404-417). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter surveys the tools HPSG has used for local word order variation: the separation of immediate dominance from linear precedence (ID/LP), flat versus binary branching structures, the different mapping from ARG-ST to valence features in SVO and SOV languages, "head movement" through an empty verbal head and the DSL feature, flat constructional analyses of auxiliary-initial clauses, and Reape-style constituent order domains that allow discontinuous constituents. English is treated briefly (fixed order, auxiliary inversion); German carries most of the argument, with Warlpiri as the extreme case. Müller argues that domain-based analyses of German run into spurious ambiguities and cannot handle apparent multiple fronting, and that binary branching with continuous constituents plus head movement is the better analysis. The last section shows, following Bender (2008), that even Australian free-order languages can be analyzed with continuous constituents by not canceling arguments from valence lists.

The chapter is theoretical. Its body never mentions DELPH-IN, the LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, or any parser; the single occurrence of "implemented" (p. 398) refers to a proposal being realized in a schema. Every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is therefore "not discussed", with the nearest relevant statement added where there is one. Bearing on the parser is high: the chapter is in effect a catalog of the alternatives to the design's assumptions, and of what each alternative costs.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. The continuous analysis of verb placement relies on an empty verbal head

- Severity: high (for German and the other Germanic V2 languages; for English only if the head-movement analysis of auxiliary inversion is chosen)
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements / leaves-equal-tokens
- Reference: Section 5.1, pp. 384-386 (Figure 5, entry (29)); Section 3, p. 371 (example (5)); Section 8, pp. 403-404
- What the chapter says: With binary branching, only sisters can be ordered, so local reordering cannot put the verb in clause-initial position (5). Binary analyses are therefore "usually paired with HPSG's analogue of what is head-movement". The analysis (Borsley 1989, building on Jacobson 1987) has three parts: a unary rule that turns the verb in its canonical form into a verb selecting a clause from which that verb is missing; a head feature DSL (DOUBLE SLASH) that carries the missing verb's LOCAL value down the head path; and an empty lexical element with PHON < > whose LOCAL value equals its own DSL value ("I am a verb that is missing itself"). Nothing moves; the empty element "is a normal element in the lexicon". The chapter lists this as the majority analysis for German (Kiss and Wesche 1991, Kiss 1995, Meurers 2000, Müller 2005a, 2021b, and others), and as the analysis of Bouma and van Noord (1998) for Dutch, Müller and Ørsnes (2015) for Danish, and Müller (2021c) for Germanic in general.
- Why it matters: the analysis the chapter recommends in order to keep constituents continuous violates the no-empty-elements assumption instead. A tree with a leaf that has no token cannot be decoded from one label per token unless the trace is folded into a neighboring token's label or compiled into a unary rule. The design gets continuity or tracelessness for free in the mainstream German analyses, never both.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter offers the flat constructional analysis (caveat 2) and domain-based linearization (caveat 3) as the trace-free alternatives and does not say what any implemented grammar does.

### 2. Flat structures: rules with more than two daughters

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3, pp. 375-377 (schema (14), Figure 1); Section 4, p. 382 (Figure 4); Section 5.2, pp. 386-387 (Figure 6, footnote 10)
- What the chapter says: "Researchers working on English and French usually assume a flat structure" (Pollard and Sag 1994: 39-40, 362; Sag 1997: 479; Ginzburg and Sag 2000: 34; Abeillé and Godard 2000): one Head-Complement Schema realizes all complements at once. The constructional analysis of English auxiliary-initial clauses (Sag et al. 2020: 117) combines the auxiliary with its subject and its VP complement "in one go", a ternary local tree. The chapter adds that binary branching "would be possible as well", as Categorial Grammar shows, and gives a binary schema (19) that covers English by always taking the first element of COMPS.
- Why it matters: the theoretical literature on English that the project plans to read next mostly assumes n-ary local trees. The tree encoding and the replay engine assume unary and binary rules, so analyses from that literature need to be binarized before they can be compared with a derivation. The chapter confirms this is possible but is not what the sources do.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The closest statement is that the binary schema (19) can be "parameterized" for fixed-order head-initial languages such as English (p. 379).

### 3. Constituent order domains: discontinuous constituents

- Severity: high for the multi-language goal; low for English
- Assumption affected: continuity / tree-encoding / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 6, pp. 390-397 (constraints (34), (37), (39); Figures 9-12)
- What the chapter says: Reape (1991, 1992, 1994) adds a list DOM that holds the daughters in pronunciation order. The mother's DOM is the shuffle of the daughters' domain contributions (34), and PHON is read off DOM (37), so the tree gives the order of combination and "does not correspond to the linearization of the DOMAIN objects" (Figure 9). Three variants are shown: whole daughters inserted (German scrambling), all domain objects of a daughter liberated into the mother's domain (Warlpiri split NPs, Donohue and Sag 1999, (39)), and partial compaction (German relative clause extraposition, Kathol and Pollard 1995), which needs "a complex relational constraint". Although the chapter argues against domains for German clause structure, it states that they "still play a role in analyzing ellipsis" and coordination (Yatabe 2001; Crysmann 2003; Beavers and Sag 2004; Yatabe and Tam 2021), and are used for French subject-verb inversion (Bonami, Godard and Marandin 1999) and French sentential adverbs (Bonami and Godard 2007).
- Why it matters: under these analyses a derivation tree is not a continuous bracketing of the token string, so a tree encoding built on spans cannot represent it, and the tree does not fix the surface order: shuffle is a disjunction (six results for two two-element lists, (35)), so replay would have to search over orders or be told the order separately. The cases where domains are said to survive (ellipsis, non-constituent coordination) occur in English too.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. Section 7 presents Bender (2008) on Wambaya as showing that free-order languages "can be handled without discontinuous constituents", and footnote 14 says the "more common analysis of extraposition" is a SLASH-like nonlocal feature EXTRA.

### 4. ID/LP: the schema does not fix daughter order, and PHON is not plain concatenation

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / continuity
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 371-375 (rules (7), (12), structures (13)); Section 3, pp. 376-377 (principle (16))
- What the chapter says: HPSG schemata "do not express information about ordering"; separate LP rules do. One Head-Adjunct Schema covers pre- and post-head adjuncts, with order decided by LP rules over a head feature PRE-MODIFIER (12); verb position is decided by LP rules over INITIAL (7). Every sign has its own PHON, and the Constituent Order Principle of Pollard and Sag (1987: 168) computes it with a function order-constituents over the daughters. For English that function "would just return a concatenation"; for other languages "it would be much more complicated", and "this function and its interaction with linear precedence constraints was never worked out in detail".
- Why it matters: in this format a rule name does not say which daughter comes first; order follows from LP constraints checked against feature values. For the derivation tree to be a deterministic recipe, each ordered variant has to become its own named rule (head-initial and head-final versions), which is the duplication the ID/LP format was designed to avoid. A grammar written in strict ID/LP style cannot be replayed without compiling LP rules into rule variants.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. The binary Head-Complement Schema may pick any element of COMPS

- Severity: medium for scrambling languages; none for English
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3, pp. 379-380 (schema (19), Figure 3); Section 6.4.1, p. 398
- What the chapter says: Schema (19) splits the head daughter's COMPS into three lists with two appends and realizes "an arbitrary element" as the non-head daughter. Fixed-order languages restrict it (head-final: last element; head-initial such as English: first element). The alternative is set-valued valence, which the chapter rejects because case assignment and binding need the order.
- Why it matters: one rule name stands for several possible unifications. Replay stays search-free only if the non-head daughter's features leave exactly one COMPS element compatible; with case syncretism or two complements of the same category this is not guaranteed, and then either the label must carry the position of the saturated complement or the engine must try alternatives. The schema is also stated with a relational constraint (see caveat 6).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Relational constraints are used throughout

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: other (the feature-structure engine: replay by unification alone)
- Reference: Section 2, p. 375 (append, footnote 2); Section 3, pp. 376-379 (synsem2signs, order-constituents, append in (19)); Section 6.1, p. 391 (shuffle); Section 6.3, p. 396 (compaction); Section 6.4.1, p. 398 (shuffle inside a valence list, (43))
- What the chapter says: The schemata are written with append, shuffle, synsem2signs, order-constituents, and compaction. Flat analyses with unboundedly many adjuncts "involve relational constraints in schemata or in lexical items or an infinite lexicon"; in Kasper's (1994) analysis the constraints "walk through lists of daughters of unbounded length in order to compute the semantics". Footnote 4 notes that the binary schema and SBCG do not need synsem2signs.
- Why it matters: a small engine that applies rules by plain unification cannot evaluate these relations. Append over fixed positions can be encoded with difference lists or fixed list positions; shuffle and compaction are nondeterministic and cannot.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter treats the relational formulation as the default and only remarks where a constraint becomes unnecessary (footnote 4).

### 7. Clause-type semantics may come from implicational constraints on the configuration, and may depend on intonation

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 5.2, pp. 386-387; Section 5.3, pp. 388-390 (Figures 7-8, footnote 12)
- What the chapter says: Auxiliary-initial clauses in English have "at least five different meanings" (polar interrogative, blessings/curses, negative imperative, exclamative, conditional); German V1 and V2 clauses have seven uses between them, and three of the V1 examples "contain the same words but differ in intonation". Three ways to state the meanings are given: subtypes of a phrasal construction (aux-initial-cxt with polar-int-cl, aux-initial-excl-cl; Fillmore 1999, Sag et al. 2020), separate lexical items or empty heads, and Müller's (2015b) mixed approach, in which one lexical rule output has underspecified semantics and implicational constraints that refer to the surrounding structure (for example a question pronoun in the left daughter, or intonation) fix the meaning. Footnote 12 adds that with coordinated verb-initial conjuncts the semantics "is determined at the top-most level", so information about verb-initial combination, mood, and intonation must be passed up to the coordinate structure; and that Ginzburg and Sag (2000: 266-267) instead add clause-type semantics with a unary rule over the complete clause.
- Why it matters: (a) under the phrasal option, "Are they crazy?" and "Are they crazy!" have the same string and the same tree shape and differ only in the construction subtype, so the tagger must choose the rule name from punctuation or context, and replay cannot detect a wrong choice. (b) Under the mixed option, the semantics of a node is completed by a constraint that looks at configuration elsewhere in the tree, so MRS is not a by-product of applying the named rules bottom-up. (c) Intonation is not in the token string.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The clause-level unary rule of Ginzburg and Sag (2000) is the option in the chapter that fits the replay design best.

### 8. Apparent multiple fronting needs an empty head inside the prefield and several traces

- Severity: medium for German; none for English
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements / leaves-equal-tokens / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 6.4.2, pp. 398-401 (examples (46)-(49))
- What the chapter says: German allows several constituents before the finite verb, provided they depend on the same verb; they can even instantiate a right sentence bracket plus a postfield ("Los damit geht es ..."). Müller (2005b, 2021b) analyzes the fronted material as a VP headed by an empty verb, licensed by a lexical rule that is "a special case of the head-movement rule". Sketch (49) contains three empty positions in one clause: the empty verbal head in the prefield, the trace of the fronted VP, and the trace of the finite verb.
- Why it matters: this is the worst case for folding empty elements into token labels: three tokenless leaves, two of them adjacent at the end of the clause.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The only trace-free competitor mentioned is Wetta's (2011) compaction of the fronted items into one domain object, which the chapter rejects (see Theory strains).

### 9. Split noun phrases without discontinuity: the parts are not one constituent

- Severity: medium for the multi-language goal
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / semantics
- Reference: Section 7, pp. 402-403 (Figure 13); data in Section 6.2, p. 394 (example (38))
- What the chapter says: In Bender's (2008) non-cancellation analysis, saturated arguments stay on the valence list, marked as satisfied. A stranded nominal ("small-DU-ERG") attaches as an adjunct to a verbal projection, and a special schema identifies its MOD value with an element of the valence list, which gives it the argument's index and case. Example (38) is glossed with two readings ("Two small children are chasing the dog" or "Two children are chasing the dog and they are small"); the chapter does not say how the analyses separate them.
- Why it matters: the tree is continuous, which supports the design, but the two parts of what UD treats as one nominal never form a constituent. The head of the modifier cannot be read off the tree; the bridge has to follow the MOD-to-valence identity in the feature structure. In the domain analysis (Figure 11) the two parts do form an NP, so the HPSG-to-UD conversion differs by analysis.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. Bender (2008) is cited as an analysis of Wambaya; the chapter does not say whether it is implemented.

### 10. Cross-linguistic variation in what counts as subject, complement, and adjunct

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 4, pp. 380-383 (mapping (24), footnotes 6-7); Section 3, pp. 378-379
- What the chapter says: English maps the first ARG-ST element to SPR (Müller's choice; "non-standard", the usual feature is SUBJ) and the rest to COMPS. For German finite verbs "it is standardly assumed" that the subject is on COMPS, so there is no VP and no subject rule. The level at which the languages agree is ARG-ST, which is used for linking, case, and binding. Separately, three of the flat analyses of adjuncts (van Noord and Bouma 1994; Abeillé and Godard 2000; Bouma, Malouf and Sag 2001) treat some adjuncts as valents.
- Why it matters: a bridge that reads nsubj off a head-specifier or head-subject rule works for English and fails for German, where every argument enters through the same head-complement rule; the relation must come from ARG-ST position or case. Under adjuncts-as-valents analyses the rule name does not separate obj/obl from advmod either.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 11. Spurious ambiguity: several derivations for one string and one meaning

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (a unique gold tree per analysis)
- Reference: Section 4, p. 382 (footnote 7); Section 6.4.1, pp. 397-398
- What the chapter says: Relaxing the Head-Specifier Schema so that subjects attach to non-maximal projections would give "das Bild von Kim" two analyses; "this kind of spurious ambiguity is usually avoided". Kathol's (2000) grammar, after shuffling the objects on the valence list to allow partial fronting, gives every Mittelfeld order two analyses: "freedom in linearization and freedom in the order of combination".
- Why it matters: where a grammar has spurious ambiguity, the treebank must fix one canonical derivation or the tagger is trained on arbitrary variation. The chapter treats avoiding it as a design criterion, which favors the design.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 12. Violable and weight-based ordering constraints

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (strict versus relaxed licensing)
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 373-374
- What the chapter says: Uszkoreit's constraints on the order of arguments in the German Mittelfeld "are assumed to be violable", and he uses weighted constraints. "While violable constraints are not part of the standard HPSG formalism, this is something desirable and something that is worked on." Abeillé and Godard use a three-valued WEIGHT feature (light, middle-weight, heavy) for French ordering.
- Why it matters: a strict replay either enforces such preferences as hard constraints (rejecting marked but grammatical orders) or omits them. For parsing, omitting them is harmless; it matters only if the grammar is later used to rank or generate.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- Continuous constituents suffice, according to the chapter's own conclusion. Section 8 (pp. 403-404): there are "reasons for assuming head-movement for German", and "even languages with extremely free constituent order can be analyzed without assuming discontinuous constituents" (Section 7, Bender 2008). The price is the empty head (caveat 1) and non-cancellation (caveat 9).
- Binary branching is the usual assumption for German and is available for English. Section 3 (pp. 377-379): binary structures with adjuncts attaching to any verbal projection handle free adjunct placement and unbounded adjunct recursion without relational constraints over daughter lists and without an infinite lexicon; binary schema (19) covers English by taking the first COMPS element; footnote 4 notes that the binary schema needs no synsem2signs relation.
- English is the easy case. Section 1 (p. 369): the order of subject, verb, and object is fixed; Section 3 (p. 377): for English, order-constituents "would just return a concatenation of the PHON values of the daughters".
- Head movement is local feature passing, not a transformation. Section 5.1 (p. 385) and Section 8 (p. 403): it is "a series of local dependencies", with DSL a head feature passed along the projection path. A replay engine handles this by ordinary unification once the tree, including the empty leaf, is given.
- Unary rules and lexical rules are standard carriers of both structure and meaning. Section 5.1 (p. 385): the verb-initial variant is licensed by a unary rule; Section 5.3 (p. 389, Figure 7): a unary projection can add the construction meaning that a phrasal construction would add; footnote 12: Ginzburg and Sag (2000) add clause-type semantics by a unary rule over the complete clause. This matches label chains of unary rules.
- A trace-free analysis of English auxiliary inversion exists and is recent mainstream. Section 5.2 (pp. 386-387): the constructional analysis of Sag et al. (2020) uses no empty element; it needs only binarization (caveat 2).
- Extraposition and fronting are treated by most HPSG work as nonlocal feature passing (SLASH, EXTRA), which keeps trees continuous. Footnote 1 (p. 372) and footnote 14 (p. 395).
- ARG-ST gives a language-independent layer for grammatical relations. Section 4 (p. 383): ditransitives in German and English have the same three NPs on ARG-ST, linked the same way to the semantics. This is a natural anchor for the UD bridge and for the "coarse universal schemas" of the relaxed output.
- One schema with parameters covers many languages. Section 3 (p. 379): schema (19) is parameterized for free order, fixed head-final, and fixed head-initial languages; Section 2 (p. 374): one Head-Adjunct Schema for both adjunct orders. This supports a small universal schema inventory for the relaxed fallback.
- The chapter states that total models rule out leaving a category unspecified (p. 401), since that would make sentences "infinitely ambiguous". This supports keeping the relaxed output formally separate from the strict, grammar-licensed output.

## Theory strains

1. Flat versus binary branching (Section 3, pp. 375-380). English and French work assumes flat structures, German work binary ones (with Reape 1994 and Bouma and van Noord 1998 as exceptions); nothing in the theory decides. Flat structures with free adjuncts need relational constraints over unbounded lists (Kasper 1994) or adjuncts as valents, "which may be problematic because of scope issues" (pointer to Levine and Hukari 2006, Chaves 2009).
2. The Constituent Order Principle was never worked out (Section 3, p. 377). The function order-constituents "and its interaction with linear precedence constraints was never worked out in detail": a founding principle of Pollard and Sag (1987) that remained a promissory note.
3. Violable constraints lie outside the formalism (Section 2, p. 373). The Mittelfeld ordering constraints are violable and weighted, which standard HPSG cannot state; the chapter calls this "desirable" and work in progress.
4. Lists versus sets for valence (Section 3, p. 380). Sets give scrambling for free (Gunji 1986; Hinrichs and Nakazawa 1989; Pollard 1996; Oliva 1992; Engelkamp, Erbach and Uszkoreit 1992) but lose the order needed for case and binding; lists keep the order but need the double append in (19).
5. Subject representation (Section 4, pp. 381-383, footnotes 6-7). SPR for subjects is "non-standard in HPSG"; SUBJ is usual; German subjects go on COMPS. The same argument sits on different valence lists in different languages and in different authors' grammars, and the unifying level is ARG-ST. Letting subjects combine with non-maximal projections instead would create spurious ambiguity in NPs.
6. Verb placement: head movement, linearization, or flat construction (Section 5, pp. 383-390). Three live analyses. Head movement needs an empty element and a dedicated feature (DSL); the constructional approach multiplies phrasal types (five for English auxiliary-initial clauses); the lexical approach would need seven fronted-verb entries for German. Müller's mixed approach uses underspecified semantics plus implicational constraints on the surrounding configuration. Müller reports assuming linearization domains "for ten years" before switching (footnote 11).
7. Where construction meaning lives (Sections 5.2-5.3, pp. 386-390). Phrasal type hierarchy (Sag 1997; Fillmore 1999; Sag et al. 2020; SBCG), lexical items or empty heads, or implicational constraints. The chapter also uses this against Minimalism: Chomsky (2010) calls the Merge-based analysis simpler, and Müller replies that the meanings must be stated somewhere, so Minimalism needs as many lexical items or empty functional heads; he calls Rizzi-style cartography "Crypto-Constructional".
8. Order domains (Section 6, pp. 390-401). Reape's original motivation, scrambling in verbal complexes, "was superseded" by Hinrichs and Nakazawa's argument attraction, because pure linearization cannot account for agreement and the remote passive. Kathol's (2000) repair for partial fronting creates spurious ambiguities. Kathol's (2001) surface-based definition of clause types by topological fields is contradicted by multiple fronting; Section 5.3 calls such approaches "doomed to failure". Yet domains remain in use for ellipsis, coordination, and French.
9. Apparent multiple fronting (Section 6.4.2, pp. 399-401). Müller (2002b) used an empty verbal head inside a domain grammar and concedes it "comes as a stipulation, since its only purpose in the grammar of German was to account for apparent multiple frontings". Wetta (2011) compacts the fronted items into one domain object, which "begs the question of what kind of object it is"; total models (King 1999) forbid leaving its category unspecified. Müller judges a relational constraint that builds a verbal projection "less principled than positing an empty element". The dispute is, at bottom, empty element versus relational constraint.
10. Extraposition (Section 6.3 and footnote 14, pp. 395-397). Four analyses coexist: partial compaction in domains (Kathol and Pollard 1995), the EXTRA nonlocal feature (Keller 1995; Müller 1999), projection of semantic indices (Kiss 2005), and a mix of index and EXTRA projection (Crysmann 2013).
11. Free word order: liberation versus non-cancellation (Sections 6.2 and 7, pp. 394-395, 402-403). Donohue and Sag (1999) liberate domain objects; Bender (2008) keeps saturated arguments on valence lists. Non-cancellation changes what saturation means, and is shared with earlier proposals for other phenomena (Meurers 1999; Przepiórkowski 1999; Müller 2008).
12. Empty elements are accepted (Sections 5.1 and 6.4.2). The chapter treats an empty lexical head as a legitimate, even preferred, device. This is at odds with the surface-oriented, trace-free practice that the project assumes of DELPH-IN grammars (the comparison is ours; the chapter does not make it).

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter says nothing about how any implemented grammar (ERG, GG, the Grammar Matrix, TRALE grammars, Enju) handles the phenomena it discusses. In particular it does not say how the ERG analyzes auxiliary inversion, or whether implemented German grammars use a verb trace. This needs to be checked in the grammars themselves.
- Bender (2008) is, to my knowledge, tied to an implemented Wambaya grammar; the chapter does not say so, and I have not verified it here.
- V2 fronting, long-distance extraction, and extraposition are explicitly deferred to Chapter 13 (Borsley and Crysmann). Whether the SLASH-based analyses there use traces or trace-free lexical/unary mechanisms is the decisive question for the no-empty-elements assumption and is not answered in this chapter.
- Verbal complexes and argument attraction (Hinrichs and Nakazawa) are mentioned only in passing; Chapter 11 (Godard and Samvelian) is the relevant chapter.
- The chapter does not discuss English phenomena where order and constituency come apart inside the clause (particle verbs, heavy NP shift, adverb placement between verb and complement), nor clitics or other cases where tokens and syntactic leaves differ.
- Nothing is said about processing cost, parsing algorithms, or how an empty head or a shuffle relation is handled by a parser, although Reape 1991 has "parsing" in its title.
- The two readings glossed for Warlpiri example (38) (restrictive versus secondary-predicate-like) are not derived by either analysis as presented; whether Bender's analysis produces both is left open in the chapter.
