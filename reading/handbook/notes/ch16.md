# Chapter 16: Coordination (Anne Abeillé and Rui P. Chaves)

Handbook pages 725-776 (text 725-765, references 765-776). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter surveys HPSG work on coordination, with the focus on morphosyntax. It presents the two ideas that most HPSG analyses share: coordinate structures are non-headed phrases, and the parallelism between coordinands is captured by feature sharing. It then goes through the status of the coordinator (marker or weak head), binary versus flat structure, correlative coordination and comparative correlatives, feature sharing and its mispredictions, agreement resolution and Closest Conjunct Agreement, lexical coordination, coordination of unlike categories, and non-constituent coordination (Argument Cluster Coordination, Right-Node Raising, Gapping). The authors state twice (pp. 726, 765) that there is "no clear consensus, even within HPSG" on the coordinator's status, on branching, on the locality of agreement, and on whether non-constituent coordination involves deletion.

The chapter is theoretical. Its only reference to implementation is one clause on p. 729: Drellishak and Bender (2005) are cited "for more discussion about how to accommodate such typological patterns in a computational HPSG platform". It never mentions the ERG, the LKB, ACE, TRALE, or Enju. Most "DELPH-IN stance" entries below are therefore "not discussed", with the nearest relevant statement added. Bearing on the parser is high: coordination is the place where the chapter's own analyses most often give up the equation of leaves with tokens, the concatenation of daughters' phonology, and identity of category between mother and daughters.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Argument Cluster Coordination as left-periphery ellipsis: the mother's PHON is shorter than the concatenation of its daughters

- Severity: high
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / tree-encoding / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 7, pp. 756-757 (constraint (71), Figure 11, example (72)); also Section 6, pp. 754-755 (example (67))
- What the chapter says: In the elliptical approach (Yatabe 2001; Crysmann 2004; Beavers and Sag 2004), "Tom gave a book to Mary, and a magazine to Sue" is a coordination of two full VPs. Constraint (71) on coord-phrase lets phonological material at the left edge of the non-initial coordinand be absent from the mother if it is identical to material at the left edge of the initial coordinand: the daughters have PHON 1 + 2 and 3 + 1 + 4, the mother has 1 + 2 + 3 + 4. "If 1 is resolved as the empty list then no ellipsis occurs, but if 1 is non-empty then ellipsis occurs." Several of these accounts "operate on linearization domain elements instead of directly on PHON". The approach is motivated by ambiguity: in (72a), "Two trees were cut down by Robin in July and by Alex in September", the ellipsis must include the subject (otherwise the same trees are cut down twice), while in (72b) it can cover the verb alone.
- Why it matters: in Figure 11 the second VP contains a word ("give") that has no token in the input. The derivation tree is not a bracketing of the token string, so one label per token cannot encode it unless the elided words are folded into the labels of neighbors. Replay is not deterministic either: the rule name does not say how long the shared prefix 1 is, and (72) shows that different lengths give different meanings for the same string. The amount of elided material would have to be part of the label.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter offers Mouret's (2006) non-elliptical cluster analysis as the alternative (see Support for the design).

### 2. Right-Node Raising as deletion under phonological identity

- Severity: high
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / tree-encoding / deterministic-replay / other (PHON is prosodically structured)
- Reference: Section 7, pp. 760-763 (examples (78), (79), (81)-(84), rule (80), Figure 13)
- What the chapter says: A "syntactic and non-elliptical account of Right-Node Raising is harder to maintain" because the phenomenon "does not seem to be sensitive to syntactic structure": the shared material can be missing from inside relative clauses, embedded questions, and adjunct clauses (78), and it can be part of a word ("the sole or co-author", "neither un- nor overly patriotic", "the neuro- and cognitive sciences", (79)). The account of Chaves (2014) and Shiraïshi et al. (2019), rule (80), is a unary rule over a list-valued MORPHO-PHONOLOGY feature: a sublist is dropped from the mother if an identical sublist (same PHON and same LEXICAL-ID) occurs later. PHON is "not simply a list of phonemes, but rather a structured list containing intonational phrases, phonological phrases, prosodic words, syllables, and segments"; only independent prosodic units can be shared (81). LID identity rules out sharing of homophones with different meanings (82) but does not require coreference ("Chris likes and Bill loves his bike" stays ambiguous). The rule "can in principle apply to any phrasal node, including non-coordinate cases" (83). When the final sublist R3 is non-empty the result is discontinuous Right-Node Raising, where the shared phrase is followed by further material ("occupied and burned the town to the ground", (84)).
- Why it matters: (a) the daughter of the unary rule is a full sentence in which the shared phrase occurs twice, so again the tree has leaves without tokens; (b) the rule is a relational constraint that splits a list into five sublists, with a choice of what is deleted; (c) it needs a prosodic representation that the token string does not provide; (d) sharing of word parts breaks the equation of tokens with lexical leaves unless the tokenizer splits prefixes and compound parts; (e) the phenomenon is not confined to coordination, so it cannot be handled inside a coordination rule alone.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter does not present a non-elliptical HPSG analysis of Right-Node Raising; it only notes that Steedman and Dowty claim syntactic boundedness and that Phillips (1996) and Chaves (2014) rebut them (footnote 28, p. 760).

### 3. Gapping: the meaning of the gapped clause is recovered from context

- Severity: high for MRS as a by-product; medium for the tree encoding
- Assumption affected: semantics / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 7, pp. 763-764 (Figure 14, example (85))
- What the chapter says: For "Robin likes Sam and Tim Sue" there are elliptical accounts (Chaves 2006) and "direct-interpretation accounts where the missing material is recovered from the preceding linguistic context" (Mouret 2006; Abeillé et al. 2014; Park 2019). In Figure 14 the second coordinand is an S whose daughters are two NPs; the Question Under Discussion of the first clause (an abstraction over the verb's arguments) is shared across the clausal daughters, which lets the second clause "combine the two NPs with the verbal semantics, and recover the propositional meaning". Gapping "is not restricted to coordinate structures": attested examples (85) occur in comparatives and in clauses with "while", "rather than", and "before".
- Why it matters: under direct interpretation the tree is continuous and has no empty leaf, which suits the tree encoding. The semantics does not come from the rules applied inside the gapped clause, though: it needs a contextual feature (QUD) that is computed from the first clause and shared with the second. A replay engine gets the right MRS only if the grammar encodes QUD and its abstraction step; otherwise the gapped clause has no predicate. The non-coordinate cases mean the fragment-clause rule must be available outside coordination, which widens the tagger's choices.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Feature sharing between coordinands is too strong for attested data, and for unlike categories the mother's category is not a daughter's

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (strict replay fails on grammatical input) / other (the engine needs underspecified or disjunctive categories)
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 741-745 (examples (33)-(36), (41)-(44), Figure 9); Section 6, pp. 753-756 (examples (64)-(69))
- What the chapter says: Constraint (11) shares the whole CAT value among the coordinands and the mother. That predicts many facts (valence, VFORM, PFORM, PRD, CASE) but also mispredicts. Inverted and non-inverted clauses coordinate in attested examples (35); AUX-mismatched VPs coordinate freely (36), which is a problem unless the AUX analysis of Sag et al. (2020) is adopted; SLASH identity "incorrectly rules out" asymmetric extraction ("Who did Sam pick up the phone and call?", (42)). The authors conclude that the construction "should impose identity of some of the features in CAT, though not all, despite the fact that one of the prime motivations for CAT was coordination phenomena". Coordination of unlike categories ("Kim is alone and without money", "Pat is a Republican and proud of it", (64)) shows that the mother's features "are not simply the intersection of the features of the coordinands" (66). Four approaches are listed: disjunctive categories imported from Type-Logical Grammar (Daniels 2002; Yatabe 2004), type underspecification (Sag 2003), gradient parts of speech (Chaves 2013), and left-periphery ellipsis (Crysmann 2001; Yatabe 2003; Beavers and Sag 2004; Chaves 2006). The authors judge that ellipsis "does not offer a complete account" and that "underspecification accounts are more promising".
- Why it matters: a grammar that follows (11) literally will reject attested sentences on replay, sending them to the relaxed output. A grammar that follows the underspecification route needs a richer type hierarchy for HEAD values and a coordination rule whose mother is more general than each daughter, which is not what plain unification of daughters with a shared tag produces. Which features the rule shares is a grammar-specific decision that the chapter leaves open.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. For case syncretism in across-the-board extraction the chapter gives a type-hierarchy solution (Levine et al. 2001; Figure 9, p. 745) that needs only ordinary typed unification.

### 5. Flat coordinate structures with any number of daughters

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 728-729; Section 3, pp. 729-732 (Figure 1, rules (7)-(9), constraints (11), (12))
- What the chapter says: Three structures are in use (Figure 1). Binary branching is used by Pollard and Sag (1994: 200-205), Yatabe (2003), Crysmann (2008), Beavers and Sag (2004), Drellishak and Bender (2005), and Chaves (2007, 2012b). The flat structure in which the coordinator forms a constituent with its coordinand is used by Abeillé (2005), Abeillé, Bonami et al. (2006), Mouret (2005, 2006), and Bîlbîie (2017). The totally flat structure is used "much less frequently" (Sag et al. 2003; Sag 2003). The binary analysis "requires two different rules" and "a special feature to prevent the coordinator from recursively applying" (*"Robin and and and Kim"); the totally flat one needs one rule and no special feature. The authors side with Chomsky (1965) that there is "no grammatical motivation for any internal structure" and conclude that "the simplest and most parsimonious structure for coordination is neither left- nor right-recursive". Their own formalization (11)-(12) uses a DTRS list of unbounded length with parametric lists (Pollard and Sag 1994: 396, fn. 2).
- Why it matters: the tree encoding and the replay engine assume unary and binary rules. The chapter's preferred analysis is n-ary with a list constraint over all daughters. A binary grammar is a recognized option, at the cost of two rules and a bookkeeping feature, and it imposes a right- or left-branching structure that the authors regard as unmotivated; treebank and UD conversions must then fix one direction by convention.
- DELPH-IN stance: Drellishak and Bender (2005) are listed among the binary-branching analyses and are the one work described as targeting "a computational HPSG platform" (p. 729). Nothing else is said.

### 6. Coordination without a coordinator token: asyndeton and bound-morpheme coordinators

- Severity: low for English; medium for the multi-language goal
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3, pp. 729-732 (patterns (6), example (10), constraint (12c))
- What the chapter says: Languages use asyndeton ("We came, we saw, we conquered"), monosyndeton, polysyndeton, and omnisyndeton. In some languages the coordinator is a bound morpheme: suffixes on one coordinand in Abelam, a conjunctive affix on the first verb in Kanuri, lengthening of the final vowel of each proper name in Telugu (10). Different strategies may apply to different categories (Japanese "to" for nominals, "te" elsewhere). The chapter handles asyndeton with a subtype asyndetic-coord-phrase whose daughters are all [COORD none] (12c), with no null coordinator. The introduction (p. 726) lists "the existence of null coordinators" among the debated questions.
- Why it matters: when the coordinator is an affix or a prosodic change, the coordination marking must be a lexical rule in the token's unary chain, and the tagger must predict it from morphology. Asyndetic coordination must be told apart from other juxtapositions by the tagger alone, since no token signals it.
- DELPH-IN stance: Drellishak and Bender (2005: 111) are the source of the Abelam, Kanuri, and Telugu data, and are cited for accommodating the typology computationally; the chapter does not describe their solution.

### 7. Lexical coordination, "light" phrases, and coordinators that inherit valence

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (canonical tree) / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 750-753 (examples (58)-(63), Figure 10); Section 3.1, pp. 733-736 (entries (13), (16))
- What the chapter says: Abeillé (2006) argues that words can be coordinated as words: "a big and tall man" (where a phrasal AP is excluded), "turning on and off the radio", French "le ou la responsable", "un film de et avec Woody Allen". In Figure 10 "likes and approves" is a V0 that keeps the SUBJ and COMPS requirements of both verbs and then takes one object. The coordinate word-level unit "must be a phrase, but it also behaves as a word"; Abeillé resolves the paradox with [WEIGHT light] phrases. Under the weak-head analysis the coordinator "inherits the complements expected by the coordinand" by concatenating COMPS lists, as in complex predicates. "Some apparent cases of lexical coordination may be analyzed as Right-Node Raising" (Beavers and Sag 2004); the two are said to differ in prosody and meaning (contrast, a prosodic boundary at the ellipsis site, same-event readings).
- Why it matters: "Kim likes and approves the plan" has at least two candidate derivations (lexical V coordination, or Right-Node Raising over two VPs or clauses), distinguished by prosody and event structure, which the token string does not show. The treebank needs one canonical choice. For the UD bridge, lexical coordination means a single object is the complement of a coordinate head, so both verbs' argument links must be read from shared valence in the feature structure; the tree gives the object only one attachment site.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Agreement resolution and Closest Conjunct Agreement

- Severity: low for English; medium for Romance, Celtic, Semitic, Bantu
- Assumption affected: other (engine operations) / continuity
- Reference: Sections 4.2-4.3, pp. 745-750 (constraints (49), (52), (54)-(57))
- What the chapter says: The mother's agreement value is computed, not copied. Sag (2003) makes person resolution type unification by ordering the person types in a hierarchy. Aguila-Multner and Crysmann (2018) encode person and gender as list-valued features (ME, YOU, M) and resolve them by list concatenation over coordinands (49). For Closest Conjunct Agreement three treatments are given: sharing the mother's INDEX with the last coordinand (Moosally 1999, Ndebele); extra head features LAGR and RAGR that expose the first and last coordinand's CONCORD on the mother (Villavicencio et al. 2005), with a disjunction in the constraint for postnominal adjectives (57); and Borsley's (2009) analysis of Welsh, in which verb-subject agreement is stated over order domains so that the verb agrees with a first coordinand that is not its sister. French data show both resolution and closest-conjunct agreement as options in the same position (51).
- Why it matters: the list-concatenation and LAGR/RAGR analyses are compatible with replay by unification if append is encoded with difference lists. The disjunction in (57) and optional patterns in (51) are harmless for parsing because the surface form resolves them. The order-domain analysis of Welsh is outside the design: it states agreement over a linear representation that the derivation tree does not supply.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Correlative coordinators and the floating first element

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: continuity / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 3.2, pp. 736-737 (examples (18)-(20), Figure 6); Section 6, pp. 755-756 (example (69))
- What the chapter says: English "either" and "both" can appear away from the left edge of the first coordinand ("Fred either bought a cooking book or he bought a gardening magazine"; "John will both read the introduction and the conclusion"). "Given the linearization flexibility of the first coordinator, it can be analyzed in English as an adverbial." French reduplicated coordinators ("et ... et", "soit ... soit") are true coordinators and fall under the omnisyndetic subtype (12b). Hofmeister (2010) is cited for an HPSG account; its title, in the reference list, is "A linearization account of either ... or constructions".
- Why it matters: in (18b) "either" sits inside the first clause while the coordination is clausal, so the adverb analysis attaches it lower than its scope, and a linearization account would make the constituent discontinuous. The dependency between "either" and "or" is not visible in a continuous tree unless a feature carries it. In (19b) the position of "both" interacts with an elliptical analysis of the second coordinand (caveat 1).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 10. Comparative correlatives and other construction-specific clause pairs

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3.3, pp. 737-740 (examples (21)-(26), constraints (27)-(29), Figure 7)
- What the chapter says: "The more I read, the more I understand" is binary with fixed order, each clause is a special head-filler clause marked by a left-edge feature CORREL, and the construction is asymmetric (head-adjunct) in English but symmetric (coordinate) in French, with both types in Spanish. The copula can be omitted ("The more intelligent the students, the better the marks", (26d)). The meaning is conditional ("if I read more, I understand more"), and the authors say "a more complete analysis would take into account the semantics as well".
- Why it matters: this is a dedicated phrasal type with its own meaning, no token marking the relation between the clauses, and a verbless clause variant. It needs its own rule names in the tag set, and the chapter gives no semantics to check an MRS against.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 11. The semantics of coordination is set aside

- Severity: medium (as a gap in what the chapter can tell us)
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: footnote 5, p. 733; Section 7, pp. 757, 761-762
- What the chapter says: "The semantics and pragmatics of coordination is a particularly complex topic which we cannot do justice to here", especially the interaction with quantifier scope and collective, distributive, and reciprocal readings. Readers are sent to Koenig and Richter (Chapter 22), Copestake et al. (2005: Section 6.7), Fast (2005), Chaves (2007, 2009, 2012a, 2012b), and Park (2019). Footnote 34 (p. 761) mentions "cumulative" Right-Node Raising ("Mia donated, and Fred spent, a total of $10,000 between them"), where the shared phrase is interpreted once over both clauses.
- Why it matters: the chapter does not establish that MRS falls out of a coordination derivation. Collective versus distributive readings and cumulative Right-Node Raising are cases where one tree corresponds to several meanings or where the meaning is not the conjunction of two clausal meanings.
- DELPH-IN stance: the only pointer is the citation of Copestake et al. (2005: Section 6.7), the MRS paper, for the semantics of coordination.

## Support for the design

- Coordination rules are ordinary schemas. The two binary rules in (7) "are unremarkable and are handled by the grammar like any other immediate dominance schema" (Section 3, p. 730). A binary, concatenative analysis of standard coordination is a recognized option with a long list of adherents, including Pollard and Sag (1994) and Beavers and Sag (2004).
- No null coordinator is needed for asyndeton: a phrasal subtype with [COORD none] daughters does the work (constraint (12c), p. 732).
- The coordinator forms a constituent with the following coordinand (Section 3, p. 731; prosody and bound-morpheme evidence). Under the marker analysis it combines through the general Head-Functor rule (14); under the weak-head analysis through the ordinary head-complement rule (pp. 733-735). Either way the coordinator is one token with one lexical entry, and its attachment is a binary rule.
- Shared valence gives shared arguments without extra machinery. In VP coordination all nodes share the same SUBJ list, so "nothing needs to be said from the semantic composition side: the verbs will have to share exactly the same referent for their subject" (Section 4.1, p. 741). This is the kind of semantics-as-by-product the replay design relies on, and the UD bridge can read shared subjects from it.
- Case syncretism and person resolution reduce to typed unification over a suitable hierarchy (Levine et al. 2001, Figure 9, p. 745; Sag 2003, p. 747). No disjunction or special operation is required.
- The Coordinate Structure Constraint may not be syntactic. If it is pragmatic (Kehler 2002; Chaves 2012b; p. 744), the coordination rule need not enforce SLASH identity, which removes one source of replay failure on attested sentences.
- A non-elliptical analysis of Argument Cluster Coordination exists (Mouret 2006; Section 7, pp. 757-760, (73)-(74), Figure 12). The clusters "a book to Mary" and "a magazine to Sue" are surface constituents, coordinated by the normal rule, and a lexical rule lets the verb take the coordinated cluster as its complement. Every leaf is a token, the tree is continuous, and the lexical rule fits the label's unary chain. The chapter gives three arguments for it over ellipsis ("as well as", the position of correlative coordinators in French, constituent negation). The cluster phrase is n-ary and non-headed, so it still needs binarization.
- Direct-interpretation Gapping has no empty element in the syntax (Figure 14, p. 764); the cost is in the semantics (caveat 3).
- The Right-Node Raising rule is unary (p. 762), so in tree-shape terms it fits a unary chain; the problem lies in what its daughter contains (caveat 2).
- Wasow's Generalization (Section 2, p. 727): a coordination of X's distributes like an X, for virtually all categories. One category-neutral schema covers coordination across the grammar, which suits a coarse universal schema for the relaxed output.
- The authors' verdict that underspecification is "more promising" than ellipsis for unlike coordination (p. 756) favors surface-true trees over deletion.

## Theory strains

1. Headed or non-headed (Section 2, pp. 726-729; Section 3, p. 730, Figure 2). HPSG takes coordination to be non-headed; Kayne (1994) and Johannessen (1998) make the coordinator the head of a ConjP. Borsley (1994, 2005) and Chaves (2007) argue against the headed view. Coordination is thus the main exception to "head-driven" structure.
2. Binary, flat, or totally flat (Section 3, pp. 729-731). All three are used inside HPSG. The binary version needs a special feature against coordinator recursion; the authors hold that no internal structure is motivated, yet most HPSG work they cite is binary.
3. Coordinator as marker or weak head (Section 3.1, pp. 733-736). The marker entry (13) is "a bit unusual in that it is transparent" for MRKG. The weak-head analysis (16) "makes certain problematic predictions": coordinands become complements and should be extractable (*"Which boy did you compare Robin and?"), which is blocked by typing the coordinator's ARG-ST members as canonical (Abeillé 2003), a stipulation.
4. Formal hygiene of the constraints (footnote 4, p. 732). The original formulations use negative constraints (CONJ ≠ nil); the chapter's version uses tags that occur only once, which "strictly speaking ... are illegitimate", and needs an added identity with the top type to be precise.
5. Correlative first elements: coordinator or adverb (Section 3.2, pp. 736-737). English "either/both" are treated as adverbials, French "et ... et" as coordinators, and Romanian is said to have both types.
6. Comparative correlatives: coordinate or subordinate (Section 3.3, pp. 737-740). Culicover and Jackendoff (1999) say coordinate in syntax, den Dikken (2006) universally subordinate, Abeillé and Borsley (2008) language-dependent. The HPSG answer is a dedicated construction family with a new feature (CORREL) and cross-classification; the semantics is left open.
7. CAT identity overshoots (Section 4.1, pp. 741-744). INV, AUX, and SLASH identity all mispredict. The conclusion that only "some of the features in CAT" should be shared undercuts the original motivation for grouping features under CAT, and the chapter does not say which features remain.
8. The Coordinate Structure Constraint: syntax or pragmatics (Section 4.1, p. 744). SLASH identity derives it, asymmetric coordination violates it, and Chaves (2012b) and Kehler (2002) move it to pragmatics.
9. Where agreement lives (Section 4.2, pp. 745-746). Pollard and Sag (1994) put agreement in INDEX (semantics); Wechsler and Zlatić (2003) add a morphosyntactic CONCORD; coordination then requires still more features (LAGR, RAGR).
10. How resolution is computed (Section 4.3, pp. 746-747). A person type hierarchy (Sag 2003) versus list-valued features with concatenation (Aguila-Multner and Crysmann 2018). Each encodes resolution in a data structure chosen for the purpose.
11. Closest Conjunct Agreement and locality (Section 4.3.1, pp. 748-750; Conclusion, p. 765). "Agreement is always local (with the whole coordinate phrase) in some approaches, whereas locality is abandoned by others." The competitors are INDEX sharing with one coordinand (Moosally 1999), edge features (Villavicencio et al. 2005), and order domains (Borsley 2009).
12. Lexical coordination (Section 5, pp. 750-753). Disputed against phrasal-only views (Kayne 1994; Bruening 2018) and against a Right-Node Raising reanalysis (Beavers and Sag 2004). The result is "an apparent paradox", a phrase that behaves as a word, repaired with the WEIGHT feature.
13. Coordination of unlike categories (Section 6, pp. 753-756). Four competing accounts (disjunctive categories, type underspecification, gradient categories, left-periphery ellipsis). Levine (2011) argues against Chaves's (2006) coercion account and "against the existence of left-periphery ellipsis"; Yatabe (2012) replies. The disjunctive account is imported from Type-Logical Grammar.
14. Non-constituent coordination: deletion or not (Section 7, pp. 756-764; Conclusion, p. 765). Argument Cluster Coordination: ellipsis (Yatabe 2001; Crysmann 2004; Beavers and Sag 2004) versus clusters (Mouret 2006), each with its own evidence ((72) for ellipsis; (75)-(77) for clusters). Right-Node Raising: elliptical accounts dominate, against bounded syntactic accounts from Categorial Grammar (Steedman; Dowty). Gapping: ellipsis (Chaves 2006) versus direct interpretation (Mouret 2006; Abeillé et al. 2014; Park 2019). The elliptical accounts bring deletion-like operations and, in several versions, order domains into a framework that otherwise presents itself as surface-oriented.
15. Phenomena assumed to be coordination-specific are not (Section 7, pp. 762-764). Right-Node Raising and Gapping both occur outside coordination, so analyses that build them into the coordination rule (as (71) does for left-periphery ellipsis) are too narrow.

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter does not say how the ERG or any other implemented grammar analyzes coordination, Right-Node Raising, Gapping, or argument clusters, and it does not describe the content of Drellishak and Bender's module beyond citing it. Whether the ERG's treebanked derivations for these constructions are continuous and token-leaved has to be checked in the grammar and treebanks.
- Universal Dependencies is not mentioned. The remarks on the UD bridge in the caveats are my inferences about what the HPSG structures would require of a converter. The chapter's non-headed analysis has no counterpart to a choice of head among coordinands, so any head assignment in a conversion is a convention imposed from outside.
- The chapter gives no frequencies. How often the high-severity constructions (argument clusters, Right-Node Raising, Gapping) occur in running text, and so how much they cost the parser in practice, is an empirical question for the treebanks.
- The reference list gives "Crysmann 2004" as a paper on intersective modifier attachment, which does not obviously match its citation in Section 7 as an elliptical account of Argument Cluster Coordination; the intended source may be Crysmann 2008 (a 2003 Formal Grammar conference paper). I have not verified this.
- Punctuation is not discussed, although the examples use commas and dashes at exactly the points where prosodic boundaries are claimed (Right-Node Raising, Gapping). For a tagger, punctuation tokens may be the main available cue.
- The chapter does not discuss the scope of modifiers and quantifiers over coordinate structures ("old men and women"), coordination of unsaturated verb sequences with different valence in detail, or "respectively" readings beyond a citation (Chaves 2012a).
- Ellipsis in general is deferred to Chapter 19 (Nykiel and Kim), extraction and the Coordinate Structure Constraint to Chapters 13 and 15, and agreement to Chapter 6.
