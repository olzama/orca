# Chapter 19: Ellipsis (Joanna Nykiel and Jong-Bok Kim)

Handbook pages 847-888 (text 847-878, references 878-888). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter surveys HPSG analyses of three families of elliptical constructions: nonsentential utterances (fragment answers, Bare Argument Ellipsis, sluicing), predicate and argument ellipsis (Post-Auxiliary Ellipsis, i.e. VP ellipsis; pseudogapping; Null Complement Anaphora; argument drop), and non-constituent coordination (gapping, Right Node Raising, Argument Cluster Coordination). Section 3 weighs the evidence for and against invisible syntactic material at the ellipsis site (case matching, preposition stranding, islands, structural mismatches, nonlinguistic antecedents) and concludes that it does not support silent sentential structure. Sections 4-6 present the analyses: a unary Head-Fragment Schema whose content comes from the dialog context (MAX-QUD, SAL-UTT) for nonsentential utterances; a noncanonical "pro" element on ARG-ST, with no empty category, for Post-Auxiliary Ellipsis; and for non-constituent coordination a mix of fragment-based, deletion-based, and order-domain-based analyses. The authors stress that the analyses are deliberately nonuniform, and that HPSG "favors the 'what you see is what you get' approach to ellipsis and makes limited use of deletion-like operations" (p. 877).

The chapter is purely theoretical. It never mentions the ERG, the LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, MRS, or any implemented grammar or parser. Every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is therefore "not discussed", with the nearest relevant statement from the chapter added. Bearing on the parser is high in one specific way: for most of the constructions the surface tree poses no problem, but the semantics is not determined by the tree.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. The content of a nonsentential utterance comes from the dialog context, not from the derivation

- Severity: high (for MRS as a by-product); low for the tree encoding
- Assumption affected: semantics / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 4, pp. 860-864 (Head-Fragment Schema (31), Figures 1-3, examples (32)-(34)); Section 3.4, pp. 859-860
- What the chapter says: The Head-Fragment Schema (31) maps "any non-verbal phrasal category (NP, AP, VP, PP, AdvP)" onto a mother of category S, provided the daughter's CAT and index match the contextual attribute SAL-UTT, "an attribute supplied by the surrounding context as a (sub)utterance of another contextual attribute", MAX-QUD. "MAX-QUD provides the propositional semantics for a nonsentential utterance". In Figure 2, "The mike" as an answer to "What did Barry break?" gets its content from the MAX-QUD value (an abstraction over break(b, i)). "The structured dialogue thus plays a key role in the retrieval of the propositional semantics." MAX-QUD can come from the most recent wh-question, by accommodation from other constituents (direct sluicing, (33)), or from nonlinguistic context ((34), a cab driver's "Which airline?"; (29a), "What floor?" in an elevator). "All existing analyses of nonsentential utterances ... are based on the Head-Fragment Schema in (31)" (p. 864). The context is updated "with every new question-under-discussion"; Ginzburg (2012) keeps it in a Dialogue Game Board (footnote 16, p. 862).
- Why it matters: the tree is trivial (one unary rule over an XP), so the tagger and the replay engine have no difficulty with the structure. The semantics does not follow from that tree. Under this analysis, the propositional or question content needs CTXT values that come from a previous utterance or from the situation. A sentence-at-a-time replay can only leave MAX-QUD and SAL-UTT unbound, giving a fragment whose predicate is unknown. The same string with different contexts gets different contents, so "a derivation tree is a complete recipe for the semantics" holds only up to an unresolved contextual parameter.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 2. Several fragment clause types over the same string

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 4, pp. 860-861 (examples (30), Figure 1, footnote 15)
- What the chapter says: Ginzburg and Sag (2000) distinguish declarative fragments (decl-frag-cl), direct sluices (slu-int-cl), and reprise sluices (dir-is-int-cl), all subtypes of hd-frag-ph crossed with a clause type (Figure 1). Reprise sluices are "requests for clarification of any part of the antecedent". Figures 2 and 3 "differ only in the value of the feature CONT": a proposition versus a question. Sluices are underspecified for IC (independent clause); declarative fragments and reprise sluices are [IC +], which blocks embedding ("*I doubt bagels"). Footnote 15 adds that this specification "needs to be remedied for speakers who accept examples like ... Lee says eggs".
- Why it matters: the tagger has to pick among fragment rule names where the token string gives little evidence: direct and reprise sluices such as "About what?" (30b, 30c) differ only in their relation to the preceding discourse. If the rule name is wrong, replay still succeeds and yields the wrong content type. The [IC +] restriction would make strict replay reject embedded fragments that some speakers accept.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Post-Auxiliary Ellipsis and argument drop: arguments on ARG-ST with no token and no local content

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics / UD-bridge / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 868-870 (examples (41)-(47), lexical description (48), Figure 6); Section 4, pp. 865-866 (Argument Realization Principle (37), items (38), (39)); Section 2.2, pp. 849-850
- What the chapter says: The elided VP is "assumed to be a pro element due to its pronominal properties". "We do not need to posit a phonologically empty pronoun if a level of argument structure is available where we can encode the required pronominal properties." By the Argument Realization Principle (37), noncanonical synsems (gap-ss, pro) on ARG-ST "need not be realized in the syntax, permitting mismatch between argument structure and syntactic valence features". The auxiliary "can" in "Sandy can" has ARG-ST <NP, VP[pro]> and an empty COMPS list (48). The lexeme "paint" likewise yields two lexical items, one with an overt PP[with] complement and one with PP[with, pro] on ARG-ST only ((38), (39)). "Ellipsis resolution is not based on syntactic reconstruction in HPSG analyses, but rather on structured discourse information"; the antecedent comes from the Dialog Game Board via MAX-QUD, so structural mismatch (voice mismatch, nominal antecedents, split antecedents, (27)), island violations ((44), (45)), and nonlinguistic antecedents (28) are all expected.
- Why it matters: (a) each lexeme with an optional or droppable argument corresponds to more than one lexical item, so the tagger's lexical-type label must say which arguments are unrealized; the chapter treats this as a lexical alternation, which fits a label on the token. (b) The meaning of the elided VP is an unresolved anaphor in the output; the replayed semantics for "Sandy can" lacks the main predicate. Split antecedents (27c) show that resolution cannot be a copy of one earlier subtree. (c) In UD, the stranded auxiliary has no lexical verb to attach to, and a pro subject or complement has no token, so the two representations disagree on what the head of such a clause is.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Right Node Raising: all existing HPSG analyses use silent material; deletion over linearized strings, below the word level, and discontinuously

- Severity: high
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / no-empty-elements / continuity / tree-encoding / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 6.2, pp. 873-876 (examples (55)-(58), constraint (59), footnotes 31, 32)
- What the chapter says: "Right Node Raising has consistently attracted HPSG analyses involving silent material ... All existing analyses of Right Node Raising (Abeillé et al. 2016; Beavers and Sag 2004; Chaves 2014; Crysmann 2008; Shiraïshi et al. 2019; Yatabe 2001; 2012) agree on this point". One strand is linearization-based (order domains; Yatabe 2001, 2012); the other uses "a deletion-like operation" (Chaves 2014; Abeillé et al. 2016; Shiraïshi et al. 2019). The shared material can be a non-constituent (55b); the conjuncts can differ in argument structure ((56a), (56b)); the target can be below the word level ("the hard- but they got us the soft-[cover edition]", "under- and ... over[generates]", (56c), (56d)); it can be a string "not subject to any known syntactic operations" (57); and it can be discontinuous ("unwilling or unable [to perform the necessary actions] without injury", (58)). Chaves's Backward Periphery Deletion Construction (59) deletes a sequence of morphophonological constituents under identity "up to morph forms" and "targets any linearized strings, whether constituents or otherwise". Right Node Raising also occurs outside coordination (footnote 30).
- Why it matters: under every analysis the chapter lists, the left conjunct is a complete phrase part of whose phonology is absent from the string. The derivation tree therefore has lexical leaves without tokens, or (in the order-domain strand) a mother whose domain is not the concatenation of its daughters. A one-label-per-token encoding cannot express that directly. Replay is not determined by the rule name: (59) is a relational constraint that splits a sequence into five parts with a choice of what is deleted. Sub-word sharing breaks the equation of tokens with lexical leaves unless the tokenizer splits prefixes and compound parts.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter reports no continuous, surface-only HPSG analysis of true Right Node Raising. It does say that Chaves (2014) removes part of the data from the deletion mechanism (see Support for the design).

### 5. Argument Cluster Coordination as ellipsis over DOM lists

- Severity: high under the ellipsis analysis; low under the cluster analysis
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / continuity / tree-encoding / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 6.3, pp. 876-877 (examples (60)-(62), schema (63), footnotes 33-35)
- What the chapter says: Two views exist: ellipsis (Yatabe 2001; Crysmann 2008; Beavers and Sag 2004) and non-standard constituents (Mouret 2006). The chapter presents only the ellipsis view, "which better fits this chapter". Beavers and Sag treat "Jan travels to Rome tomorrow, to Paris on Friday, and will fly to Tokyo on Sunday" as VP coordination with an elided verb in the second conjunct (62). Their Coordination Schema (63) uses the DOM lists of linearization theory and lets "some elements in the daughters' DOM lists ... be absent from the mother's DOM list": the daughters have DOM A + B1 + D and C + A + B2 + D, the mother has A + B1 + C + B2 + D. With A empty the schema gives ordinary coordination; with A non-empty, Argument Cluster Coordination; with A and D both non-empty, examples like (62). "The content of the DOM list consists of prosodic constituents."
- Why it matters: the same schema licenses plain coordination and ellipsis depending on how the lists A and D are resolved, so the rule name alone does not determine the result of replay, and the second conjunct contains a verb with no token. It also presupposes order domains (DOM), which the design excludes by assuming concatenative derivations.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The schema is "a simplified version" of Beavers and Sag (2004: 27). The DOM proposal is attributed to Crysmann (2003), whose title in the reference list is "On the treatment of word order in a computational grammar of German"; the chapter text says nothing about its being implemented.

### 6. Gapping as a fragment over a cluster phrase: non-binary daughters, underspecified category, content from the first conjunct

- Severity: medium (high for the semantics of the gapped conjunct)
- Assumption affected: semantics / binary-unary-rules / tree-encoding / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 6.1, pp. 870-873 (examples (49)-(54), Figure 7, constraint (52), footnotes 27-29)
- What the chapter says: Analyses "fall into two kinds": deletion-like (Chaves 2009, drawing on Beavers and Sag 2004) and fragment-based (Abeillé et al. 2014, drawing on Ginzburg and Sag 2000). In the latter, the gapped conjunct is a head-fragment phrase whose single daughter is a cluster phrase (XP+) containing the remnants (Figure 7); "there is no unpronounced verbal head". The cluster's mother "has an underspecified syntactic category" and so does the fragment phrase. The coordination construction is nonheaded; the HEAD value of the conjuncts "is not fixed but contains an upper bound (supertype)", and in gapping only the first clause shares HEAD with the mother. "The meanings of the gapping remnants are computed from the meaning of the leftmost nonelliptical verbal conjunct." Constraint (52) unifies the HEAD value of each SAL-UTT member with the corresponding cluster element. Remnants need not match their correlates in category ("crazy" and "an incredible bore", (50)), can have an implicit correlate (a pro subject in Italian, (51)), and can come in a different order (54) "as long as some symmetric discourse relation holds", which is encoded in BACKGROUND. Gapping occurs where a full finite clause cannot (53) and outside coordination (footnote 28). "A cluster refers to any sequence of dependents" (footnote 29).
- Why it matters: the surface tree is continuous and has no empty leaf, which suits the tree encoding. Three things do not fit as given: the cluster phrase takes a sequence of daughters of unspecified length (the figure shows two, the definition says "any sequence"); the content of the gapped conjunct depends on SAL-UTT, a contextual value that must be identified with the dependents of the verb in the first conjunct, which is a step the chapter does not spell out as a rule-internal identity; and the reordering in (54) means that remnants cannot be aligned with correlates by position alone. A UD bridge also has no verb to head the gapped conjunct.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Sprouting: the form of a fragment depends on unrealized ARG-ST elements of an earlier clause

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics / other (information that must survive in the feature structure after replay)
- Reference: Section 3.1, pp. 854-855 (examples (16), (17)); Section 4, pp. 865-867 (examples (35)-(40), Figures 4, 5)
- What the chapter says: With an overt correlate ("painted the wall with something"), the fragment can be "With what?" or "What?", because the antecedent "activates not only the PP information but also its internal structure". With no correlate (sprouting), only the PP is possible, because the implicit PP[pro] on the verb's ARG-ST is the only available SAL-UTT. "The interlocutor can have access to this ARG-ST information, but nothing further."
- Why it matters: under this proposal, the ARG-ST of a verb, including its unrealized members, must remain visible in the analysis of a finished clause so that a later utterance can be checked against it. A parser whose output is the MRS alone would discard what the analysis relies on. This affects cross-sentence licensing only.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter says the sprouting problem "has not received much attention in the HPSG literature, though see Kim (2015)" (p. 855) and credits the sketch to discussions with Anne Abeillé (footnote 20).

### 8. Strict matching between fragment and antecedent rules out attested data

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (strict replay failing on acceptable input)
- Reference: Section 3.1, pp. 851-853 (examples (13)-(15)); Section 4, p. 868 (and footnote 22)
- What the chapter says: (31) requires the fragment's CAT, including case, to match SAL-UTT. The authors state that "(31) incorrectly rules out case mismatch in languages like Hungarian for speakers that do accept it". Korean fragments can drop case markers where the full clause cannot ((14), (15)); Kim (2015) adds "a case hierarchy specific to Korean".
- Why it matters: relevant only once context is modeled, and for the multi-language goal. If the matching is implemented as plain unification, some acceptable fragments fail.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- No empty elements for ellipsis. The chapter's overall conclusion is that "HPSG favors the 'what you see is what you get' approach to ellipsis and makes limited use of deletion-like operations" (Section 7, p. 877). "HPSG doesn't assume that nonsentential utterances are underlyingly sentential structures" (Section 2.1, p. 849).
- Fragments are a unary rule over an ordinary phrase. The Head-Fragment Schema (31) has one daughter, a non-verbal XP, and a mother of category S (Section 4, pp. 862-864, Figures 2, 3). The tree is continuous, its leaves are the tokens, and the rule fits a unary-chain label.
- VP ellipsis and argument drop are lexical. The missing VP or argument is a noncanonical synsem on ARG-ST that is not mapped to COMPS; "we do not need to posit a phonologically empty pronoun" (Section 5, pp. 868-870, (48), Figure 6). The alternation is a choice between lexical items ((38) versus (39), p. 866), which is what a per-token lexical-type label can carry.
- Gapping has a surface-only analysis. In Abeillé et al. (2014) "there is no unpronounced verbal head", and the analysis "does not reconstruct a syntactic gapped clause" (Section 6.1, pp. 872-873). The tree in Figure 7 is continuous.
- Argument Cluster Coordination has a non-elliptical alternative with "non-standard constituents" (Mouret 2006), which the chapter mentions and defers to Chapter 16 (Section 6.3, p. 876).
- Part of what looks like Right Node Raising needs no deletion. Chaves (2014) splits the phenomenon into extraposition (via the EXTRA feature), VP or N' ellipsis (direct interpretation), and true Right Node Raising; "only true Right Node Raising should be accounted for via the mechanism of optional surface-based deletion" (Section 6.2, p. 875, footnote 31).
- Resolution of ellipsis is kept out of syntax. Since resolution uses "structured discourse information" and not syntactic reconstruction (Section 5, p. 869), a sentence-level parser can stop at an underspecified result and leave resolution to a later component. The chapter does not draw this conclusion itself; it follows from the division it describes.
- The empirical argument against silent structure (Section 3, pp. 851-860) backs the no-empty-elements assumption for these constructions: case matching is weaker or stronger than deletion predicts (Hungarian, Korean); fragments without prepositions occur in languages without preposition stranding; fragments ignore islands; Post-Auxiliary Ellipsis tolerates voice mismatch and nonlinguistic antecedents.

## Theory strains

- Silent structure versus direct interpretation (Section 3, pp. 851-860; Section 7, p. 877). The central dispute is with Minimalist PF-deletion analyses (Merchant 2001, 2005a, 2013). The authors grant that "there is evidence to support both of these views" (p. 849). Case matching is "crosslinguistically robust" and favors silent structure; Hungarian and Korean cut against it.
- HPSG is not uniform on ellipsis, by design. The authors' stated purpose is "to highlight the nonuniformity of these analyses" (p. 848). Three options are all in use: coordinating non-canonical constituents, deletion-like operations, and coordinating VPs with nonsentential utterances (Section 2.3, p. 850). The WYSIWYG stance is given up for Right Node Raising, where all analyses use silent material (Section 6.2, p. 874).
- Gapping: deletion (Chaves 2009) versus fragment (Abeillé et al. 2014) (Section 6.1, p. 871). The classification is itself unsettled: gapping "could be classified together with other nonsentential utterances" and not with non-constituent coordination.
- Right Node Raising: order-domain analyses (Yatabe 2001, 2012) versus deletion (Chaves 2014; Abeillé et al. 2016; Shiraïshi et al. 2019), and within deletion a dispute over the identity condition. Chaves requires identity of morph forms; Abeillé et al. (2016) and Shiraïshi et al. (2019) argue that "morphophonological identity is too strong", with a split between functional and lexical categories in French (Section 6.2, pp. 874-876). Some authors need "more than one mechanism" (p. 874).
- Argument Cluster Coordination: ellipsis (Yatabe 2001; Crysmann 2008; Beavers and Sag 2004) versus non-standard constituents (Mouret 2006) (Section 6.3, p. 876). The ellipsis argument rests on "the assumption that only constituents of like category can coordinate", while footnote 33 concedes "numerous examples ... where unlike categories are coordinated".
- The Head-Fragment Schema is admittedly wrong in places. It "incorrectly rules out case mismatch" in Hungarian (p. 868); the [IC +] stipulation fails for speakers who accept "Lee says eggs" (footnote 15, p. 861); Korean needs a language-specific case hierarchy (footnote 22, p. 868). The authors call these "loose ends".
- Sprouting. Requiring the fragment to match an implicit PP "has not received much attention in the HPSG literature" (Section 3.1, p. 855). The proposed account relies on what the discourse "activates via syntactic realizations" (p. 867): an overt PP also activates its internal NP, an implicit one does not. This is stated informally, with no constraint given. Footnote 7 (p. 854) cites corpus examples where adjunct sprouts do drop the preposition.
- Islands and Post-Auxiliary Ellipsis. "We do not yet have a complete understanding of when or why island effects show up in Post-Auxiliary Ellipsis. Its behavior is at best inconsistent" (Section 3.2, p. 857). Whether Bare Argument Ellipsis respects islands is also disputed (footnote 9, p. 855).
- Structural mismatch: grammatical or repaired? One view treats mismatches as grammatical and blames discourse or processing for degraded acceptability (Kehler 2002; Miller 2011, 2014; Miller and Pullum 2014; Kim et al. 2011); the other posits a grammatical ban plus repair (Arregui et al. 2006; Grant et al. 2012). HPSG takes the first view, though the authors note that it leaves the consistent voice-mismatch penalty of Kim and Runner (2018) "perhaps unexpected" and appeal to "construction-specific constraints" (Section 3.3, pp. 858-859).
- Much of the explanation is moved to discourse and processing. Acceptability differences for prepositionless fragments are "for reasons still poorly understood" (p. 854); the rarity of nonlinguistic antecedents is attributed to how easily a situation gives rise to a MAX-QUD (footnote 21, p. 868). The grammar proper licenses more than is accepted.
- Null Complement Anaphora "has received relatively little attention in modern syntactic theory, including in HPSG", is limited to a set of main verbs, and "its exact nature remains controversial" (footnote 24, p. 869).
- Pseudogapping is an exception to the generalization that Post-Auxiliary Ellipsis applies only to phrasal categories (p. 868) and is left out of the chapter (footnote 26, p. 870).
- Pseudosluices in Korean and Mandarin: cleft-source analyses (Merchant 2001) versus Kim (2015), who separates embedded pseudosluices from root sluices (footnote 18, p. 864).
- The Argument Realization Principle as given (37) is "an adapted version" of Ginzburg and Sag (2000: 171) and Bouma et al. (2001: 11) (footnote 19, p. 865); the formulation varies between sources.

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter says nothing about any implemented grammar. How the ERG treats fragments, VP ellipsis, gapping, Right Node Raising, and Argument Cluster Coordination has to be checked in the grammar itself.
- The chapter does not say how an underspecified semantic representation such as MRS would represent an unresolved fragment or elided VP. All its semantics is given as informal lambda terms over MAX-QUD, in the Ginzburg and Sag (2000) situation-semantic setting.
- No formal statement is given of how SAL-UTT of the gapped conjunct is tied to the first conjunct inside one sentence; (52) only relates SAL-UTT to the cluster.
- Comparative ellipsis, stripping as a separate construction, N' ellipsis (mentioned once, p. 875), and antecedent-contained deletion are not treated.
- Nothing is said about how frequent each construction is, which would matter for deciding how much of this the tagger must handle and how much can go to the relaxed output.
- The UD treatment of ellipsis (promotion of a dependent, the orphan relation, enhanced dependencies with empty nodes) is not mentioned, so the UD-bridge remarks above are my inferences from the HPSG structures the chapter gives.
