# Chapter 21: Morphology (Berthold Crysmann)

Handbook pages 947-999 (text 947-991, references from 991). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter surveys HPSG work on derivation and inflection. Section 2 traces how lexical rules changed from meta-level mappings between lexical items into partial descriptions inside the hierarchical lexicon (Krieger and Nerbonne 1993, Riehemann 1998, Koenig 1999 with Online Type Construction). Section 3 sets out the classical problems of inflection (cumulation, multiple and overlapping exponence, zero exponence, syncretism, Paninian defaults), the three families of theories (Item-and-Arrangement, Item-and-Process, Word-and-Paradigm), and earlier HPSG proposals. Section 4, the larger half, introduces Information-based Morphology (IbM; Crysmann and Bonami 2016), in which a word carries a flat list of morphs with position-class indices, a set of morphosyntactic properties, and a set of realization rules relating the two, with blocking compiled into the rule hierarchy.

The chapter says little about parsing. Its statements about implemented grammars are confined to one passage (Section 3.3, pp. 958-959): grammar implementations typically follow the Item-and-Process line, and in the LKB "character unification" states morphophonological changes "that can be attached to (unary) lexical rules"; Goodman and Bender (2010) treat dependencies between morphological rules; Crysmann (2015; 2017b) implements non-concatenative morphology. TRALE, Enju, and ACE are not mentioned. For English and the ERG the bearing on the parser is modest, and that one passage supports the design. The caveats matter mostly for the long-term multilingual aim, because the model the chapter presents as the emerging consensus for inflection (IbM) is not a chain of unary rules.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. The consensus model of inflection (IbM) has no rule chain and no word-internal derivation

- Severity: medium (low for the ERG, higher for the multilingual aim)
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules; tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 965-969 (constraints 9, 13-16); Section 5, p. 991
- What the chapter says: In IbM a word has INFL with MPH (a list of morphs with position-class indices), MS (a set of morphosyntactic properties), and RR (a set of realization rules). A well-formedness principle (15) requires that the rules' expressed properties (MUD) add up to MS and that their morph lists shuffle into the word's MPH in ascending position-class order (16). IbM "eliminates structure from inflectional morphology" (p. 966): there is "no hierarchy involved", in contrast to Paradigm Function Morphology and A-Morphous Morphology, whose "abundant use of derivation structure" the chapter counts against them (p. 967). The chapter calls IbM a "consensus model" that "seems to have arrived" (p. 991).
- Why it matters: the planned label encodes "lexical type plus chain of unary/lexical rules". Under IbM the inflection of a word is a set of simultaneously applying realization rules checked by one word-level principle, so there is no ordered chain and no unary node per affix in a derivation tree. A grammar written this way would need the label to carry a set of rule names (or the MS property set), and replay would be one constraint check at the word, not a sequence of unary rule applications.
- DELPH-IN stance: the chapter says implementations "typically" follow the Item-and-Process line with unary lexical rules (LKB, pp. 958-959), which is the model the design assumes. It does not say whether IbM has been implemented on any platform.

### 2. Blocking (Paninian competition) is not part of the standard HPSG logic

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 3.3, pp. 963-964 (Koenig's Blocking Principle, footnote 5); Section 4.3, pp. 971-974 (definition 18, footnote 8)
- What the chapter says: "Competition between rules or lexical entries does not follow from the logic standardly assumed within HPSG: if a rule can apply, it will apply, no matter whether there are any more specific or more general rules that could have applied as well" (p. 972). Blocking therefore "necessitates a change to the logic". Two options are described: static (compile time), where a closure operation adds the negation of each competitor's conditions to the more general rule so that the two descriptions become "fully disjoint" (18b); and dynamic (run time), where rules are sorted by specificity and the more specific one is tried first (fn. 8). Koenig's cheaper alternative is a diacritic feature ([IRR +] on irregular bases, [IRR -] on the regular rule), whose cost is "minimal" but which "will not scale up to a full treatment of Paninian competition" (p. 963, fn. 5).
- Why it matters: replay tests whether the named rule unifies. If the grammar does not encode blocking in the rule descriptions, replay will license a general (regular) rule that the tagger picked where a more specific or irregular form should have preempted it, and the output would wrongly count as strict. With the static compilation or with diacritic features, blocking is ordinary unification failure and replay checks it. With the dynamic version, licensing depends on what other rules could have applied, which replay does not look at.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter does not say how LKB grammars handle blocking.

### 3. IbM and related analyses use formal devices beyond plain typed unification

- Severity: medium (for a grammar that adopts these analyses; none for the ERG as described here)
- Assumption affected: other (expressive power of the feature-structure engine); deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 3.3, pp. 959-960 (distributed disjunctions, default inheritance); Section 4.1, pp. 968-969 (15, 16); Section 4.3, pp. 972-974 (18, 21, 22); Section 4.4, pp. 983-984 (30, 31)
- What the chapter says: IbM uses set-valued features, set union and "non-trivial set union" (disjoint union), the shuffle relation on morph lists, a negative ordering constraint (16b), negated descriptions inside MS added by the Paninian closure (21, 22), spans of position classes (fn. 10), and arithmetic on position indices as "a convenient shortcut" for a list encoding (p. 984). Krieger and Nerbonne's approach needs distributed disjunctions and default inheritance and "has not been widely adopted, partially because few versions of HPSG support default inheritance and even fewer support distributed disjunctions" (p. 960). Online Type Construction and the Paninian closure are operations on the type hierarchy (closure, expansion) performed before or during use (fn. 7, p. 971).
- Why it matters: a "small" engine that does only typed unification cannot evaluate these constraints directly. Either the grammar is compiled first (types expanded, competition made disjoint, relations encoded as lists), or the engine has to grow relational constraints, sets, and negation.
- DELPH-IN stance: only the LKB's "character unification" on unary lexical rules is mentioned (p. 959). Whether DELPH-IN formalisms can express the IbM machinery is not discussed.

### 4. The form of a word underdetermines its rule chain and property set

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (label ambiguity); semantics
- Reference: Section 3.1, pp. 955-957 (zero exponence, syncretism, Table 21.1); Section 4.3, pp. 974-976 (default zero realization); Section 4.5.3, p. 989 (Oneida)
- What the chapter says: Inflection is many-to-many between form and function. Zero exponence is "extremely wide-spread" (sheep, hit/hit; German Rechner with two overt cells of eight; Brezen with none). Syncretic forms may share a natural class, be a default, or be "morphomic" with no natural class at all (Old French mur/mur-s). In IbM zero exponence is a rule type with an empty morph list and an underspecified MUD. In Oneida, features outranked on a markedness hierarchy are "optionally interpreted": a negative marker "can be interpreted as negative or negative and partitive" (p. 989).
- Why it matters: for a bare or syncretic form the tagger must choose among several rule chains (or cells) from sentence context alone, since the string gives no evidence. Where the grammar offers one underspecified rule for a natural class, one label suffices; morphomic syncretism has no such single description, so the label set must list the cells. The Oneida case shows that the same morph sequence can map to different property sets, and so to different semantics, without any difference in form.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Inflectional meaning is not composed rule by rule

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 3.1, p. 955; Section 3.2, p. 958; Section 3.3, pp. 958-959; Section 4.1, p. 968
- What the chapter says: "while rule-by-rule compositionality can count as the success story of syntax and semantics, this does not hold in the same way for inflection" (p. 955). Item-and-Process models, in which each rule application adds form and meaning together, "do not recognise the m : n nature of inflectional morphology and are therefore expected to have problems with e.g. multiple exponence" (p. 958). The description-level lexical rule approach used in implementations is classed as Item-and-Process (p. 958).
- Why it matters: with MRS as a by-product of replaying unary lexical rules, each rule contributes its piece. Circumfixes (ge-...-t), double agreement marking (Nyanja), and overlapping exponence need either one rule that does two form changes, or two rules of which only one contributes the property, with a dependency between them that the grammar must enforce.
- DELPH-IN stance: Goodman and Bender (2010) are cited for "a proposal as to how requirements for certain inflections and dependencies between morphological rules, e.g. the parts of extended or overlapping exponence, can be captured in a more systematic way" (p. 959). No detail is given.

### 6. Clitic and pronominal affix clusters are analyzed as word-internal inflection

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens; UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 3.3, p. 959 (Miller and Sag 1997; Crysmann 2003); Section 4.4, pp. 982-984 (Italian examples 29, Figures 11-12; Sorani Kurdish, Table 21.7, rule 31; footnote 14 on European Portuguese mesoclisis)
- What the chapter says: French, Italian, and Portuguese pronominal "clitics" are treated as affix clusters realized by inflectional rules of the verb (Italian me-lo-da-te / da-te-me-lo). Sorani Kurdish person markers are "endoclitic": they surface after the first morph of the word, whatever it is, so they can stand between a prefix and the stem (da=jân-nard-im). Placement is computed with pivot features inside the word's morph list.
- Why it matters: in these analyses the pronominal markers are not signs and have no leaf of their own; the verb with its cluster is one leaf. Any tokenization that splits the markers off gives tokens with no corresponding leaf, and a second-position marker inside a word cannot be split off as a contiguous token sequence at the word edge.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Derivation is unbounded, and lexical rules feed each other

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (size of the label inventory)
- Reference: Section 2.1, p. 949; Section 2.3, p. 954 and footnote 3; Section 4, footnote 6, p. 965
- What the chapter says: Inflection is "essentially finite (modulo case stacking)", but derivation need not be: German Vor-silbe, Vor-vor-silbe, and so on (p. 949). "A full treatment of derivational processes will still require recursive rule types" (p. 954). Mueller (2013) and Mueller and Wechsler (2014) argue that grammatical function change depends on one lexical rule applying to the output of another, and that in Turkish causatives "a rule may even apply more than once" (fn. 3). In Oneida, derivation can target partially inflected bases, and inflected and derived nominals are incorporated into polysynthetic verbs (fn. 6).
- Why it matters: if the label encodes the whole unary chain of a token, the set of possible labels is not finite in principle, and the order "derivation first, inflection last" cannot be built into the label format for all languages. Koenig also notes that uninflected roots and partially derived words must not function as lexical signs, "so it is necessary to enforce that inflection be applied" (p. 961); replay has to check that the chain is complete.
- DELPH-IN stance: Goodman and Bender (2010) are cited for stating "requirements for certain inflections" (p. 959). Recursion in implemented grammars is not discussed.

### 8. Online Type Construction: alternations as inferred types, not rule applications

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding; binary-unary-rules
- Reference: Section 2.3, pp. 952-954 (Figures 2-3, footnote 3); Section 4.2, pp. 970-971 (Figure 6, footnote 7)
- What the chapter says: In Koenig's type-underspecified lexicon, the active and passive variants of a verb are not related by a rule. Each is a conjunction of one leaf type per dimension (kill-lxm and pass-lxm), inferred on demand; "the unexpanded lexicon is just a system of types". IbM builds its realization rule instances the same way. Under the "dynamic view" the admissible types are inferred online; under the "static view" the underspecified hierarchy is a shortcut for the grammar writer (fn. 7).
- Why it matters: under this analysis a passive verb has no unary passive node in its derivation; the alternation is in the choice of lexical type. The names that the label and the derivation tree refer to would be conjunctions of types that are not listed anywhere, so the label would have to be factored by dimension or the hierarchy expanded in advance.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter reports Mueller's and Mueller and Wechsler's skepticism about this analysis of grammatical function change (fn. 3) and its own verdict that Online Type Construction "is confined to finite domains" (p. 954).

### 9. Word-syntactic and morpheme-based analyses put signs below the word

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens
- Reference: Section 2.1, p. 949; Section 3.3, p. 958
- What the chapter says: Krieger and Nerbonne (1993) treat derivational affixes "as signs that select the bases with which they combine", so a derived word has binary internal structure with an affix leaf. Item-and-Arrangement proposals for inflection exist too: Van Eynde (1994) and "more recently" Emerson and Copestake (2015) are "notable exceptions" to the preference for process or realizational models.
- Why it matters: in such a grammar leaves are morphemes, and tokens would have to be segmented before tagging.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed (the chapter does not say whether Emerson and Copestake's proposal is implemented).

### 10. Non-concatenative morphology

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (the form-side check of a lexical rule)
- Reference: Section 2.2, p. 950; Section 3.1, p. 955; Section 3.3, pp. 958-959
- What the chapter says: Riehemann's unary-rule view gives no privileged status to concatenation and covers modification, conversion, and subtractive back formation (self-destruct from self-destruction). Umlaut marks plural in German (Muetter-n). In lexical-rule approaches the form change is "some (often undefined) function on the phonology of the daughter" (p. 958).
- Why it matters: replay of a lexical rule has to confirm that the token's surface form is what the rule produces from the stem; for stem modification, reduplication, or tone this is more than stripping an affix.
- DELPH-IN stance: LKB character unification states the morphophonological changes on unary lexical rules; Crysmann (2015; 2017b) are cited as "implementations of non-concatenative morphology" (tone and reduplication in a computational grammar of Hausa) (p. 959).

## Support for the design

- Implemented grammars use chains of unary lexical rules. "Work on morphology in grammar implementation typically follows this line" (Item-and-Process: marking tied to rule application), and in the LKB the form changes are "attached to (unary) lexical rules" (Section 3.3, pp. 958-959). This is the structure the label's rule chain encodes.
- Derivation as unary rules, with only the base as a sign. Riehemann (1998) argues against word syntax: "only the morphological base, a lexeme, should be considered a sign", and affixes are "syncategorematically introduced by rule application" (Section 2.2, p. 950). Krieger and Nerbonne already suggest unary rules for conversion (p. 949). Leaves stay equal to words.
- No zero morphemes. Morpheme-based models are criticized because they "need to make extensive appeal to zero morphemes", and the HPSG community shows "a clear preference for IP or WP models over IA" (Sections 3.2-3.3, pp. 957-958). In IbM zero exponence is a rule with an empty morph list, and "nothing much needs to be said about empty slots" (pp. 975, 985). This matches the no-empty-elements assumption at the word level.
- Lexical rules are descriptions in the type hierarchy. The chapter reports a settled consensus that "lexical rules are description-level devices organised into cross-cutting inheritance type hierarchies" (Section 5, p. 990), so a lexical rule is a named type that can be applied by unification, as replay requires.
- Inflection is finite. Inflection "is essentially finite (modulo case stacking)" (p. 949) and inflectional paradigms "may be large but finite" (fn. 4, p. 961), so the inflectional part of the label inventory is bounded.
- Blocking can be made a matter of plain unification. Koenig's [IRR] diacritic has "minimal" cost for the regular/irregular case (p. 963), and the static Paninian closure makes competing rule descriptions "fully disjoint" (p. 972). In both cases a wrong rule choice fails in replay.
- Same logic for morphology and syntax. IbM "exploits the same logic as HPSG, namely typed feature structure inheritance networks" (p. 991), so morphology in this style needs no second formalism next to the feature-structure engine (subject to caveat 3).
- Inflection needs no word-internal tree. "Inflectional morphology does not provide much evidence for internal structure" (p. 981). The tree encoding does not have to represent structure inside words for inflection.

## Theory strains

- Status of lexical rules (Section 1, p. 948; Section 2.3, p. 952). Early HPSG took lexical rules as meta-level mappings between lexical items (after Bresnan); Koenig (1999) rejects both the generative and the redundancy-rule reading in favor of partial descriptions in the hierarchical lexicon; Meurers (2001) formalizes description-level lexical rules "as standardly used in HPSG" (fn. 1).
- Underspecification versus lexical rules for grammatical function change (Section 2.3, pp. 952-954, fn. 3). Koenig derives passive by cross-classification in the type hierarchy. Mueller (2013) and Mueller and Wechsler (2014) "take a highly sceptical stance": such alternations interact, one rule applies to the output of another, and Turkish causatives apply more than once. The chapter itself concedes that Online Type Construction is limited to finite domains.
- Word syntax versus unary rules for derivation (Sections 2.1-2.2, pp. 949-950). Krieger and Nerbonne treat affixes as selecting signs; Riehemann argues against it.
- The same authors use different models for derivation and inflection (Section 3.3, pp. 959-960). Krieger and Nerbonne's inflection is word-and-paradigm while their derivation is morpheme-based, a contrast the chapter calls "quite stark"; Koenig is word-based for inflection and Item-and-Process for derivation.
- No agreed model of inflection for a long time (Section 3.3, pp. 958, 964). "There is no a priori expectation" as to which model fits HPSG, and "every one of the three models have been proposed". The Item-and-Process model is "tacitly assumed by many HPSG syntacticians" and "merely attaches some morpho-phonological function to a lexical rule", a function that is "often undefined".
- Importing an external morphology (Section 3.3, pp. 959, 964). Bonami plugs Paradigm Function Morphology into HPSG at the word level; the chapter objects that this "begs the question why we need a different formalism". Koenig's word-based model has "problems of scalability with morphotactically complex systems". "Neither approach has proven to be fully satisfactory."
- Blocking is outside the logic (Section 3.3, pp. 963-964; Section 4.3, pp. 971-972). Paninian competition requires "a change to the logic". The dynamic reading "leaves open the question why this kind of competition is mainly restricted to lexical knowledge"; the static reading needs the underspecified lexicon to be expanded first. Diacritic exception features need one stipulation per default/override pair (fn. 5).
- Default zero exponence (Section 4.3, pp. 975-976). IbM work claimed "repeatedly" that a single zero-exponence default suffices. The chapter shows that the current formulation of Panini's principle does not deliver this and settles for one subtype per inflectional dimension, "slightly less general than what might have been hoped for". The Identity Function Default of Paradigm Function Morphology makes paradigm gaps hard to model.
- Two treatments of multiple marking (Section 4.3, pp. 976-979). Overlapping exponence is handled by conditioning (allomorphy), pure multiple exponence (Nyanja) by composing rules. The chapter asks "whether or not the approaches should be harmonised" and declines, because harmonizing would force negation, tense, and relative marking into one multi-dimensional hierarchy.
- Word-based versus rule-based morphology (Section 4.5, pp. 985-988). Gestalt exponence in Estonian is "one of the strongest arguments" for the word-based view (Blevins 2005; 2016); the chapter argues that word-based accounts miss partial generalizations and cannot reuse exponents across parallel position classes (Swahili subject and object markers), a problem it says also affects Koenig's (1999) word-level analysis.
- Is inflection a property of words only? (Section 4, fn. 6, p. 965). IbM assumes so as a "simplifying assumption"; Koenig and Michelson (2020) give "compelling evidence" from Oneida that derivation targets inflected bases, so the interface between lexical types and inflection needs to be generalized.
- Flat versus layered morphotactics (Section 4.4, pp. 982-983). Italian cluster placement raises "whether morphotactics is in need of a more layered structure"; IbM answers with pivot features (STM-PC, 1ST-PC) distributed over every morph. Position indices are written with arithmetic but "actually represented as lists underlyingly" (p. 984). MPH was a set in earlier IbM work and is now a list, "slightly redundant" (p. 966).
- Formal devices with little platform support (Section 3.3, p. 960). Distributed disjunctions and default inheritance are supported by "few versions of HPSG"; Koenig argues distributed disjunctions will not scale.
- The consensus claim (Section 5, pp. 990-991). The chapter states that a consensus model of inflection "seems to have arrived" and names IbM; the chapter's author is a co-author of that model, and the alternatives the chapter lists (word-based, Paradigm Function Morphology in HPSG, morpheme-based) are recent.

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not statements from the chapter.

- The chapter never mentions the ERG, ACE, TRALE, or Enju, and gives no detail of how LKB orthographemic rules work, how irregular forms are listed, or how implemented grammars block regular forms of irregular stems. The DELPH-IN documentation and Copestake (2002) would have to answer this.
- Universal Dependencies is not mentioned. The link from caveat 6 to the UD bridge rests on my assumption that UD treebanks split some clitics into separate syntactic words; this should be checked per language against the UD guidelines.
- Tokenization as such is not discussed: nothing on punctuation, contracted forms, English possessive 's, or multiword tokens.
- Compounding is mentioned only in passing (Figure 1 has a compound type; French compounding studies are cited on p. 955). No analysis is given, so the chapter does not say whether compounds are one leaf or several.
- Periphrasis (inflectional values expressed by several words) appears only in the titles of cited works by Bonami; the chapter does not discuss it, although it bears on the mapping between morphological features and tokens.
- The semantics of inflection and derivation is not treated in MRS terms anywhere in the chapter; caveat 5 is an inference from the chapter's general statement about compositionality.
- Morphological analysis for unknown words (guessing a lexical type and rule chain for a word not in the lexicon) is not discussed.
