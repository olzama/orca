# Chapter 24: Processing (Thomas Wasow)

Handbook pages 1081-1104 (text 1081-1097, references 1097-1104). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter argues that the architecture of HPSG fits what is known about human language processing, although little psycholinguistic research has used HPSG. Section 2 lists general facts about human processing: incrementality, non-modularity, the central role of individual words, the influence of context, and speed and accuracy. Section 3 matches them to design properties of HPSG: constraint-based, surface-oriented, informationally rich representations, lexicalism, and underspecification. Section 4 discusses two phenomena: island constraints, where the chapter favors hybrid accounts that split the work between grammar and processing, and the subject/object relative clause asymmetry, which the chapter explains with the SLASH-less analysis of subject relatives in Sag (1997).

The chapter is about human processing. It has little direct bearing on the parser design. It does not mention DELPH-IN, the ERG, LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, MRS, derivation trees, or dependency grammar. The only contacts with computational work are: the remark that HPSG was motivated by computational tractability and that the same grammar is used for parsing and generation (Section 1, p. 1084); the citation of Linardaki (2006) and Miyao and Tsujii (2008) for probabilistic HPSG (Section 1, p. 1083); and a footnote on early parsers built on transformational grammars (Section 3.1, p. 1088, footnote 6). Every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is therefore "not discussed" unless stated. All caveats are of low severity.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Grammars are categorical; preferences are probabilistic and may sit outside the grammar

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (division of labor between tagger and grammar; strict versus relaxed output)
- Reference: Section 1, pp. 1082-1084 (including footnotes 3 and 4)
- What the chapter says: Following Bresnan et al. (2001) and Hawkins (2004; 2014), a preference that is only a statistical tendency in one language (English: avoidance of third-person subjects with first- or second-person objects or obliques) can be a categorical requirement in another (Lummi). There are two ways to capture this. One gives up categorical grammars and attaches probabilities to feature values; the chapter calls a full probabilistic HPSG "a large undertaking" and points to Linardaki (2006) and Miyao and Tsujii (2008). The other keeps the grammar categorical and states the same generalization either in the grammar or as a probabilistic performance constraint, depending on the language. Footnote 4 reports that Ivan Sag believed grammatical generalizations should be categorical.
- Why it matters: The planned split (probabilistic tagger, categorical replay) corresponds to the second option. The caveat is for the multilingual goal: the same generalization may have to live in the grammar for one language and in the tagger for another, so the boundary between "strict failure" and "dispreferred" is language-specific.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter cites Miyao and Tsujii (2008) only as work "along these lines" and does not name any system.

### 2. Island constraints may be partly or wholly extra-grammatical

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (what a replay success or failure means)
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 1092-1094
- What the chapter says: Some HPSG proponents (Chaves 2012; 2021; Hofmeister and Sag 2010; Hofmeister et al. 2013; Chaves and Putnam 2020) attribute certain island phenomena entirely to extra-grammatical factors; some transformational grammarians (Phillips 2013; Sprouse et al. 2012) hold that islands are part of grammar. The chapter does not settle the dispute and proposes that languages differ in which islands are grammaticalized. Example: in Chaves (2012), the conjunct constraint follows from the architecture of HPSG, while the element constraint has exceptions and is attributed to extra-grammatical factors.
- Why it matters: Replay tests licensing by the grammar only. If a grammar does not encode an island, replay will license unacceptable extractions; if a grammar encodes an island that has exceptions (the element constraint), replay will reject attested sentences and send them to the relaxed path. Which islands a grammar encodes is a per-language decision. (The consequence for replay is my inference; the chapter does not discuss implemented grammars here.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Superficially similar strings have derivations that differ in SLASH

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding, UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 4.2, pp. 1095-1096 (example 5, footnote 12); Section 5, p. 1096 (example 6)
- What the chapter says: In Sag (1997), finite subject relative clauses involve no SLASH, and non-subject relative clauses have a non-empty SLASH value. In Pollard and Sag (1994: Section 4.3), "to please" has a non-empty SLASH in "Chris is easy to please" and an empty SLASH in "Chris is eager to please".
- Why it matters: The tagger has to choose between an extraction derivation and a non-extraction derivation for strings that look alike, on lexical grounds (easy versus eager) or on the position of the overt NP. For the UD bridge, the two kinds of relative clause would map to derivations of different shapes. (The UD consequence is my inference; the chapter does not mention dependencies.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. One syntactic structure can carry more than one meaning; disambiguation uses prosody and world knowledge

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics, deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 2.2, p. 1086 (example 2); Section 2.4, p. 1087; Section 2.5, p. 1087
- What the chapter says: "I forgot how good beer tastes" has two parses, distinguishable by stress and by preceding context. "Dogs must be carried" has two meanings with the same syntactic structure. Ambiguities "abound in normal sentences" (the chapter points to Wasow 2015 and to Bender and Emerson 2021, Section 2.2.2), yet comprehenders rarely notice them.
- Why it matters: For the first kind, the tagger must choose a derivation from text alone, without prosody and with limited context. For the second kind, a derivation tree does not determine a single reading, so the semantics obtained by replay must leave such readings open. The chapter does not say how; it does not mention MRS or scope underspecification.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed (only the cross-reference to Chapter 25 for ambiguity).

### 5. Incremental processing with partial meanings for initial substrings

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (processing order)
- Reference: Section 2.1, pp. 1084-1085; Section 3.5, p. 1092
- What the chapter says: Human production and comprehension proceed from the beginning to the end of the utterance, with anticipation of later material (German dative "dem Kind ... helfen"). A grammar that fits a model of language use should provide representations of initial substrings that can be assigned partial meanings. Underspecified feature structure descriptions make this easy for HPSG.
- Why it matters: This is a claim about human processing and about what the grammar formalism permits. The planned parser tags the whole sentence and then replays a complete tree, so it is not incremental in this sense. That is no problem for the stated goals; it matters only if the parser is later proposed as a psycholinguistic model or used for streaming input.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Unpronounced but syntactically represented elements

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 3.2, pp. 1089-1090; Section 3.3, p. 1091
- What the chapter says: Branigan and Pickering (2017) conclude from priming that syntactic representations are shallow and monostratal and that there is no support for "the syntactic representation of empty categories associated with the movement of constituents". The same paper is quoted as saying that "some elements that are not phonologically represented may be syntactically represented" and that some semantically specified elements are not specified syntactically. The chapter does not say which elements these are and does not reconcile the statements.
- Why it matters: The first statement supports the no-empty-elements assumption. The second leaves open that some unpronounced elements are syntactically real, which a trace-free grammar would have to handle by unary rules or lexical means. The chapter gives no HPSG analysis on this point.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- Constraint-based, no directionality (Section 3.1, p. 1088; Section 1, p. 1084): well-formedness is the simultaneous satisfaction of all constraints, so the same grammar serves parsing and generation. Order of rule application is not part of the grammar, which is compatible with replaying a given tree in whatever order the tree gives.
- Surface-oriented and monostratal (Section 3.2, pp. 1089-1090): no derivation of surface properties from abstract underlying structures, no movement, no second level of syntactic structure. This is in line with one derivation tree over the surface tokens.
- Local mother-daughter relations (Section 3.3, p. 1091): priming evidence (Branigan and Pickering 2017) supports syntactic representations that capture local relations between a mother and its daughters, independent of the larger context and of the internal structure of the daughters. This matches a derivation tree whose nodes are rule names.
- Lexicalism (Section 3.4, pp. 1091-1092): most of the information that licenses sentences is in lexical entries, organized in a hierarchy of lexical types; "it is largely the information coming from the words that determines the well-formedness of larger expressions". This supports a design in which a per-token label carries the lexical type. The chapter adds that the processor needs access to individual words and not just their types, since difficulty depends on word choice.
- Importance of words (Section 2.3, pp. 1086-1087): subcategorization preferences, semantic category, and frequency of individual words influence processing, which is consistent with making a lexically informed tagger carry the disambiguation.
- Categorical grammar plus separate probabilistic component (Section 1, p. 1083): the second option for the Performance-Grammar Correspondence Hypothesis is the same division of labor as tagger plus replay (see caveat 1).
- Informationally rich signs (Section 3.3, p. 1090): phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and context are in one feature structure, linked by coindexing, so semantics is available together with syntax. The chapter says this about representations, not about derivation trees or MRS.
- Computational tractability (Section 1, p. 1084): some design features of HPSG trace back to the need to run on the computers of the 1980s.

## Theory strains

- Categorical versus probabilistic grammar (Section 1, p. 1083, footnote 4): Wasow entertains probabilities on feature values; Sag held that grammatical generalizations should be categorical, and the idea was left out of their joint publications. A full probabilistic HPSG does not exist in worked-out form according to the chapter.
- Islands: grammar or processing (Section 4.1, pp. 1092-1094): disputed between HPSG proponents (Chaves, Hofmeister, Sag) and transformational grammarians (Phillips, Sprouse). Inside HPSG, the Coordinate Structure Constraint is split: the conjunct constraint is architectural, the element constraint is extra-grammatical (Chaves 2012). The chapter treats the status of each island as an empirical, language-specific question, which leaves the grammar's coverage of islands undetermined.
- Subject relatives without SLASH (Section 4.2, pp. 1095-1096, footnote 12): Sag (1997) treats finite subject relatives as fundamentally different from other relatives, "based entirely on distributional evidence"; the idea goes back to Gazdar (1981). The chapter presents it as fitting processing evidence for English; whether other languages have the same grammatical difference is left open. Competing explanations of the asymmetry are distance-based (Wanner and Maratsos 1978; Gibson 1998; Hawkins 2004), and the cross-linguistic evidence (Chinese, Japanese) is called "somewhat equivocal".
- Easy versus eager (Section 5, p. 1096): the SLASH analysis of "easy to please" in Pollard and Sag (1994: Section 4.3) is offered as an untested processing prediction.
- Evidence base (Section 3.1, p. 1089, footnote 8; Section 1, p. 1081): the priming-based conclusions of Branigan and Pickering (2017) that the chapter leans on are "controversial"; little psycholinguistic work has been guided by HPSG (Konieczny 1996 is the named exception).
- X-bar structure (Section 3.3, p. 1090): the list of lexical information in MacDonald et al. (1994) matches HPSG "with the possible exception of X-bar structure".
- Lexical decomposition (Section 3.4, p. 1092): "any lexical decomposition would have to be strongly motivated by the morphology", a constraint on analyses that the chapter states without discussion.
- Non-linguistic context in signs (Section 3.3, p. 1090): feature structures can incorporate social and other non-linguistic context, but "this has not been extensively explored".

## Not covered here

The following are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter says nothing about implemented grammars or parsers, parsing efficiency, derivation trees, MRS, or UD.
- It does not say how scope ambiguity of the "Dogs must be carried" kind is represented in HPSG semantics (underspecified semantics is the subject of other chapters).
- It does not discuss word order domains, discontinuous constituents, traces versus trace-free extraction, or the arity of rules, which are the points most relevant to the continuity, no-empty-elements, and binary-unary assumptions.
- It does not identify which unpronounced elements Branigan and Pickering regard as syntactically represented; that would need to be checked in their paper.
- Bouma is not cited in this chapter.
