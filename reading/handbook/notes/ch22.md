# Chapter 22: Semantics (Jean-Pierre Koenig and Frank Richter)

Handbook pages 1001-1042 (text 1001-1035, references 1035-1042). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter traces how semantic representations have been integrated into HPSG. It starts with the Situation Semantics of Pollard and Sag (1987), with restricted parameters and a case-based Semantics Principle, then covers the 1990s work on scope: Cooper storage for quantifiers (Pollard and Sag 1994, Pollard and Yoo 1998, Przepiórkowski 1998) and the scope of adjuncts under recursive modification (Kasper 1997). It then presents the sorting of semantic objects (used for linking) and the constructional semantics of Ginzburg and Sag (2000), where clause types carry message types and unary "type-shift" constructions are needed. The second half presents the two underspecification frameworks, Minimal Recursion Semantics (MRS) and Lexical Resource Semantics (LRS), with a short mention of Glue Semantics for HPSG.

The chapter is theoretical. It does not name the ERG, LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, or the Grammar Matrix, and it says nothing about parsing algorithms. Its contacts with implementation are: MRS "developed out of computational semantic engineering considerations related to machine translation" in Verbmobil (Section 6.1.1, p. 1019); scope resolution for MRS is argued to be better "left to a separate algorithm" outside the grammar (p. 1021); Robust MRS can be used with shallow analyses (footnote 5, p. 1020); Frank and Reyle implemented Underspecified DRT in HPSG (p. 1019); and Glue Semantics has "highly efficient techniques for semantic derivation already implemented for LFG" (p. 1019). The "DELPH-IN stance" entries below report only these statements.

For the planned parser the chapter is mostly reassuring for the English/ERG case (MRS composition is simple and tied to the construction type) and is a warning for the many-languages goal: under two of the semantic theories described (Cooper storage, LRS) a derivation tree does not determine the semantics.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Cooper storage: the syntactic tree does not determine where quantifiers are retrieved

- Severity: low for the ERG (which uses MRS), high for any grammar that follows Pollard and Sag (1994) or Pollard and Yoo (1998)
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / semantics
- Reference: Section 3.1, pp. 1005-1009 (Figure 1, principles 4a-b, constraint 6, feature geometry 9, constraints 10-11)
- What the chapter says: each quantifier starts in QSTORE and at every node is either retrieved (RETRIEVED list) or passed up. Scope readings "correspond to the various nodes at which the quantifier can be retrieved from storage". RETRIEVED is any list over a subset of the daughters' stores (4a), and "the order of two quantifiers in the same RETRIEVED list (i.e. retrieved at the same node) is unconstrained", so the constraints "underdetermine quantifier scope" (p. 1007). The chapter presents this as a virtue: "no syntactic ambiguity needs to be posited to account for what is a strictly semantic phenomenon".
- Why it matters: in such a grammar one derivation tree (one sequence of rule names) is compatible with several feature structures that differ in RETRIEVED, QSTORE and QUANTS. Replay by unification with no search would stop at an underspecified structure or would have to enumerate retrieval choices. The tree is then not a complete recipe for the semantics.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed for DELPH-IN grammars. Within the storage tradition, the chapter reports that Przepiórkowski (1998) proposed "a strictly lexicalized retrieval mechanism which removes structural ambiguities arising from different possible retrieval sites for quantifiers along a syntactic head path" (p. 1009). The chapter's account of MRS (caveat 2) is the alternative that avoids retrieval altogether.

### 2. MRS obtained from the grammar is underspecified; scoped readings need a separate step

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 6.1.1, pp. 1019-1021 (examples 31-33); Section 6.1.2, pp. 1021-1023 (structures 34, 36, Figure 4)
- What the chapter says: the grammar fixes the restriction of each quantifier and some outscoping relations through qeq constraints, and leaves the BODY of quantifiers open. Resolving scope "amounts to adding an HCONS". "Although it is possible to use a typed feature structure formalism to resolve scope ambiguities, Copestake et al. (1995: 309-311) argue that it is more efficient for the relevant resolution process not to be part of the grammar and be left to a separate algorithm" (p. 1021).
- Why it matters: this is the form of "MRS as a by-product" that the design can count on: one underspecified MRS per derivation, with no scope decisions inside replay. A consumer that needs fully scoped formulas needs a separate resolution component. The chapter gives no algorithm for it.
- DELPH-IN stance: scope resolution is kept outside the grammar, on the argument of Copestake et al. (1995). No system is named.

### 3. LRS: one syntactic structure admits several semantic values, fixed only at the utterance level

- Severity: medium (none for the ERG; high for reuse of the replay engine with LRS-based grammars)
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / semantics
- Reference: Section 6.2, p. 1026; Section 6.2.1, pp. 1027-1033 (structure 44, principles 45a-c, Figures 5-7); Section 6.2.2, pp. 1033-1034
- What the chapter says: in LRS "grammar principles may admit a large number of structures, which in this case can be multiple semantic representations compatible with one and the same syntactic structure"; the readings of a two-quantifier sentence are "distinct possible values of a semantics feature" (p. 1026). "An ambiguous utterance receives structural analyses that are potentially only distinguished by different EXCONT values of their root node" (p. 1028). The principles use relations that are not plain unification: subterm constraints, "all and only" conditions on the PARTS list, and an utterance-level clause of the EXCONT Principle. In Figure 6 the identity of the quantifier's restrictor and scope with the available terms is "determined at the utterance level by the lack of other material" (p. 1031). The representation language is encoded in Relational Speciate Reentrant Language (p. 1033).
- Why it matters: a derivation tree is not a deterministic recipe for an LRS semantics. Replay would need constraint solving over subterm relations and a closure step at the root, and it would return a set of readings.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter presents MRS and LRS as "two competing approaches" (p. 1019) and says nothing about implementations of LRS.

### 4. Identity of semantic contributions across words (negative concord, polyadic quantifiers)

- Severity: medium for the many-languages goal, low for English
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 6.2.1, p. 1027 and pp. 1031-1033 (Figure 7, example 47)
- What the chapter says: "As a special property setting LRS apart from other semantic underspecification frameworks", LRS allows the contributions of different lexemes to be one and the same object. In Polish "nikt nie przyszedł" the negation of the n-word and the negation of the verb are identified, giving the single-negation reading; a principle requires that a negated finite verb be in the scope of at most one negation in its EXCONT. In "Every unicorn prefers different meadows", "every" and "different" jointly build one polyadic quantifier, a reading that "cannot be expressed by two independent monadic quantifiers" (Keenan 1992). The chapter says such functors "cannot be semantically decomposed or cannot be decomposed within the structural limits of a surface-oriented syntax" (p. 1032).
- Why it matters: MRS composition as described in (39) appends the RELS of the daughters, so each lexical contribution stays a separate predication. The chapter offers these cases as something LRS does and other underspecification frameworks do not. A multilingual parser that takes MRS as its only semantic output inherits that limit for negative concord languages, unless the grammar handles concord by other means. The chapter gives no MRS analysis of these data.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Unary constructions whose only function is semantic (Ginzburg and Sag 2000)

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (unary chains at phrase level with no surface cue) / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 1016-1018 (constructions 22, 28, 29; examples 26-27; footnote 4)
- What the chapter says: the constructional analysis "adds to the 'traditional' subject-predicate construction a type-shift unary rule that maps a state of affairs description onto a proposition" (p. 1016). In situ interrogatives ("You'll be leaving WHEN exactly?") are syntactically declarative head-subject clauses and need a further unary construction (is-inter-cl and its subsort dir-is-inter-cl) that retrieves the stored parameter and turns the proposition into a question. "Assigning distinct messages to different clause types while maintaining a surface-oriented approach requires quite a few such unary branching constructions whose function is strictly semantic" (p. 1018). Reprise and non-reprise uses of in situ wh-phrases (26 vs. 27) are distinct subsorts over the same kind of string.
- Why it matters: these are unary rules in the derivation, which the tree encoding can name, so replay is unaffected. The tagger must predict phrase-level unary steps that leave no trace in the string, and must choose between subsorts on discourse grounds (reprise or not). Such nodes have no counterpart in a dependency tree, so the direction from UD to derivations has to supply them (the chapter says nothing about dependencies; this consequence is my inference).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed for DELPH-IN. Footnote 4 (p. 1018) reports that Müller (2015) argues a surface-oriented grammar need not rely on a hierarchy of clause types: "appropriate lexical specifications on verbs (as the heads of sentences) and phrasal principles that exploit the local internal structure of a sentence's immediate daughters can be used to achieve the same effects".

### 6. In situ wh-parameters are free to be retrieved in any higher clause

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / semantics
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 1016-1017 (examples 23-24, Filler Inclusion Constraint 25)
- What the chapter says: a wh-phrase that is the filler of a head-filler phrase must contribute its parameter to the PARAMS of that clause (25). The parameter of an in situ wh-phrase is "free either to be retrieved in the clause in which [it] occur[s] or to be retrieved in a higher clause". This yields the Baker (1970) readings of "Who wondered who saw what?".
- Why it matters: this is the storage problem of caveat 1 for wh-parameters. With two interrogative clauses and an in situ wh-phrase, the same tree is compatible with more than one PARAMS assignment, unless the retrieval site is recorded by a rule name in the derivation.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Semantic composition is case-based: coarse syntactic schemas do not fix the semantics

- Severity: medium for the "relaxed" fallback, low for strict replay
- Assumption affected: semantics (relaxed output) / other
- Reference: Section 1, pp. 1001-1002; Section 2, pp. 1004-1005 (principle 3); Section 3.2, p. 1009 (principle 13); Section 6.1.2, pp. 1023-1024 (rule 39); Section 6.2.1, p. 1029; Section 6.2.2, p. 1034
- What the chapter says: "only the spirit of the rule-to-rule approach is adhered to, as there can be more than one semantic operation per class of syntactic structures" (pp. 1001-1002). "Syntactic combinatorics is less varied than semantic combinatorics", and "one cannot maintain a surface-oriented approach to syntax ... without resorting to case-based definitions of the semantic import of syntactic combinatorics" (pp. 1004-1005). In MRS the HOOK of a phrase comes from "the semantic head daughter, which is determined uniquely for each construction type", and this clause "amounts to a case-based definition, as is true of all Semantics Principles since Pollard and Sag (1987)"; Copestake et al. (2005) "only discuss intersective and scopal constructions" (p. 1023). In LRS the clauses of the Semantics Principle dispatch on the logical type of the daughter (generalized quantifier or not).
- Why it matters: for strict replay this is harmless, because the fine-grained rule name selects the case. For the relaxed output composed from coarse universal schemas it is a real constraint: a schema such as head-adjunct or head-complement does not say whether composition is intersective or scopal, or which daughter is the semantic head. The relaxed schemas need at least that distinction if they are to return usable semantics.
- DELPH-IN stance: the MRS composition rule (39) ties the semantic head to the construction type. Nothing further is said about particular grammars.

### 8. Head-adjunct structures: syntactic head and semantic head differ

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / semantics
- Reference: Section 3.2, pp. 1009-1012 (principles 13 and 17, structures 15-16, 18-19, Figure 2)
- What the chapter says: in head-complement and head-subject structures syntactic and semantic headedness match; "in the case of head-adjunct structures, syntactic and semantic headedness do not match" (p. 1009). Under Pollard and Sag (1994) the content of the phrase is that of the adjunct daughter, which gives the wrong meaning for "a potentially controversial plan" (the most deeply embedded adjunct gets widest scope). Kasper (1997) repairs this with internal and external content (ICONT, ECONT) inside MOD.
- Why it matters: a conversion between derivations and dependency trees that reads the semantic functor off the syntactic head will be wrong for scopal modifiers, and nested modifiers ("potentially" inside "controversial" inside the noun phrase) need the bracketing to be right for the scope to be right. The chapter does not discuss dependencies; the link to UD is my inference. For the relaxed output this is another instance of caveat 7.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed directly. The MRS section says intersective adjectival modification is a subsort of an "intersective_phrase" rule that identifies the labels of modifier and head (p. 1025) and that Copestake et al. (2005) separate intersective and scopal constructions (p. 1023).

### 9. Scope of raised and extracted quantifiers below their surface position

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics / no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 3.1, pp. 1007-1009 (examples 7-8)
- What the chapter says: Pollard and Sag (1994) cannot give "a unicorn" narrow scope in "A unicorn appears to be approaching", because "raised arguments only occur once, in their surface position" and quantifiers are never retrieved lower than that. The same holds for the narrow reading of "Five books, I believe John read", since a filler shares only LOCAL with the gap. Pollard and Yoo (1998) move storage into LOCAL and restrict retrieval to semantically potent heads; their analysis "only covers trace-based extraction". Przepiórkowski's (1998) version "is compatible with trace-based and traceless analyses of extraction" (p. 1009).
- Why it matters: one published solution depends on traces, which the design excludes. The chapter does not say how these readings come out in MRS, so whether the underspecified MRS of the ERG covers them has to be checked elsewhere.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 10. Small points in the LRS section that touch the tree assumptions

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / continuity
- Reference: Section 6.2.1, p. 1027 (footnote 8), p. 1031
- What the chapter says: "Lexical items may be phrasal" (footnote 8). An extraposed relative clause belonging to "every dog" "could consistently contribute its meaning representation to the restrictor" of the quantifier from outside the noun phrase (p. 1031).
- Why it matters: in LRS a lexical resource need not be a single token, and semantic material can join a quantifier's restrictor without being inside its syntactic constituent. Both are properties of LRS analyses and not of MRS as presented here.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- One stratum, one tree, semantics on every node. "Every node in a syntactic tree includes all appropriate levels of structure" (Section 1, p. 1001); "HPSG is monostratal" (Section 2, p. 1004); compositionality is "directly incorporated by principles that regulate the value of the mother's SEM attribute, given the SEM values of the daughters and their mode of syntactic combination" (p. 1004). This is the property that makes semantics a by-product of replaying a tree.
- MRS composition is simple and tied to the rule. Rule (39), pp. 1023-1024: RELS and HCONS of the mother are the appended lists of the daughters, the HOOK comes from the semantic head, "which is determined uniquely for each construction type", and one slot of the semantic head is identified with the other daughter's HOOK. "Semantic composition is particularly simple" (p. 1024). Given the rule name, there is nothing to search.
- Scope ambiguity is not derivational ambiguity. Storage and MRS both avoid positing syntactic ambiguity for scope (p. 1007, pp. 1020-1021). The tagger never has to choose a scope reading, and one tag sequence corresponds to one underspecified MRS.
- Scope resolution belongs outside the grammar (Copestake et al. 1995, reported p. 1021), so the replay engine does not need it.
- Robust MRS for degraded analyses. Footnote 5, p. 1020: RMRS (Copestake 2007) contributes arguments through independent elementary predications and "can be profitably used with shallower analyses, 'including part-of-speech tagging, noun phrase chunking and stochastic parsers which operate without detailed lexicon'"; Peldszus and Schlangen (2012) use it for incremental construction of meaning representations. This is a ready-made target format for the "relaxed" output.
- Syntactic structure is not expected to mirror semantic structure. Section 7, p. 1034: the "relative independence of syntactic and semantic structure" is a common denominator of all HPSG semantic theories. A surface tree with no movement is the normal case, so the continuity assumption is in line with the semantics chapter.
- Semantic work can be moved into the lexicon. Przepiórkowski's lexicalized retrieval (p. 1009) and Müller's (2015) lexical alternative to clause-type constructions (footnote 4, p. 1018) both reduce the number of semantic decisions that are not visible in the derivation.
- Sorted semantic relations give compact linking constraints (Section 4, pp. 1012-1013, constraint 20), which is useful for inspectable grammars across languages.
- Flat representations were motivated by cross-language comparability (Section 6.1.1, pp. 1019-1020: "fierce black cat" and "gato negro y feroz" get the same bag of predications), which fits the multilingual aim.

## Theory strains

- The Semantics Principle has always been a list of cases. Pollard and Sag (1987) distinguish quantifier and non-quantifier complements; Pollard and Sag (1994) add a head-adjunct case; MRS has a clause per construction type; LRS dispatches on logical type. The chapter treats this as the price of a simple syntax (Sections 1-2, pp. 1001-1005; Section 6.1.2, p. 1023). Glue Semantics is reported to remove the need to distinguish cases by the semantic type of the daughters (Section 6, p. 1019).
- Quantifier scope went through four mechanisms. Pollard and Sag (1987) could model only left-to-right scope and say so themselves; Pollard and Sag (1994) add Cooper storage and cannot get narrow scope for raised or extracted quantifiers; Pollard and Yoo (1998) change the feature geometry (QSTORE and POOL in LOCAL) and cover only trace-based extraction; Przepiórkowski (1998) lexicalizes retrieval. The chapter calls examples (7)-(8) an illustration of "the complexities of combining a surface-oriented approach to syntax with a descriptively adequate model of semantic composition" (Section 3.1, pp. 1005-1009).
- Adjunct semantics in Pollard and Sag (1994) gives wrong results for recursive modification. Kasper's (1997) repair adds ICONT and ECONT inside MOD; Asudeh and Crouch (2002) use Glue and Egg (2004) uses underspecification for the same problem (Section 3.2, pp. 1009-1012).
- Constructional clause types need semantic-only unary constructions. Ginzburg and Sag (2000) require "quite a few such unary branching constructions whose function is strictly semantic"; Müller (2015) argues the same effects follow from lexical specifications on verbs and phrasal principles, with no hierarchy of clause types (Section 5, pp. 1016-1018, footnote 4).
- Question meaning: Ginzburg and Sag's propositional abstracts with parameters versus Hamblin-style sets of answers (Hamblin 1973; Groenendijk and Stokhof 1997), and a message ontology richer than the proposition/illocutionary force division of Searle (1969) (Section 5, pp. 1014-1015).
- MRS versus LRS versus Glue. MRS and LRS are "two competing approaches" (Section 6, p. 1019). MRS encodes underspecified representations as feature structures; LRS uses the feature logic to underspecify fully specific formulas of a standard logic (Section 6.2, p. 1026).
- The chapter's criticism of MRS. Flatness "comes at a cost": representations are "cluttered with uninterpretable symbols (handles)" and "do not correspond to sub-pieces of a well-formed formula"; model-theoretic interpretation requires hooks and holes, "abstract objects of dubious semantic import", unlike the abstract objects of DRT, which are built from interpretable ones. Label identity in "white horse" is "a consequence of the semantic representation language", not of truth conditions (Section 6.1.2, pp. 1024-1026).
- Phenomena claimed to be beyond standard composition over a surface-oriented syntax: negative concord and irreducibly polyadic quantification, which LRS handles by identifying or jointly constructing contributions of several words. Richter (2016) and Lahm (2016) give competing LRS accounts of "different" (Section 6.2.1, pp. 1031-1033).
- The original choice of Situation Semantics is described as "probably somewhat a matter of happenstance" (Section 2, p. 1002), and later work replaced it with several other representation languages (Ty2, Montague's Intensional Logic, MRS).
- Against Mainstream Generative Grammar: Quantifier Raising and the assumption that constituency mirrors semantic constituency require multiple strata, with "little evidence that quantifier scope affects surface syntactic structure" (Section 3, p. 1005; Section 7, pp. 1034-1035). The chapter also admits that HPSG work before the late 1990s did "not have too much new insight to contribute" on the syntax-semantics interface beyond showing that a surface-oriented grammar can get things right (Section 5, p. 1013).
- Negative result on rigor in LRS practice: publications use logical expressions with metavariables instead of literal feature-logic descriptions, since the latter "are very cumbersome to read and write" (Section 6.2.2, p. 1034).

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks on what the chapter leaves out, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter does not describe how MRS is built in the ERG or any DELPH-IN grammar: no C-CONT (semantic contributions of constructions), no account of which rules add predications, no treatment of unary rules in MRS composition beyond the general rule (39). The claim that MRS falls out of replay has to be verified against Copestake et al. (2005) and the grammar itself.
- No named systems (LKB, ACE, PET, TRALE, Enju) and nothing about whether LRS has been implemented.
- No discussion of dependency representations derived from MRS (for example DMRS or EDS) or of any relation between HPSG semantics and dependency treebanks, so the chapter gives no direct input to the UD bridge. The points on the UD bridge in caveats 5 and 8 are my inferences.
- Whether MRS accounts for narrow scope of raised or extracted quantifiers (examples 7-8), negative concord, or polyadic readings is not stated; the chapter discusses those only for storage and LRS.
- Argument linking, binding, information structure and anaphora are deferred to other chapters (9, 20, 23).
- Robust or partial semantics for failed parses is mentioned only through RMRS in a footnote; there is no discussion of composing semantics from fragments.
