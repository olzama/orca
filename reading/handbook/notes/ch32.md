# Chapter 32: HPSG and Construction Grammar (Stefan Müller)

Handbook pages 1497-1553 (text 1497-1536, then references).

## Summary

The chapter argues that HPSG is a Construction Grammar (CxG): it is surface-oriented, pairs form with meaning at every level, needs no innate domain-specific knowledge, and organizes lexical items, lexical rules, and phrasal schemata in one inheritance hierarchy. It compares Constructional HPSG (Sag 1997) with Sign-Based Construction Grammar (SBCG) and criticizes several SBCG design choices (the MOTHER feature, a single valence list, dropping the Head Feature Principle and the LOCAL feature, selectable FORM values). Section 2 defends lexical valence against phrasal argument structure constructions with four arguments (derivational morphology, partial verb phrase fronting, coercion, non-predictable valence). Section 3 presents Riehemann's type-based analysis of German *-bar* derivation, and Section 4 shows two areas that do need phrasal constructions: the headless NPN construction and the family of filler-gap sub-constructions.

**Bearing on the parser: moderate.** Most of the chapter is framework comparison. The parts that matter are the passages on empty elements, the n-ary headless NPN construction, non-local idioms, discontinuity, and lexical rules. The chapter says very little about implemented grammars; where it does, it is noted below.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Empty elements are used in parts of the HPSG literature and are defended as sometimes preferable

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements (and leaves-equal-tokens)
- Reference: Section 1.2.1, pp. 1502-1504; Section 4.2, p. 1535
- Description: The chapter states that "most of the alternative theories do use empty elements" and cites Bender (2001) and Sag, Wasow and Bender (2003: 464) for HPSG/SBCG. It says that grammars with empty elements "often are more compact than those without empty elements and express generalizations more directly", with Bender (2001) on copulaless sentences in African American Vernacular English and Müller (2004) on nounless NPs in German as examples. Müller proposes a weakened Tenet 3: empty elements are forbidden unless there is language-internal evidence for them. Pollard and Sag (1994: Chapter 5) used empty heads for relative clauses (p. 1535). A grammar written this way has leaves that are no tokens, so its derivations do not fit the tree encoding without a prior conversion.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed for DELPH-IN grammars as such. The chapter says that any phrase structure grammar with empty elements can be translated into one without them (Bar-Hillel, Perles and Shamir 1961: 153, Lemma 4.1), and that trace-free versions of HPSG exist (Bouma et al. 2001; Sag 2010: 508). Sag (2010) replaces the empty relativizers by a family of filler-head sub-constructions (Section 4.2).

### 2. The NPN construction: headless, n-ary, with an unbounded daughters list

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules (also UD-bridge and tree-encoding)
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 1530-1534, example (40); footnote 1, p. 1497; footnote 28, p. 1532
- Description: Bargmann's (2015) analysis of *student after student*, *day after day after day* is a single phrasal construction whose DTRS list is a first nominal daughter followed by "an arbitrarily long list of *after* N pairs" (a Kleene-plus over pairs). No daughter is the head: the mother is a third-person singular non-count nominal, the daughters are count nouns, and the preposition is not treated as head. Müller argues explicitly that a binary head-driven treatment (*day after [day [after day]]*) would need "several highly idiosyncratic lexical items" and projection of nominal information through the preposition. The construction also enforces identity of the nouns and of their adjectives (*bad day after bad day*, \**bad day after awful day*), which is a constraint across non-adjacent daughters. Footnote 1 notes that HPSG "is not entirely head-driven anymore". For the parser: an n-ary rule with variable arity does not fit a binary tree encoding directly, and a headless phrase gives the UD conversion no head daughter to project. Footnote 28 adds that the category of the result (NP or N-bar with obligatorily dropped determiner) is itself unsettled.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed. The chapter gives only the theoretical analysis; it does not say how any implemented grammar treats NPN.

### 3. Flat head-complement structures in theoretical HPSG

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules
- Reference: Section 1.3.1, p. 1513, example (8) and footnote 18; Section 1.3.2.1, p. 1514, example (10); Section 1.3.2.3, p. 1516, example (12)
- Description: The Head-Complement Schema of Sag (1997) and the SBCG head-complement constructions license a head with all its complements as sisters (NON-HEAD-DTRS is a list of n elements). Footnote 18 says the schema "licenses flat structures" and points to Chapter 10 for binary branching versions. Grammars or treebanks that follow the flat schemata need binarization before the tree encoding applies.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed.

### 4. Constructions that span more than a local tree (idioms, stored chunks)

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (rule names per node), deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 1.2.4, pp. 1506-1509; Section 1.3, pp. 1510-1511
- Description: In the original CxG definition, constructions "may span wider ranges of the sentential tree" than a mother and her daughters. Constructional HPSG (signs contain their daughters) can state such multi-level descriptions directly, as TAG does for *take into account* (Figure 1). Richter and Sailer (2009) analyze the German idiom *glauben, X tritt ein Pferd* with a partly fixed non-local configuration. If a grammar licenses an analysis through such a multi-level construction, a derivation tree labeled with one local rule name per node does not record it, and replay of the local rules alone would not impose it. Müller shows that Richter and Sailer's restrictions are too strict (example 6) and concludes that "it is an open question whether there are idioms that make nonlocal Constructions necessary".
- DELPH-IN stance: The local alternative is credited to the CSLI grammar: the idiom theory of Sag (2007), Kay, Sag and Flickinger (2015), and Kay and Michaelis (2017) works by a series of local selections of specialized lexical items via LEXICAL IDENTIFIER (LID), and footnote 10 (p. 1508) says this "was part of the grammar fragment that has been developed at the CSLI by Dan Flickinger" years before SBCG. The XARG feature (Bender and Flickinger 1999) is named as the device that makes one argument visible at the top of a projection (p. 1515).

### 5. Discontinuous realization of a head and its dependents

- Severity: medium for German and other long-term targets; low for English
- Assumption affected: continuity (also UD-bridge)
- Reference: Section 1.3, p. 1511; Section 2, p. 1522, examples (20)-(21); Section 2.2, pp. 1524-1525, examples (24)-(25)
- Description: Three points. (a) Embodied Construction Grammar allows discontinuous constituents and is called "a notational variant of Reape-style HPSG" (Reape 1994), so a discontinuous-constituent strand of HPSG exists; the chapter refers to Chapter 10, Section 6 for it. (b) German partial verb phrase fronting (*Erzählen wird er seiner Tochter ein Märchen können*) spreads the arguments of one verb over the prefield and the middle field; the fronted and unfronted arguments "must add up to the total set of arguments selected by the verb". The solutions cited keep trees but use Categorial-Grammar-like valence (Höhle 2019; Müller 1996; Meurers 1999), so the dependents of a verb are saturated by a different head higher up, which the UD conversion has to undo. (c) *Mary tossed me a juice and Peter a water*: *tossed* and *Peter a water* are discontinuous yet instantiate the ditransitive; the chapter uses this against phrasal patterns and gives no analysis of the coordination here.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed. For English the chapter's own lexical analysis of *Kim really loves Sandy* is continuous: verb and object form a VP, the adverb attaches to the VP.

### 6. Valence-changing lexical rules multiply what a token's label must encode

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (label inventory)
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 1521-1522; Section 2.1, pp. 1523-1524; Section 2.3, pp. 1525-1526; Section 3, pp. 1527-1530
- Description: HPSG treats resultatives (*She fished the pond empty*), benefactive ditransitives (*He baked her a cake*, *She smiled herself an upgrade*), passive, and *-bar* derivation by lexical rules (lexical constructions) that change valence and add meaning. Rules feed each other: the resultative rule's output is input to *-bar* derivation (*leerfischbar*). The base lexical type of a verb therefore does not determine its valence in context; the tagger has to predict the right chain of lexical rules, and productive coercion means verbs occur with chains unseen for that lemma in training. Derivation also has subregular and irregular members (*entschuldbar* drops *-ig*; *essbar*, *brennbar*, *unentrinnbar*; 7% of Riehemann's adjectives are exceptions), handled by subtypes in a hierarchy, so a derived word may be either a rule output or a listed item.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed as an engine matter. Briscoe and Copestake (1999) are cited for a lexical rule that maps transitive *bake* to a ditransitive and adds benefactive semantics, and for pairing lexical rules with probabilities to model productivity.

### 7. Phonological (FORM) information can be selected

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (what information the leaves must carry)
- Reference: Section 1.3.2.5, pp. 1518-1519, examples (14)-(15); Section 1.3.2.6, pp. 1519-1520
- Description: The English *a*/*an* alternation depends on the phonology of the following nominal. SBCG makes FORM selectable; Constructional HPSG keeps PHON outside SYNSEM so that heads cannot select phonology. Müller mentions two options: a selection feature for determiners, or phrase-level phonological constraints. Either way, replay of a grammar that models *a*/*an* needs a phonological onset property on the token feature structures.
- DELPH-IN stance: The *a*/*an* example is attributed to Flickinger (mail to the HPSG mailing list, 2016); the chapter says "the distinction can be modeled by assuming a selection feature for determiners" but does not describe the ERG mechanism.

### 8. Constructions contribute meaning of their own, sometimes non-compositionally

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics (MRS as a by-product)
- Reference: Section 1.1, pp. 1499-1500; Section 2.3, p. 1526; Section 4.1, pp. 1531-1532; Section 4.2, p. 1535
- Description: Phrasal and lexical constructions add semantics that no daughter carries: the benefactive lexical rule, the wh-exclamative sub-construction, and the NPN construction, whose meaning (a plurality with an order relation) is stated on the mother while the mother is syntactically singular (a syntax-semantics mismatch). Bargmann's NPN semantics is given as a lambda term under a non-standard SR feature, with no MRS version in the chapter. Replay yields this meaning only if every such construction is a named rule with its own semantic contribution; the relaxed fallback with coarse universal schemas would lose it.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed. The chapter notes only that SBCG's Frame Semantics representations "are based on MRS" (Section 1.3.2.7, p. 1520).

### 9. HPSG variants differ in feature geometry and in formal devices

- Severity: low (matters only when grammars other than DELPH-IN ones are loaded)
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (engine assumptions), other
- Reference: Sections 1.3.2.1-1.3.2.6, pp. 1514-1520
- Description: Constructional HPSG signs contain their daughters; SBCG constructions have MOTHER and DTRS and signs have no daughters. SBCG has one VAL list, SYN/SEM in place of SYNSEM/LOCAL, and no Head Feature Principle; identity is stated per construction with a "!" notation that shares everything except the features named. Müller argues the "!" device "involves some non-trivial machinery in the background" (the type must be shared without structure sharing; it is unclear how it stays monotonic). A plain unification engine cannot take such a description as is. Some analyses depend on model-theoretic totality (unconstrained FORM of a gap, or unconstrained CONT in the *-bar* schema, "would be compatible with infinitely many models"), a concern that does not arise in the same way in a unification-based replay.
- DELPH-IN stance: Footnote 26 (p. 1527) says that Copestake and Briscoe (1992), Briscoe and Copestake (1999), and Meurers (2001) represent lexical rules with IN and OUT features, which "actually corresponds to the MOTHER/DTRS format of SBCG".

## Support for the design

- **Surface orientation.** Tenet 3 ("no underlying levels of syntax or any phonologically empty elements") is one HPSG can meet: "all grammars can be turned into grammars without empty elements" (Section 1.2.1, pp. 1503-1504), and trace-free HPSG exists (Bouma et al. 2001; Sag 2010).
- **Lexicalism makes the lexical label informative.** "Argument structure is encoded lexically" in Constructional HPSG and SBCG (Section 1.3.3, p. 1521); valence is non-predictable from semantics and must be stated per lexical item (Section 2.4, pp. 1526-1527). This is the property a tagger of lexical types exploits. The chapter draws the parallel to Lexicalized TAG, "where a rich syntactic structure is associated to a lexical anchor".
- **Lexical rules are unary constructions.** Valence alternations, passive, and derivation are lexical rules, i.e., unary steps over a lexical item (Sections 2 and 3). The IN/OUT (or MOTHER/DTRS) format of lexical rules matches a unary node in a derivation tree (footnote 26, p. 1527).
- **Local licensing.** In SBCG every sign is licensed as the mother of a local construct (the definition in (11), p. 1515), and idioms can be handled by chains of local selection (pp. 1507-1508). A tree whose nodes name local rules is then a complete record.
- **Continuity for English adverb placement.** The lexical approach analyzes *Kim really loves Sandy* with a continuous VP (p. 1522).
- **One type hierarchy for lexical items, lexical rules, and phrasal schemata** (Section 1.2.3, pp. 1505-1506). Specific constructions are subtypes of general schemata, for example the filler-gap sub-constructions under one Filler-Head Schema (Section 4.2, p. 1535). This is the kind of structure a fallback from specific rules to coarse schemas can follow.
- **Dependencies as the primitive.** Müller concludes that what is acquired is "the fact that there are dependencies between certain elements in phrases" (p. 1522), which is congenial to a dependency bridge.
- **Weights.** Adding weights or probabilities to HPSG "has been done (Brew 1995; Briscoe & Copestake 1999; Miyao & Tsujii 2008), and it should be done to a larger extent" (p. 1511).

## Theory strains

- **MOTHER feature and locality (SBCG vs. Constructional HPSG).** Section 1.3.2.1, pp. 1514-1515; Section 1.3.2.5, pp. 1518-1519. SBCG removes daughters from signs to restrict selection; Müller argues the restriction is empty because structure sharing (XARG) circumvents it, and that it brings new problems. Van Eynde's Big Mess analysis, which constrains the phrasal type of a daughter, cannot be transferred to SBCG.
- **Stored chunks.** Section 1.2.4, p. 1509; Section 1.3, p. 1511. Humans store chunks with internal structure; Constructional HPSG and TAG can represent them, SBCG (and GB, Minimalism, CG, LFG, DG) cannot.
- **One valence list vs. SPR/COMPS.** Section 1.3.2.2, p. 1516. Sag (2012) returns to a single list "despite arguments ... by Borsley (1987)"; "an argument for this was never given". Generalizations over maximal projections (German extraposition) are lost.
- **Head Feature Principle vs. the "!" notation.** Section 1.3.2.3, pp. 1516-1517. The notation is not obviously monotonic and needs unstated machinery.
- **Selectable phonology.** Sections 1.3.2.5-1.3.2.6, pp. 1518-1520. SBCG lets heads see the phonology of raised subjects and, unless SYN/SEM/STORE are shared piecewise, of fillers. Sag's piecewise sharing abandons the original motivation of LOCAL and leaves the gap's FORM unconstrained, to be stipulated as empty.
- **Mutual selection of determiner and noun.** Footnote 20, p. 1518: Pollard and Sag (1994) have the noun select the determiner via SPR and the determiner select the noun via SPEC.
- **Semantic role labels.** Section 1.3.2.7, pp. 1520-1521 and footnote 21. Predicate-specific roles (KISSER/KISSEE in Pollard and Sag; INGESTOR in SBCG) block generalizations that AGENT/PATIENT or ACTOR/UNDERGOER allow.
- **Lexical vs. phrasal argument structure.** Section 2, pp. 1521-1527. HPSG and SBCG against Goldberg and most CxG variants, phrasal LFG, and Embodied and Fluid CxG. Croft (2003) calls it "a false dichotomy"; Müller maintains that real differences remain.
- **Empty elements.** Section 1.2.1, pp. 1503-1504. CxG bans them; Müller proposes a weaker ban and holds that grammars with them can state generalizations more directly.
- **Non-local idioms.** Section 1.2.4, pp. 1508-1509. Richter and Sailer's (2009) non-local analysis vs. local selection; the empirical basis is disputed (example 6), and the question is left open.
- **NPN construction.** Section 4.1, pp. 1530-1534. Phrasal headless construction (Bargmann 2015, after Jackendoff 2008) vs. head-based or reduplication accounts (G. Müller 2011; Travis 2003), which are argued to fail. Whether the result is NP or N-bar is unresolved (footnote 28). Hudson's Word Grammar account needs extra primitives (footnote 29).
- **Filler-gap constructions.** Section 4.2, pp. 1534-1535. One schema plus empty heads (Pollard and Sag 1994) vs. a family of sub-constructions (Sag 1997; 2010). Borsley (2006): the empty-head route needs many empty heads.
- **The -bar schema.** Section 3, p. 1528, footnote 27. The type *trans-verb* on the input is redundant and, given productive particle verbs, wrong; whether to share the index or the whole CONT of the accusative object depends on formal assumptions and on the structural/lexical case distinction.
- **Formal status of earlier CxG.** Section 1.3, p. 1510. Berkeley CxG unification "was formally inconsistent"; the differences between Constructional HPSG and SBCG are "to some extent cosmetic".

## Not covered here

The following are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter does not say how the ERG or any other implemented grammar analyzes NPN, the *the -er the -er* construction, *let alone*, or the *way* construction. Whether the ERG has binary rules for these needs to be checked in the grammar itself.
- The chapter does not mention C-CONT or any other mechanism by which a DELPH-IN rule contributes relations to MRS, although this is what caveat 8 depends on in practice.
- The chapter does not discuss how n-ary or variable-arity constructions are binarized in implementations, nor that DELPH-IN formalisms restrict rules to fixed arity.
- Token-level issues that interact with idioms and constructions (multiword expressions as one or several leaves) are not treated here; Chapter 17 (Sailer, idioms) is the place to look.
- Gapping and non-constituent coordination, raised by example (21), get no analysis in this chapter; see the coordination and ellipsis chapters.
- The chapter says nothing about parsing efficiency or about derivation trees as recipes, so it neither confirms nor challenges deterministic replay directly.
