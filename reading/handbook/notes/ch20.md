# Chapter 20: Anaphoric binding (Stefan Müller)

Handbook pages 889-944 (text 889-933, references from 933). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter introduces HPSG's Binding Theory. It starts from the Government and Binding (GB) account based on c-command (Section 1) and presents the Pollard and Sag (1992, 1994) alternative, in which Principles A, B, and C are stated over the obliqueness order of the ARG-ST list (local o-command, o-command, o-binding), with exempt anaphors, prepositional objects, extraction, inalienable possession, and long-distance reflexives (Principle Z) as test cases (Section 2). Section 3 presents the "totally non-configurational" recursive definition of o-command and shows that it fails for adjuncts and for unheaded constructions, which leads to mixed proposals with dominance (vc-command, Hukari and Levine 1995; Walker 2011). Sections 4 and 5 cover binding in passives (nested ARG-ST lists with PRO, Manning and Sag 1998) and in Toba Batak and Balinese, where ARG-ST order and grammatical functions come apart. Section 6 presents Branco's (2002) list-threading approach, which collects possible antecedents in lists under NONLOCAL|BIND and resolves binding inside the lexical item of each pronoun; the chapter presents it as the solution to the i-within-i and coordination problems.

The chapter is theoretical. It says nothing about DELPH-IN, the LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, or any other implementation, and nothing about parsing. Every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is therefore "not discussed". (The reference list contains Hellan 2005, "Implementing Norwegian reflexives in an HPSG grammar", but the chapter text describes it only as an account "using special nonlocal features for binding information", p. 923.)

Bearing on the parser: small for tree structure, moderate for semantics. Binding in HPSG is stated over ARG-ST and indices, so it constrains which indices may be identified and leaves the derivation tree alone. The points to keep are (1) one tree is compatible with several binding patterns, so the tree alone does not fix coindexation; (2) the binding principles in every version presented are stated with relations (o-command, dominance, relational constraints such as principleA/principleB) that go beyond what a single local rule application checks; (3) several analyses are carried by lexical rules that change ARG-ST without any token.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. The derivation tree does not determine binding (coindexation)

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics (and deterministic-replay, in the sense "the tree is a complete recipe for the semantics")
- Reference: Section 1, pp. 889-893 (examples 1, 6, 7); Section 2, pp. 894-899 (definitions 11-13, principles 15); Section 2.3, pp. 904-905; Section 6.3, pp. 927-932
- What the chapter says: Binding is identity of referential indices (PER, NUM, GEN) between nominal objects (8, p. 894). The principles say where coindexation is required (Principle A, for a locally o-commanded anaphor) and where it is forbidden (Principles B and C). For one and the same tree, several indexings remain: in "Peter thinks that Mary likes him", the pronoun may be bound by "Peter" or refer to something not mentioned in the sentence (6, p. 891). A locally o-commanded anaphor with two less oblique coarguments has two possible binders ("John showed Mary herself/himself" is decided by gender, but the ARG-ST in (19) allows either, p. 899). Exempt anaphors and pronouns can have antecedents in another sentence (5/29, pp. 891, 904), and the choice between "him" and "himself" in (30) depends on the point of view of the speaker (p. 904). In Branco's (2002) account, the lexical item of a pronoun carries a list ANTEC of possible antecedents, which is computed from the lists of the whole discourse (LIST-U), so the result of the grammar is a candidate set, and the choice is left open (pp. 927-931).
- Why it matters: the label sequence encodes rule names and lexical types. It carries no information about which antecedent a pronoun has. Replay can at most produce the constraints (or candidate lists); it cannot produce resolved coindexation. Where a reflexive has exactly one possible local binder, a grammar could identify the indices by unification, but the chapter does not say that any grammar does this. If the strict output is meant to include binding, it needs a component after replay. If it is not, the MRS obtained by replay leaves pronoun and reflexive indices unrelated to their antecedents.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter mentions MRS only for comparison: Branco's UDRT representation has attributes "similar to the notions assumed in Minimal Recursion Semantics" (L-MAX/L-MIN, SUBORD like HCONS, CONDS like RELS), and Branco adds a feature ANAPH for binding on top of these (p. 927). The chapter does not say whether MRS as used in grammars has anything corresponding.

### 2. The binding principles are stated with relations that are not local rule applications

- Severity: medium (only if binding is to be checked by the engine; low otherwise)
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 897-898 (11-13); Section 3, pp. 908-915 (40, 45, 46); Section 6.3, pp. 927-932 (73, 75, 76)
- What the chapter says: The original o-command (12) refers to dominance: Y o-commands Z if Y locally o-commands some X dominating Z. The revised version (40) is recursive over ARG-ST membership and head projection (token identity of HEAD values). The vc-command of Hukari and Levine (45) and Walker (46) refers to dominance and to the node where a valence list becomes empty. Principle C is a negative condition over unbounded distance ("a non-pronoun must be o-free"). Branco's version threads four lists (LIST-A, LIST-Z, LIST-U, LIST-LU) through every node, shares LIST-U between all nodes of the discourse, and calls relational constraints (principleA, principleB, principleC, principleZ) inside lexical items; the binding lists differ from other NONLOCAL features "in that nothing is ever removed from them" (p. 928). Wechsler (1999) adds that Principle A is existential (bound on some ARG-ST list) and Principle B universal (free on all ARG-ST lists on which the pronoun appears), because a raised element is on several ARG-ST lists (p. 922).
- Why it matters: replay applies named rules by unification to a mother and its daughters. None of the versions presented reduces to that alone. The o-command versions are conditions on the finished structure; Branco's version is closest to replay (list threading by the schemas), but it needs relational constraints, a discourse-level schema, and a list that the top node passes back down to all nodes (LIST-U identified with the collected LIST-LU, p. 930). A typed-feature-structure engine without relational constraints would need a separate procedure for any of them.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Lexical rules without tokens: passive, extraposition "it", body-part nouns, voice

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (the chain of lexical rules in the label)
- Reference: Section 2, p. 898 (extraposition lexical rule); Section 2.3, p. 905 (33); Section 2.4, pp. 905-907 (35, 36); Section 4, pp. 915-917 (49, 53); Section 5, pp. 918-919 (55)
- What the chapter says: Several analyses rest on lexical rules or alternative lexical items that rearrange ARG-ST: the passive rule (49), which demotes the subject and adds an optional by-PP with the same index; the extraposition rule of Pollard and Sag (1994), which adds an expletive "it" and a clause; Koenig's (1999) rule for body-part nouns (35), which maps a noun selecting a possessive onto one selecting a definite determiner and puts a reflexive NP on ARG-ST that corresponds to no word; and the Toba Batak active and objective voice items (55), which differ only in the mapping from ARG-ST to SUBJ and COMPS. For Russian passives, Manning and Sag (1998) nest the ARG-ST list of the base lexeme inside the ARG-ST of the passive, with a PRO element, and use "a complex relational constraint" that flattens the nested lists and removes PRO when mapping to valence (p. 917).
- Why it matters: these are all unary lexical operations, so they fit the label scheme, and the chapter notes that Koenig's analysis works "without assuming any additional structure or additional empty pronominal elements" (p. 907). The cost is on the tagger: whether a definite body-part NP is the inalienable-possession variant is not visible on the token and depends on the verb and its other arguments. The nested ARG-ST with a relational constraint is, again, more than plain unification.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. ARG-ST order versus grammatical functions (Toba Batak, Balinese)

- Severity: low for English, medium for the multilingual aim
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 918-922 (54-61, Figure 9)
- What the chapter says: In Toba Batak and Balinese, either the actor or the undergoer can be mapped to SUBJ depending on voice, and neither clause type is a passive of the other (Manning and Sag 1998; Wechsler and Arka 1998). Binding follows ARG-ST (actor first), so the anaphor can be the SUBJ and its binder a COMPS element (57b, 58b, 61b).
- Why it matters (my inference, the chapter does not mention dependencies): a conversion between derivations and dependency labels cannot read "subject" off the first ARG-ST element in such languages; the SUBJ valence feature and the ARG-ST position give different answers, and the bridge has to pick one per language.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Unheaded constructions and varying branching

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules; UD-bridge (head choice)
- Reference: Section 2.1, p. 900; Section 3, p. 912; Section 3, p. 915
- What the chapter says: Since 1994, "more and more constructional schemata were suggested that do not necessarily have a head": a headless schema that combines the relative phrase and the clause (Müller 1999a; Sag 2010; Müller and Machicao y Priemer 2019), free relatives with the relative phrase as head (Wright and Kathol 2003), and Mandarin serial verb constructions in which no VP is the head (Müller and Lipenkova 2009) (p. 912). Both ternary-branching VPs (Pollard and Sag 1994: 40) and binary-branching ones have been assumed (p. 900). In HPSG grammars of German, all arguments of a finite verb are on one valence list and there is no VP node (p. 915).
- Why it matters: rule names without a head daughter give no head for the dependency conversion and for the coarse universal schemas of the relaxed output; the head has to be stipulated per rule. Ternary rules would not fit a binary tree encoding, but the chapter only reports that both branchings exist in the literature.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Empty elements in the analyses presented

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 2.2, pp. 901-903 (Figure 5, fn. 14); Section 3, pp. 908, 912; Section 6.3, p. 930
- What the chapter says: Pollard and Sag (1994) use traces (lexical items without daughters) and an empty relativizer. Later work "abolished traces altogether" (Bouma, Malouf and Sag 2001), though Müller and Machicao y Priemer (2019) keep a trace-based approach (p. 908). For binding it "does not matter whether there is a trace or not", because the ARG-ST list is the same in both analyses (p. 902). Branco's account uses an empty element ctx for the non-linguistic context, which combines with one or more sentences into a text fragment (p. 930).
- Why it matters: the ARG-ST-based theory itself is neutral, which supports the design. Only Branco's ctx element and the discourse-level schema go beyond a sentence-level derivation with one leaf per token.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Indices that correspond to no node, and word order effects

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics; other
- Reference: fn. 10, p. 894; Section 6.2, pp. 925-927; Section 3, pp. 911-912, 915 (48)
- What the chapter says: Antecedents can be conjoined or split ("Tom told Mary that they should leave"), with resolved person and gender; the approach of Section 6 can "introduce additional indices for binding that are not related to individual nodes in a tree" (fn. 10). A coordinated NP has a plural index of its own, and the indices of the conjuncts are embedded in it, so conjuncts are not local to coarguments of the coordination (68-70). For languages with freer constituent order such as German, Binding Theories "that do not take the linearization of elements into account" have problems (p. 912), and (48) shows a subject-object asymmetry with a fronted adjunct that none of the definitions covers.
- Why it matters: a binding component would need indices for sets of referents, and for German it would need surface order, which the derivation tree provides.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- Binding does not force tree shapes. "In HPSG, the branching that is assumed does not depend on binding facts" (Section 2.1, p. 900), and binding data "does not force syntacticians to assume structures branching in a certain way", in contrast to GB and Minimalism, where empty nodes are assumed for ditransitives (Section 7, pp. 932-933). The derivation trees of the grammar can stay as they are.
- No reconstruction and no dependence on traces. The filler shares only its LOCAL value with the gap; traceless and trace-based analyses give the same ARG-ST, so the binding theory works without empty elements (Section 2.2, pp. 901-903).
- Case-marking prepositions share the index of their NP, so PP arguments behave like NP arguments without extra structure (Section 2.1, pp. 900-901).
- Raising is handled by structure sharing of ARG-ST elements, also across voice alternations in Balinese, "without any further stipulations" (Section 5, pp. 920-922).
- Koenig's body-part analysis needs no "additional structure or additional empty pronominal elements" (Section 2.4, p. 907): a lexical rule does the work.
- The newest approach is lexical: in Branco (2002), "all information that is needed to determine possible binders of the pronoun are available in the lexical item of the pronoun" (Section 6.3, p. 930), and construction-specific list threading is done by the schemas. This is the version closest to something a replay engine could compute as a by-product, given relational constraints.
- Binding violations may not be a matter of syntactic licensing at all. Branco (2002) argues that sentences with wrong bindings "are not syntactically ill-formed, but rather semantically deviant" (p. 927); Principle C has been proposed to be pragmatic (Bolinger 1979; Bresnan 2001; Bouma, Malouf and Sag 2001: 44; Varaschin, Culicover and Winkler 2021) (pp. 893-894); i-within-i effects may be semantic or pragmatic (fn. 28, p. 923). On these views, a replay that ignores binding still licenses exactly the right trees.
- Some bad strings are excluded by case and valence alone ("Himself sleeps", p. 904; "Himself disappointed John", p. 916), which ordinary unification in replay catches.
- The mixed theories that the chapter ends up favoring for adjuncts need dominance (pp. 912-915), and the chapter notes that in SBCG one "would have to refer to the derivation tree" (fn. 23, p. 910). A system whose primary object is the derivation tree has this information available.

## Theory strains

1. Configurational versus argument-structure-based versus thematic binding. HPSG's theory is "a mix" of thematic and configurational theories; purely semantic accounts fail on raising (3) and passive (50-51); GB's c-command fails on "Mary talked to John about himself" (21b) and forces particular branchings. Sections 1, 2.1, 4; pp. 890-891, 900, 916.
2. Whether Principle C belongs in grammar. Bolinger (1979), Bresnan (2001), and Bouma, Malouf and Sag (2001) leave it to pragmatics; Walker (2011) argues it is needed; Varaschin, Culicover and Winkler (2021) argue for dropping it. Section 1, pp. 893-894.
3. Reconstruction. Without it, "[Karl's friend], he knows" is wrongly allowed; with Müller's (1999b) changed definition of dominance, the Pollard and Sag account of (25) is lost; Wechsler (p.c.) notes that the totally non-configurational definition also "reconstructs" and wrongly excludes (25). No resolution; pragmatics or processing is offered as a way out. Section 2.2, fn. 14, p. 903.
4. Exempt anaphors. English anaphors that are not locally o-commanded are exempt (Pollard and Sag 1992, 1994); German anaphors are not exempt (Kiss 2012), so the theory differs per language. Judgments on "around him/himself" conflict in the literature (fn. 16). Section 2.3, pp. 904-905.
5. Extraposition "it". Pollard and Sag (1994) treat it as non-referential, which predicts "It bothers myself that Sandy snores" to be good; Müller (1999b) concludes that it must be referential, which in turn conflicts with the account of (14) (see fn. 13). Sections 2 and 2.3, pp. 898, 905.
6. Crosslinguistic variation requires a fourth principle (Principle Z) and more types of pronominal elements (Dalrymple 1993; Xue, Pollard and Sag 1994; Branco and Marrafa 1999); whether Binding Theory is universal is posed as an open question. Sections 2.5 and 5, pp. 907-908, 918.
7. Adjuncts. The totally non-configurational o-command (40) does not reach into adjuncts ("He knows the woman loved by John"). Adjuncts on ARG-ST (Chung 1998; Przepiórkowski 1999; Manning, Sag and Iida 1999) cause Principle C conflicts in the nominal domain; a second index for binding is called "fairly ad hoc"; the DEPS list (Bouma, Malouf and Sag 2001) is criticized for scope (Levine and Hukari 2006) and cannot express the difference between VP and sentential adjuncts (42, 43). Conclusion: "a totally non-configurational Binding Theory seems to be impossible because of adjuncts". Section 3, pp. 910-912, 915.
8. Unheaded constructions break the projection clause of (40) ("He knows whoever is loved by John"; headless relative clause schemas; Mandarin serial verbs). Section 3, p. 912.
9. vc-command: Hukari and Levine's (1995) definition contains a modal "would be formed" that Walker (2011) says cannot be formalized; the two definitions differ in whether specifier structures and the sister VP are covered; neither covers the German asymmetry in (48). Section 3, pp. 912-915.
10. SBCG and binding. The notion of projection in (40) cannot be formalized in SBCG, whose signs have no daughters; one must refer to the derivation tree, which is external to the signs. Section 3, fn. 23, p. 910.
11. Nested ARG-ST with PRO for Russian passives and Chi-Mwi:ni needs a "complex relational constraint" to flatten the list for valence; the alternative is separate lists for binding. Section 4, p. 917.
12. Imprecision of the principles under raising: an element can be locally o-commanded on several ARG-ST lists (van Noord and Bouma 1997; Müller 1999b); Wechsler (1999) stipulates an existential reading for Principle A and a universal one for Principle B. Section 5, p. 922.
13. Nominal heads as binders and i-within-i. The o-command theory says nothing about "his_i father_i"; a general i-within-i condition would wrongly exclude German prenominal participles with reflexives (66) and English reduced relatives (67); "some special constraint seems to be needed". Section 6.1, pp. 923-925.
14. Coordination. Conjuncts are not local to coarguments, so "sich" in (68, 70a) is wrongly exempt and "ihn" in (70b) wrongly allowed; Reinhart and Reuland's (1993) predicate-based account covers only distributive readings. Section 6.2, pp. 925-927.
15. Binding versus filler-gap dependencies. HPSG holds that they are "crucially different" (p. 893), yet Bredenkamp (1996), Hellan (2005), and Branco (2002) use the nonlocal machinery for binding; Bredenkamp's use of SLASH "probably would result in conflicts with true nonlocal dependencies" (p. 923). Branco's lists are NONLOCAL features that are never bound off.
16. Branco's approach has its own loose end: principleB leaves "herself" in the antecedent list of "she" in (74), and this is excluded by an extra argument ("since she binds herself") (p. 931). The chapter also introduces the approach as one that "looks technical at first" (p. 927).
17. Linking and body-part NPs: Koenig (1999) uses the binding mechanism in place of argument linking plus an Active Zone restriction; Wechsler (p.c.) suggests a reanalysis via weak definites (fn. 19, p. 906).

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not statements of the chapter.

- The chapter never says whether the ERG or any other DELPH-IN grammar implements any binding principle, or how reflexives and pronouns appear in ERG MRSs. This has to be checked in the grammar itself before deciding whether the strict output should say anything about binding.
- Nothing is said about how implemented systems treat coreference in general (inside or outside the grammar), or about the cost of relational constraints in TRALE-style versus LKB-style formalisms.
- Control (obligatory coindexation of an unexpressed subject) is not treated in this chapter; raising is mentioned only as it interacts with ARG-ST.
- Bound-variable versus e-type readings are mentioned only in passing for Branco's VAR and R-MARK (p. 928); the interaction of binding with quantifier scope in MRS is not discussed.
- UD has no binding annotation in its basic trees, so the UD bridge is affected only through the subject/actor question in caveat 4; that link is my inference.
- Hellan (2005) may be an implemented account (the title says so); the chapter gives no details.
