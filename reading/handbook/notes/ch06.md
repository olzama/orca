# Chapter 6: Agreement (Stephen Wechsler)

Handbook pages 219-244 (text 219-239, references 240-244). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter presents agreement in HPSG as a side effect of mechanisms that exist for other reasons: valence saturation (verb agreement, via phi features on ARG-ST items), modification (modifier agreement, via phi features inside MOD), and binding (anaphoric agreement, via phi features on referential indices). It develops the two-feature INDEX/CONCORD theory (Pollard and Sag 1994; Kathol 1999; Wechsler and Zlatić 2000, 2003), motivates it diachronically (INDEX from incorporated pronouns, CONCORD from incorporated noun classifiers), and uses it for mixed agreement such as French polite *vous*. It treats the choice between formal, semantic, and default agreement through selective underspecification and the Agreement Marking Principle (Wechsler 2011), and a unary metonymy schema for Swedish "pancake sentences". Two non-local cases close the chapter: long-distance agreement in Passamaquoddy, reanalyzed as anaphoric agreement of a null argument (LeSourd 2018), and superficial (string-adjacency) agreement in Welsh, stated on order domains (Borsley 2009).

The chapter is theoretical. It says nothing about parsing, and its only mention of an implemented grammar is a footnote naming a TRALE grammar of Persian. Most of it supports the design; the caveats concern a few constructions outside English and the semantics of agreement.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Superficial agreement is stated on order domains (DOM), not on the tree

- Severity: medium (low for English/ERG, higher for the multi-language goal)
- Assumption affected: continuity / tree-encoding / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 7.2, pp. 238-239
- What the chapter says: In West Flemish (complementizer agreement, Haegeman 1992) and Welsh, agreement is conditioned by string adjacency, with no grammatical relation between trigger and target. Welsh agreeing elements agree with an immediately following NP if and only if it is a pronoun; the trigger can be a subject, a prepositional object, or the first conjunct of a coordinate subject (ex. 31). Borsley (2009: 257) states this as an implicational constraint over the DOM list of linearization theory (ex. 32): when an element bearing AGR is immediately followed in DOM by a personal pronoun, AGR is identified with the pronoun's index. The DOM list "encodes linear precedence between constituents that are not necessarily sisters".
- Why it matters: the constraint is neither a property of a rule in the derivation tree nor a local daughter-mother relation. A replay engine that only applies named unary and binary rules has no place to check it, unless the grammar carries a DOM-like list or re-encodes adjacency (for example through an edge feature). First-conjunct agreement (31c) also means the target's features do not match those of the whole subject phrase, so a naive subject-verb unification would wrongly fail.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter only points to linearization theory (Reape 1994; Müller 1995, 1999; Kathol 2000).

### 2. Long-distance agreement: coreference with an unspecified embedded argument

- Severity: medium for other languages, none for English
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / semantics
- Reference: Section 7.1, pp. 236-238
- What the chapter says: In Passamaquoddy (also reported for Tsez and Hindi-Urdu), a matrix verb agrees with a phrase inside a doubly embedded clause (ex. 28). LeSourd (2018) analyzes this as anaphoric agreement of a null matrix object with an antecedent in the lower clause (a prolepsis construction, parallel to "long-distance raising", ex. 29). The lexical entry (30) "stipulates" that the matrix object NP corefers with "some" argument j of "some" elementary predication PRD on the complement clause's RESTR list. The analysis uses the simplified Minimal Recursion Semantics of Sag, Wasow and Bender (2003), where a node's RESTR list is the concatenation of its daughters' lists.
- Why it matters: the derivation tree does not say which embedded argument is the coreferent one. The entry picks an arbitrary member of a list of unbounded length, so replay of the tree is not a complete, deterministic recipe for the semantics here; the choice is either a search step or an extra decision the tagger would have to supply. The constraint also inspects the accumulated semantics of a complement, which is unusual for a lexical entry.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed (the only link is that the analysis is formulated with a simplified MRS RESTR list).

### 3. Null arguments: on ARG-ST, absent from valence lists and from the string

- Severity: low (mostly supportive, see below)
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements / leaves-equal-tokens / UD-bridge / semantics
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 222-223; also Section 7.1, p. 237
- What the chapter says: Pro-drop is modeled by letting the dropped argument appear on ARG-ST without appearing on SUBJ or COMPS (ex. 7, where "the SUBJ list item is optional"). No null pronoun is assumed. The verb's phi features are specified whether or not the pronoun appears. In the Passamaquoddy analysis the matrix clause contains a null argument cross-referenced by a verbal suffix, and example (29) is printed with an "e" in the embedded clause.
- Why it matters: this is compatible with the no-empty-elements assumption. Two small complications remain: (a) whether the subject is realized is a choice among lexical variants or lexical rules, which the tag must encode for each verb token; (b) the dropped argument has a semantic index with phi features but no token, so it has no counterpart in a UD tree, and the MRS has an argument with no surface anchor.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. Footnote 2 (p. 222) cites Müller and Ghayoomi (2010), a TRALE implementation for Persian, for a pro-drop analysis along these lines.

### 4. A unary phrasal construction that changes the index and adds a predication

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / semantics / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 233-234
- What the chapter says: Swedish "pancake sentences" (ex. 23: plural common-gender subject, neuter singular predicate adjective) are analyzed with a non-branching metonymy-phrase (ex. 24, adapted from Wechsler 2013: 82). The mother NP has a new index of type *unm* (no phi features) and its RESTR adds an *involve(s, i)* relation to the daughter's restrictions.
- Why it matters: the rule is unary, so it fits the binary/unary assumption. It is, however, a string-vacuous rule with semantic content: the only surface evidence is the agreement mismatch on a different word, so the tagger has to place the unary rule on the subject NP using information from the predicate. It adds an MRS predication with no token, and UD has no node or label that corresponds to it.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Agreement features are sometimes semantically interpreted, conditioned on underspecification

- Severity: medium for the semantics, low for syntax
- Assumption affected: semantics / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 221-222; Section 5, pp. 231-232; Section 6, pp. 234-236
- What the chapter says: The Agreement Marking Principle (Wechsler 2011) states that target agreement features are semantically interpreted whenever the trigger is underspecified for the formal features the target is sensitive to. Examples: Serbo-Croatian *sudija* 'judge' (gender on adjective and participle tells the sex of the judge, ex. 5); French *Dupont est compétent/compétente* (ex. 21b); English verb number with coordinated clausal subjects (ex. 20); French polite *vous* with a singular predicate adjective (ex. 26).
- Why it matters: the meaning contributed by a target depends on whether the trigger leaves a feature unspecified. The chapter states the principle in prose and gives no feature-structure formalization of it. Testing "is underspecified" is not something plain unification does, so if the MRS is meant to reflect these interpretations (female judge, single addressee, plurality of situations), the grammar needs some explicit encoding. Remark of mine, not from the chapter: this is the point to check against the primary source before assuming MRS falls out for free.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Disjunctive and default specifications on targets

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (other)
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 232-233
- What the chapter says: Targets have a default form used when there is no trigger or agreement is blocked. Swedish *gott* has a SUBJ item whose index is "disjunctively specified for either neuter singular or type *unm*" (Wechsler and Zlatić 2003: 154), *unm* being the type of indices without phi features, such as those introduced by verbs.
- Why it matters: a disjunctive value has to be compiled into the type hierarchy (a common supertype) or into two lexical entries; in the second case the tagger must choose between them.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Agreement as hard unification: graded judgments and pragmatic agreement

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / semantics
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 220-221; Section 4.1, pp. 224-226; Section 5, pp. 231-232
- What the chapter says: Incompatible features make the structure ill-formed (ex. 3a). Several data points are graded, though: "??" for the dispreferred number in (20), "?*" in (12), and INDEX agreement of a locally bound reflexive is described as "preferred". Outside the binding domain, pronouns may agree either formally (INDEX) or pragmatically (ex. 10, 11); pragmatic agreement "applies everywhere" and is a matter of semantic consistency of coreferential expressions.
- Why it matters: in strict mode any agreement clash fails replay, so graded or variable agreement in real text goes to the relaxed fallback. Cross-sentential and non-coargument pronoun agreement is outside what the derivation determines, so replay neither checks nor resolves it.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Two loci for phi features (INDEX and CONCORD) versus one feature bundle per token

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 4.2, pp. 226-231; Section 6, pp. 234-236
- What the chapter says: A noun carries number and gender twice, in CONCORD (a HEAD feature, together with CASE, without PERSON) and in INDEX (with PERSON, without CASE); the values are normally shared (ex. 17) but can diverge for special nouns and pronouns. French *vous* has a second person plural index and no CONCORD phi features; finite verbs agree in INDEX, predicate adjectives in CONCORD.
- Why it matters: the chapter does not mention UD. Remark of mine: a conversion between feature structures and a single per-token morphological feature set has to decide which of the two loci it reads or writes, and mixed-agreement items are exactly where the two differ.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- Agreement needs no dedicated mechanism (Section 1, p. 220; Section 3.1, p. 223; Section 4.2, p. 226). It "piggy-backs" on valence saturation, modification, and binding, with no counterpart of probe/goal or AGREE. For replay this means that applying the head-subject, head-complement, and head-adjunct rules by unification already enforces agreement; no extra pass is needed.
- Locality (Section 3.1, pp. 223-224). Locality conditions on agreement follow from those of the subject-head, complement-head, and modifier-head relations. Trigger features reach the phrase through the Head Feature Principle (CONCORD) or the Semantics Principle (INDEX). Target features sit inside ARG-ST or MOD and do not project to VP, S, or AP. Everything is checked at the local tree where the rule applies.
- No feature copying, no directionality (Section 2, pp. 221-222). The representation is the same whichever element supplies a feature, and both trigger and target may be underspecified. This fits order-independent unification during replay.
- No empty pronouns (Section 2, p. 222). Pollard and Sag (1994: 64) argue against a paradigm of null pronouns; the pro-dropped argument is on ARG-ST only. This agrees with the no-empty-elements assumption.
- The metonymy analysis is a unary rule (Section 5, pp. 233-234), chosen over an ellipsis analysis with silent material. The preferred HPSG analysis stays within unary/binary rules over overt tokens.
- Semantics composed in tandem (Section 5, p. 232). "Syntactic and semantic representations are composed in tandem", and the long-distance agreement analysis uses a RESTR list built by concatenation of the daughters' lists (Section 7.1, p. 237), which is the MRS-as-by-product picture.
- The standard cases of English agreement (ex. 1-3, 13-14, 19) are purely lexical specifications on SUBJ/ARG-ST items, so for the ERG the tagger's lexical type choice carries all agreement information.

## Theory strains

- French predicate adjectives with polite *vous* (Section 6, pp. 235-236). Kathol (1999: 230): predicate adjectives are specified for semantic agreement, finite verbs for formal agreement. Wechsler (2011): the split is INDEX versus CONCORD plus the Agreement Marking Principle. The chapter's counterexample to Kathol is the plurale tantum *ciseaux* (ex. 27). Of Wechsler's four suppositions, (i) and (iii) are said to follow from the theory; (ii) (*vous* lacks CONCORD phi features) and (iv) (predicate adjectives agree in CONCORD) are left as assumptions.
- Swedish pancake sentences (Section 5, pp. 233-234). Metonymy schema with index shift (Wechsler 2013) versus an ellipsis analysis with a silent infinitival (Faarlund 1977; Enger 2004; Josefsson 2009). The argument for metonymy is that the subject behaves like an NP (no extraposition, ex. 25).
- Where the phi features live (Section 4.2, p. 226 and fn. 5). The second agreement feature was a HEAD feature in Pollard and Sag (1994: Section 2.5.1), AGR in Kathol (1999), CONCORD in Wechsler and Zlatić (2000, 2003). Number and gender are represented twice with reentrancy that special nouns break. The justification offered for the architecture is largely diachronic (Section 4.2, pp. 227-231), which the chapter itself presents as "clues" for the synchronic formalization.
- The Agreement Marking Principle (Section 5, p. 232) is posited as a separate principle, given in prose, in a chapter whose claim is that agreement needs no special devices.
- Long-distance agreement (Section 7.1, pp. 236-238). The lexical entry "stipulates" coreference with some argument somewhere in the complement's RESTR list. The simple locality picture is "challenged" and rescued by reanalysis as anaphora; the analysis relies on Passamaquoddy lacking Principle C effects (fn. 7).
- Superficial agreement (Section 7.2, pp. 238-239). Here agreement is not a side effect of valence, modification, or binding: it needs a dedicated implicational constraint and the DOM machinery of linearization theory.
- Disjunctive index specification and the *unm* index type for Swedish neuter singular defaults (Section 5, p. 233).
- Binding Theory (Section 4.1, p. 225, fn. 4). Müller (1999: Section 20.4.4) is cited for similar German cases and "problems for HPSG's Binding Theory". INDEX agreement is categorical in principle, but the data in (12) are given as a preference ("?*").
- Against other frameworks (Section 1, p. 220; Section 2, pp. 221-222; Section 3.1, p. 223). The chapter argues against transformational feature copying (trigger lacking the feature, pro-drop requiring matching null-pronoun paradigms) and against stipulated locality conditions in Minimalism (Chomsky 2000). The INDEX/CONCORD theory has been adopted in LFG (King and Dalrymple 2004) and GB/Minimalism (Danon 2011) (fn. 5).

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter does not say how any implemented grammar (ERG, LKB/ACE, Enju) represents agreement, for instance whether the ERG keeps separate INDEX and CONCORD features or how it handles English cases such as collective nouns or *there*-agreement.
- Agreement with coordinate phrases (feature resolution, closest-conjunct agreement) is deferred to chapter 16; it is likely the agreement topic with the most consequences for replay and for the UD bridge.
- No discussion of case syncretism or feature neutrality, which are typical sources of non-trivial type hierarchies for phi features.
- No formal statement of the Agreement Marking Principle; Wechsler 2011 should be checked for whether it can be compiled into lexical entries.
- No mention of UD or of dependency representations; caveat 8 is my inference.
- No discussion of how robust parsing should treat agreement errors in real text; caveat 7 is partly my inference from the chapter's graded examples.
