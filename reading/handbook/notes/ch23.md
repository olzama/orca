# Chapter 23: Information structure (Kordula De Kuthy)

Handbook pages 1043-1078 (text 1043-1074, references 1074-1078). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter surveys how HPSG work has added information structure (focus/background, topic/comment, given/new) to the sign. It compares the proposals by where the feature sits (on the sign, in CONTEXT, in CONTENT, in synsem) and by what its values are (whole signs, indices, chunks of logical form, MRS elementary predications). It then presents the principles that set these values: word-level principles that tie focus or topic to pitch accents, and phrase-level principles that pass the values up or let focus "project" to a larger constituent. Two further sections show information structure used as a licensing condition on word order (Catalan and Greek clitic dislocation, German NP-PP split, partial VP fronting and multiple fronting, English predicate preposing) and its link to an enriched prosodic representation in PHON.

The chapter is theoretical. The only implemented system it mentions is the LinGO Grammar Matrix, as the home of Song (2017) and its ICONS extension of MRS (p. 1058); Paggio (2009) is described as "part of a grammar of Danish" that uses MRS (p. 1046). The ERG, LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, parsing efficiency, and dependency representations are not mentioned. Bearing on the parser is moderate: almost everything concerns an extra layer of meaning that written text mostly does not mark, and the main lesson is how that layer can be kept out of the derivation (underspecification) or forced into it (accent-driven lexical rules and phrasal subtypes).

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Focus projection: one string and one tree, several information structures

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / semantics
- Reference: Section 3.2.1, pp. 1051-1052 (principle 10); Section 3.2.2, pp. 1053-1055 (principle 13, footnote 7); Section 3.2.3, pp. 1056-1058 (examples 16-17, rules 18)
- What the chapter says: an accent on one word is compatible with focus on that word or on larger constituents that contain it. Engdahl and Vallduví state this as an either/or principle on phrases. De Kuthy (2002) and De Kuthy and Meurers (2003) state it as a disjunctive principle on every phrase, and say that projection, where possible, "is always optional". In Song (2017), with the focus-projection lexical rule, "either only the book, or the entire VP sent Lee the book, or even the entire sentence" can be focused.
- Why it matters: in the structured-meaning line the same tree with the same rule names satisfies the grammar in several ways that differ only in INFO-STRUC values. A derivation tree is then not a complete recipe for the feature structure: replay would have to pick a disjunct or return a set. (The chapter does not discuss derivation trees; this consequence is my inference from the disjunctive principles.)
- DELPH-IN stance: Song and Bender (2012) and Song (2017) avoid the problem by underspecification: the readings in (16) "are further specializations of one MRS which is associated with one syntactic structure", with the type of the ICONS element left open (pp. 1056-1057). This is the Grammar Matrix solution; the chapter does not say what the ERG does.

### 2. The trigger for information structure is prosody, which a text tagger does not see

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / semantics
- Reference: Section 3.1, pp. 1049-1051 (principles 6, 8); Section 5, p. 1061 (principle 23); Section 7, pp. 1070-1073 (constraints 38-41)
- What the chapter says: in English, German, and Spanish the word-level principles are conditioned on PHON|ACCENT (A and B accents, rising and falling accents, pitch accent and boundary tone features). In Song (2017) the accents are introduced by lexical rules: `fc-lex-rule` (41) requires a high-star pitch accent, sets MKG to fc-only, and adds a semantic-focus element to ICONS; constraint (40) on `lex-rule` ties MKG|FC to prosodic prominence (UT|DTE).
- Why it matters: if a grammar encodes accents as lexical rules, those rules are steps in the unary chain that the tag must name, yet nothing in the written token decides between the accented and the unaccented variant. The tagger would be guessing, or the rules must be left out of the label set and the values left underspecified. (The chapter does not discuss written input; this is my inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: the chapter presents the Song (2017) lexical rules as the Grammar Matrix treatment for English A and B accents. It says that most approaches "do not go beyond the postulation of one or two particular accents" used as illustration (p. 1071). Whether any grammar applies these rules when parsing text is not discussed.

### 3. Zero-marked lexical rules that split a verb into focus-projecting and non-projecting variants

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (size and ambiguity of the lexical-rule chain in the tag)
- Reference: Section 3.2.3, pp. 1057-1058 (rules 18a-b); Section 3.1, pp. 1050-1051 (types 9)
- What the chapter says: "there are two lexical items for the verb send", derived by `no-focus-projection-rule` and `focus-projection-rule`, which differ in the MKG|FC value of the last complement and in the ICONS contribution through C-CONT. Lexical items also fall into four `icons-lex-item` types according to how many ICONS elements they contribute (none for expletives and relative pronouns, one or two for verbs with clausal arguments).
- Why it matters: both rules are unary and fit the tag format, but they have no visible form and are decided by an accent on a different word (the last complement). Each transitive and ditransitive verb gets an extra binary choice in its label.
- DELPH-IN stance: these are the rules of Song (2017), part of the Grammar Matrix. The chapter adds that the Matrix "contains a multitude of lexical and phrasal types and principles", some of them "necessary for the specific way in which grammar fragments in the LinGO Grammar Matrix are implemented and processed", and declines to list them (p. 1058).

### 4. Phrasal types cross-classified with information structure multiply the rule inventory

- Severity: medium (for a grammar built this way), low for the ERG as far as the chapter says
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 3.2.3, pp. 1055-1056 (principle 15, Figure 3, footnotes 8-9); Section 4, pp. 1058-1060 (Figure 4, constraints 20)
- What the chapter says: in Paggio (2009) the type `focus-inheritance` "has to be cross-classified with every basic phrasal subtype (such as hd-comp, hd-spec, hd-adj, etc.)", giving types such as `focus-hd-adj`; similar types exist for background inheritance, chosen by the accent status of the non-head daughter; clause types such as `decl-main-all-focus` and `inv-topic-comment` fix the values at the top. Song (2017) has `topic-comment` and `top-scr-comp-head` phrase types for Japanese.
- Why it matters: node labels in the derivation tree are rule names. With this design every syntactic schema comes in several information-structure variants over the same string and the same bracketing, so the tagger's structural labels must encode a choice that depends on accent or context, and the UD bridge would map several distinct derivations to one dependency tree. (The UD consequence is my inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: Paggio's grammar uses MRS; the chapter does not name its platform. The chapter notes two formal errors in Paggio's list specifications (footnotes 8-9, pp. 1055-1056). For Song (2017) see caveat 3.

### 5. Principles stated with relations and disjunction, beyond plain unification of a rule

- Severity: medium (for replay of grammars written in this style)
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 3.2.2, pp. 1053-1054 (principle 13, footnote 6: `collect-focus`, `any-dtr`); Section 5, pp. 1062-1063 (principle 24, footnotes 12-14: `dtrs-list`, `given-sign-list`, shuffle); Section 6, p. 1065 (constraint 29, disjoint union, footnote 15); Section 7, pp. 1071-1072 (`decl-tune`, constraint 38)
- What the chapter says: the focus projection principles are implicational constraints on `phrase` with three or four disjuncts and auxiliary recursive relations over daughter lists and over PHON; the givenness disjunct uses Reape's shuffle relation to say that the daughters are given signs with one focused sign mixed in; the Greek Linkhood Constraint uses disjoint union without defining it; Bildhauer's prosody keeps a flat PHON list of prosodic words governed by relational constraints.
- Why it matters: a replay engine that only unifies a named rule with its daughters cannot evaluate these principles. They need relational constraint solving, and their disjunctions reintroduce choice at every phrase (see caveat 1). (The chapter does not compare formalisms; this is my inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed as such. The chapter does show the Grammar Matrix alternative, which uses difference lists for RELS, HCONS, and ICONS, "an explicit pointer to the last element of the list" (p. 1047), and puts constructional contributions in C-CONT (pp. 1057, 1073).

### 6. Index-valued information structure cannot single out a modifier (limits of MRS/ICONS output)

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 1046-1049 (structures 3-5)
- What the chapter says: using indices as values (Paggio 2009, Song and Bender 2012, Song 2017) "is again problematic whenever two constituents share their index value, but only one of them is assigned a particular information structural function". With an intersective adjective sharing the noun's index, "there is no way to relate a particular information structure function (e.g., contrast) to the adjective alone (as in RED car)". De Kuthy (2002) also argues that the Pollard and Sag (1994) semantics is unsuitable "because the semantic composition is not done in parallel with the syntactic build-up of a phrase", and adopts Sailer's (2000) Ty2 logical forms.
- Why it matters: if information structure is to come out of replay as part of the MRS (ICONS), the output inherits this expressive gap. The competing proposal needs a different semantic representation language from the one the parser targets.
- DELPH-IN stance: ICONS in the Grammar Matrix is the index-based design that the criticism is aimed at (structure 4, p. 1047): TARGET is shared with a sign's ARG0, CLAUSE with the INDEX of the clause's semantic head. The chapter reports no reply to the objection.

### 7. Traces and empty heads in the analyses that the chapter builds on

- Severity: low for English, medium for the many-languages goal
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 2, p. 1047; Section 6, p. 1068 (Figure 5), p. 1069 (constraint 35, footnote 19)
- What the chapter says: De Kuthy (2002) places INFO-STRUC outside local objects because, with a trace-based account of unbounded dependencies, "traces should not contribute anything to the information structure of a sentence"; she wants the approach to be "independent of the decision of which kind of UDC theory one assumes". Figure 5 (partial VP fronting) has a leaf with empty PHON and a SLASH value. Müller's analysis of German multiple fronting, used by Bildhauer and Cook (2010), identifies the construction through a HEAD|DSL value on the filler that carries "information about an empty verbal head"; DSL models "the HPSG equivalent of verb movement".
- Why it matters: several of the German word order analyses linked to information structure presuppose traces or an empty verbal head. A grammar without empty elements needs a traceless reformulation of each, and the tree encoding has no token to attach such leaves to.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Dislocation, clitic doubling, and discontinuous NPs as information-structure-licensed word orders

- Severity: low for English, medium for the many-languages goal
- Assumption affected: continuity / leaves-equal-tokens / binary-unary-rules / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 6, pp. 1063-1067 (examples 25, 28, 30; schema 26; LP constraint 27; constraint 29); footnote 14, p. 1063
- What the chapter says: Catalan non-focal arguments are clitic-dislocated to the left (links) or right (tails) by a Head-Dislocation Schema with a DISLOC-DTRS value, each dislocated daughter bound by an element in the head's content, ordered by the LP statement LINK > FOCUS > TAIL. Greek clitic left dislocation is a head-filler phrase whose filler is "duplicated in the morphology by a pronominal affix" recorded on a nonlocal CLITIC list. German NP-PP split is called a discontinuous NP, with a condition that NP and PP may not belong to the same focus or topic. The focus principles are stated generally so as to cover "flatter structures" as well as binary ones.
- Why it matters: the dislocation schema can have several non-head daughters and is ordered by an LP constraint over information-structure values, which a binary tree encoding must fix by convention. A doubled clitic that is an affix in the grammar may be a separate token in a treebank. The chapter does not say whether the German split is derived by extraction or by a discontinuous constituent, so its bearing on continuity is open. (The treebank-token and encoding consequences are my inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Constraints that hold only of unembedded signs

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (root condition)
- Reference: Section 5, p. 1061, footnote 11; Section 7, p. 1072 (constraint 38)
- What the chapter says: De Kuthy and Meurers (2011) make INFO-STRUC appropriate only for `unembedded-sign`, composed from STRUCTURED-MEANING; Bildhauer (2008) restricts the declarative tune to signs with EMBED −.
- Why it matters: replay needs an explicit root step where such constraints apply; this matches having root conditions in the grammar and is a small matter.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 10. Information structure depends on discourse context outside the sentence

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 1, pp. 1043-1045 (example 1); Section 3.2.3, p. 1056 (example 16); Section 5, pp. 1060-1063 (example 22)
- What the chapter says: focus and givenness are defined relative to a context question and prior discourse; the GIVEN feature records material already in the discourse, and the deaccenting analysis of "a GREEN convertible" depends on "convertible" being given.
- Why it matters: a sentence-level parser has no access to this, so fully specified information structure cannot be a by-product of replay on isolated sentences; only an underspecified value can.
- DELPH-IN stance: Song and Bender's underspecified ICONS is motivated by exactly this ambiguity "with respect to the context" (p. 1056).

## Support for the design

- Underspecification keeps information structure out of the derivation. Song and Bender (2012) associate the competing focus readings with "one MRS which is associated with one syntactic structure" and leave the ICONS type open (pp. 1056-1057). This is compatible with one tree, one deterministic replay, one MRS.
- Information structure as part of MRS. In the Grammar Matrix approach ICONS sits beside RELS and HCONS inside CONTENT, built with difference lists and C-CONT (structure 4, p. 1047; rules 18, 41). It would be obtained by the same replay that yields the MRS.
- Lexical rules as the vehicle. Accent and focus-projection effects are stated as lexical rules over `lex-rule-infl-affixed` daughters (pp. 1057, 1073), which are unary steps of the kind the tag already encodes.
- Many-languages precedent. Song (2017) is described as part of the LinGO Grammar Matrix "with the aim of parsing and generating sentences from a large number of different languages" (p. 1058).
- Binary structures. The chapter's examples assume binary branching, and the general principles simplify under that assumption (footnote 14, p. 1063).
- Word order analyses as ordinary schemas. Greek and French left dislocation, German fronted VPs and multiple fronting, and English predicate preposing are all treated as head-filler phrases with added constraints (pp. 1065-1070), so no new structural device is needed for them beyond what extraction already requires.
- No separate information-structure tree. All approaches add features to the existing sign; none adds a second constituent structure (Section 8, p. 1073).

## Theory strains

- Where the feature lives (Section 2, pp. 1045-1048; Section 8, p. 1073). Four positions compete: a feature on signs with atomic sort values (Manandhar 1994b); inside CONTEXT (Engdahl and Vallduví 1996; Paggio 2009); inside CONTENT/MRS (Song and Bender 2012; Song 2017); inside synsem, hence visible to selection (Bildhauer and Cook 2010, 2011); on the sign but outside synsem (De Kuthy 2002; Bildhauer 2008). The chapter's conclusion lists this as an open point of difference.
- What the values are (pp. 1046-1049): whole signs, lists of indices, lists of logical-form terms, lists of elementary predications. Index values fail for "RED car"; sign values come with a principle that the chapter judges incompletely worked out.
- Manandhar's syntactic markup (pp. 1045-1046): criticized for "a proliferation of syntactic markup of non-syntactic properties" and for not supporting directly interacting modules.
- Dependence on the theory of extraction (p. 1047): placing information structure in local objects clashes with trace-based UDC analyses, so the choice of feature geometry is entangled with the trace/traceless dispute.
- Choice of semantic framework (p. 1048; Section 5, pp. 1060-1061): Pollard and Sag (1994) semantics is rejected by De Kuthy as not compositional in step with syntax; Ty2 (Sailer 2000) and structured meanings compete with MRS, and with alternative semantics (Schwarzschild 1999; Rooth 1992) for givenness.
- Formal looseness in a founding proposal (footnote 5, p. 1051; p. 1052): Engdahl and Vallduví's "instantiated" values are "incompatible with the model theoretic view of HPSG in Pollard & Sag (1994)", since feature structures are total and only descriptions can be underspecified; several aspects of their Figure 2 "are not properly spelled out", including focus inheritance inside NPs.
- Errors and gaps in formalizations: Paggio's list specifications are "not correctly specified" (footnotes 8-9, pp. 1055-1056); Alexopoulou and Kolliakou "provide no exact definition" of disjoint union (footnote 15, p. 1065); Paggio "seems to assume" a different structure for subject-initial and non-subject-initial V2 clauses in Danish (footnote 10, p. 1059).
- Focus projection is stipulated piecemeal (pp. 1053-1055, 1061-1063): a growing disjunction with one clause per category (noun and preposition: rightmost daughter; verb: daughter lexically marked FPP +; givenness: all other daughters given), an open "∨ …", and a lexical diacritic FPP on arguments. Song's lexical rules instead tie projection to the last complement (p. 1057). The two accounts make different cuts and neither is derived from anything more general.
- Topics are an afterthought (Section 4, pp. 1058-1060): most approaches add a TOPIC feature or a LINK and assume "similar principles as the ones introduced for focus", without separate motivation. Information status (given/new) "has received comparatively little interest within the HPSG community" (p. 1044).
- Prosody is mostly a placeholder (p. 1071): "most approaches do not go beyond the postulation of one or two particular accents, which are then somehow encoded as part of the PHON value"; only Bildhauer (2008) gives a worked prosodic architecture.
- Explanation versus restatement (Section 6, p. 1063): the chapter claims that word order constraints "previously stipulated in syntax" can be derived from discourse integration, yet the analyses shown add construction-specific constraints, types, and features (Head-Dislocation Schema, CLITIC, DT, `pres`, `a-top-com`, STATUS, `pred-prepos-ph`), one per construction and language.
- Relation to other frameworks (pp. 1045, 1052, 1060): Manandhar's approach is likened to F-marking in Generative Grammar (Jackendoff 1972; Selkirk 1984); structured meanings come from von Stechow, Jacobs, and Krifka; Engdahl and Vallduví adopt Vallduví's information packaging.

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter does not say whether the ERG encodes any information structure (for example ICONS, or constructions such as clefts and topicalization) or how ERG derivations relate to the Grammar Matrix machinery of Song (2017). This needs checking in the grammar itself.
- Written-text cues to information structure in English (clefts, pseudo-clefts, focus particles such as "only", fronting, punctuation, capitalization for emphasis) are barely touched; "only" appears once in an example (22b) with no analysis. Association with focus is not covered.
- Nothing is said about processing: no discussion of parsing cost of the disjunctive and relational principles, of how implemented grammars restrict them, or of generation.
- Nothing is said about dependency representations or UD. UD has relations that overlap with the phenomena here (dislocated elements, clitic doubling), and the mapping would have to be worked out independently.
- The chapter does not state how German NP-PP split is derived syntactically in De Kuthy (2002), so whether it challenges continuity cannot be judged from this chapter alone.
- Morphological marking of topic and focus is mentioned only through Japanese wa; languages with focus particles or focus-sensitive verbal morphology are named as a class (p. 1058) with no analysis.
