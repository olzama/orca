# Chapter 8: Nominal structures (Frank Van Eynde)

Handbook pages 275-313 (text 275-308, references 309-313). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter surveys three HPSG treatments of the noun phrase. The specifier treatment (Pollard and Sag 1994; Ginzburg and Sag 2000) keeps the noun as head, has the noun select the determiner through SPR, and has the determiner select the noun's content through SPEC. The DP treatment (Netter 1994, 1996) makes the determiner a functional head that takes the nominal as complement. The functor treatment (Van Eynde 1998, 2006; Allegranza 1998, 2007; adopted in SBCG, Sag 2012) keeps the noun as head, drops SPR, MOD and SPEC, and treats determiners and adjuncts alike as non-head "functors" that select the head through SELECT and pass their MARKING value to the mother. Section 2 applies the three to ordinary nominals and argues that the DP treatment fits a monostratal, surface-oriented framework least well. Section 3 introduces Constructional HPSG (bidimensional phrase-type hierarchies) and applies it to gerunds, the Big Mess Construction (*so good a bargain*), and irregular preposition-plus-nominal combinations (*beaucoup de farine*, *these sort of problems*), with pointers to binominal NPs, apposition, extraction out of NPs, and predicative nominals.

The chapter is purely theoretical. It never mentions parsing, an implemented grammar, or any system (no DELPH-IN, ERG, LKB, ACE, TRALE, or Enju), so every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is "not discussed". Its bearing on the parser is indirect: nothing in it breaks the design outright, and most of it supports the design. The main practical lesson is that the head and the bracketing of quite ordinary nominals differ from one HPSG analysis to the next, which matters for the UD bridge and for any "universal" relaxed schemas.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Extraction out of nominals and other discontinuous NPs

- Severity: medium (low for English, higher for the multi-language goal)
- Assumption affected: continuity / tree-encoding / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 3.5, p. 307
- What the chapter says: French *Combien as-tu lu [ _ de livres en latin]?* (ex. 51) and Dutch *Wat zijn dat [ _ voor vreemde geluiden]?* (ex. 52) are "nominals with an extracted wh-word"; the examples are printed with a gap inside the bracketed NP. The chapter gives no analysis, only pointers: Abeillé et al. (2004: 20-21) for French, Van Eynde (2004: 47-50) for Dutch, and De Kuthy (2002) for "other kinds of discontinuous NPs" (German).
- Why it matters: the quantifier or wh-word and the rest of its nominal are not adjacent. Whether these are handled as an ordinary filler-gap dependency (compatible with a continuous tree and no empty leaf) or as true discontinuity is not stated here, so it has to be checked in the primary sources. Either way the nominal's left edge is missing from the string, and UD would attach the two parts in one tree.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 2. The head and the bracketing of a nominal depend on the analysis chosen

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / tree-encoding (and the choice of "coarse universal schemas" for relaxed output)
- Reference: Sections 2.1-2.3, pp. 278-295; Section 3.3, pp. 299-304; Section 3.4, pp. 304-306; Section 3.5, pp. 306-307
- What the chapter says:
  - *that sister of Leslie*: the noun heads the whole phrase in the specifier and functor treatments (Figures 3, 9); the determiner heads it in the DP treatment (Figure 6).
  - *so good a bargain*: left-branching [[[so good] a] bargain] in Ginzburg and Sag (2000: 201); [[so good] [a bargain]] with AP and NP as sisters in all functor treatments (pp. 300-301).
  - *a hundred pages*: left-branching [[a hundred] pages], because *a* cannot combine with a plural noun (Section 2.3.3, p. 293).
  - *the Queen of England's sister*: [[[the Queen of England] 's] sister], with *'s* heading a DetP that is the specifier of *sister* (Section 2.1.3, pp. 283-284).
  - *beaucoup de farine*: *de* is a "weak head" that takes *farine* as its complement in Abeillé et al. (2004), but a non-head functor in the functor treatment, where *farine* is the head of *de farine* (pp. 304-305).
  - *these sort of problems* and *her nitwit of a husband*: in the functor analyses (Maekawa 2015; Van Eynde 2018) the second noun is the head of the whole NP, and *of* plus the first noun are functors (pp. 306-307).
- Why it matters: the HPSG-to-UD and UD-to-HPSG conversions need head rules and bracketing rules per construction, and these are properties of a particular grammar, with no single "HPSG" answer. The same holds for relaxed output built from universal schemas: "head-specifier", "head-functor" and "head-complement" are rival descriptions of the same determiner-noun combination. The chapter does not say which analysis any implemented grammar uses.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Possessive *'s* is a syntactic word

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 2.1.3, pp. 283-284, footnote 8; footnote 20, p. 299
- What the chapter says: in the specifier treatment *'s* is a determiner that takes an NP specifier and heads a DetP (Figure 5, AVM 15). Footnote 8 argues it is not an affix (*the Queen's of England sister* is bad) and compares it to the reduced auxiliary in *he's ill*, which heads a VP. The analysis is recursive (*John's uncle's car*, footnote 9). Malouf (2000), by contrast, treats the English possessive as a genitive (footnote 20).
- Why it matters: under the word analysis, *'s* must be a leaf of its own, so "leaves correspond to tokens" depends on a tokenization that splits the clitic; under the genitive analysis it is part of the preceding word's form but marks a whole phrase. The tagger's token inventory has to follow whichever analysis the grammar takes.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Constructions that constrain the phrase type of their daughters; growing rule inventory

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3.1, pp. 295-297; Section 3.3.2, pp. 300-304; Section 3.2, pp. 298-299
- What the chapter says: Constructional HPSG replaces the "small inventory of highly abstract phrase types" (seven in Pollard and Sag 1994: 391) with a fine-grained, bidimensional hierarchy with multiple inheritance (HEADEDNESS crossed with CLAUSALITY; Figures 12, 14, 17). Maximal types add idiosyncratic constraints. The *big-mess-phrase* constraint (44) requires its head daughter to be of type *regular-nominal-phrase* with MARKING *a*, and its non-head daughter to be a *head-functor-phrase* headed by an adjective with MARKING *marked*. Recursion is blocked by a type incompatibility between *regular-nominal-phrase* and *big-mess-phrase*. Semantic composition (index sharing, union of restrictions) is stated on the CLAUSALITY types (42, 43).
- Why it matters: (a) the label vocabulary of the tagger grows with the number of maximal construction types; (b) a rule here inspects which rule built its daughter, so the replay engine must keep the phrase type of each sign available to later rules (a daughter cannot be reduced to its SYNSEM); (c) a rule's constraints come from several supertypes at once, so the engine needs the full multiple-inheritance type hierarchy. None of this conflicts with search-free replay, since the tree names the rule at each node.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Quantifier storage: scope is fixed at a retrieval site

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: semantics / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 2.1.2, pp. 282-283 (AVM 14); also AVMs (15) p. 284 and (47) p. 305
- What the chapter says: in the Ginzburg and Sag (2000) semantics the chapter uses, a quantifying determiner's content (a *quant-rel*) "is put in store, to be retrieved at the place where its scope is determined". The chapter does not say where retrieval happens or what decides it. The semantic representation throughout is index-restriction pairs (*scope-object*, *parameter*, *quant-rel*) with STORE.
- Why it matters: if the retrieval site is a free choice, the derivation tree alone does not fix the semantics. The chapter's semantics is also a different formalism from MRS, so its semantic details (SPEC as *semantic-object*, parameter-to-quantifier shift) do not carry over directly to "MRS as a by-product".
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed (MRS is never mentioned in this chapter).

### 6. Mixed categories: gerunds with nominal outside and verbal inside

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 3.2, pp. 297-299; Section 3.5, p. 307
- What the chapter says: gerund phrases have the distribution of NPs but take NP complements, adverbs, and either an accusative or a possessive subject (ex. 32). Malouf (2000) makes *gerund* a subtype of both *noun* and *relational* (Figure 13), derives gerunds from present participles by a lexical rule (33) that puts the subject on both SUBJ and SPR, and licenses the two subject options with two constructions: *nonfin-head-subj-cx* (accusative subject, a clause) and *noun-poss-cx* (possessive, a non-clause). Nominals with an adjectival core (*the very poor*) get a comparable treatment in Arnold and Spencer (2015).
- Why it matters: one token carries a part of speech that is noun and verb-like at once, and the same word string *Brown('s) painting his daughter* is a clause or a non-clause depending on the subject's case. A UD mapping has to assign one UPOS and one set of relation labels to such heads. For the tagger this is one more unary lexical rule in the label, and the choice of the two constructions is visible in the tree, so replay is unaffected.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. String-vacuous alternatives: lexical rule versus non-branching phrase, and homophonous entries

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / binary-unary-rules
- Reference: Section 3.5, pp. 307-308 and footnote 24; Section 2.3.2, p. 293; Section 3.2, p. 298
- What the chapter says: predicative nominals (*Leslie is my sister*) get an unexpressed subject through a lexical rule in Ginzburg and Sag (2000: 409), and through a non-branching phrasal projection in Müller (2009: 225); Van Eynde (2015) uses neither. Adnominal and pronominal *that* are the same part of speech and differ only in SELECT value (p. 293). Gerunds are homophonous with the present participles they derive from (p. 298).
- Why it matters: all options are unary and fit the design, but each is a decision with no surface reflex that the tagger must make from context (which lexical entry, whether a unary rule applies, and at word or phrase level).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Semantically vacuous words and weak heads

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 3.4, pp. 304-306; Section 3.5, pp. 307-308
- What the chapter says: in the weak-head analysis (46), French *de* copies HEAD, SUBJ, SPR and CONTENT from its complement, so it is a noun in *beaucoup de farine* and a verb in *de sortir un peu* (ex. 48). In the functor analysis *de* is "uniformly treated as a semantically vacuous preposition". The copula is a semantically vacuous raising verb in Ginzburg and Sag (2000) and a contentful relation (THEME, ATTRIBUTE) in Van Eynde (2015: 158-163).
- Why it matters: some tokens contribute no predication to the semantics, and which ones do is analysis-dependent, so token-to-predicate alignment (useful for evaluating MRS and for the UD bridge) is not one-to-one. A weak head also has no part of speech of its own, which complicates any POS-based conversion.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Head-argument phrases may have more than one non-head sister

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules
- Reference: Section 2.3.2, p. 291; footnote 18, p. 296
- What the chapter says: "In head-argument phrases, the head daughter selects its non-head sister(s) by means of valence features", and Pollard and Sag's seven schemata include *head-subject-complements*. All trees actually drawn in the chapter are binary, and the weak-head entry (46) has a one-element COMPS list.
- Why it matters: theoretical HPSG does not commit to binary branching, so analyses taken from the literature may need binarizing before they fit the encoding.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- Surface orientation is the chapter's own yardstick. All three treatments are "surface-oriented and monostratal" (Section 2.4, p. 295), and the DP treatment is criticized because it "does not mesh as well with the monostratal surface-oriented nature of the HPSG framework" (abstract, p. 275; conclusion, p. 308).
- No HPSG analysis presented in the chapter uses an empty element. The only empty position mentioned is the "potentially empty specifier" of the transformational DP analysis (Section 1, p. 277, Figure 2). Bare plurals and mass nouns are described as "well-formed without a specifier" (Section 2.3.1, p. 288), and the functor treatment needs nothing extra for them, since a nominal without a determiner is simply *unmarked*.
- Every tree in the chapter is continuous, with words as leaves and binary branching (Figures 3-7, 9, 10, 15, 16, 18).
- Syntax and semantics are built together: "Phrase formation and semantic composition are, hence, modeled in tandem" and semantic representations "do not constitute a separate level" (Section 2.1.2, p. 281). This is the property that makes semantics a by-product of replay.
- Combination is driven by lexical information: the properties of phrases "are mainly determined by properties of the constituent words" (Section 3.1, pp. 295-296). Co-occurrence restrictions are carried by lexical features (SPR, COMPS, MOD, SPEC, SELECT, MARKING) and propagate locally from daughter to mother (Head Feature Principle; MARKING constraints 27, 28). A tag that fixes the lexical type therefore fixes most of what replay needs.
- Lexical rules (gerund rule 33; predicative-noun rule) and Müller's (2009) non-branching projection are unary operations, as the tag format expects.
- Even the idiosyncratic constructions are handled by adding named phrase types to a hierarchy (Section 3.3.2, p. 304: "the addition of a type to the bidimensional hierarchy of phrase types"), which corresponds to adding a rule name to the label vocabulary.
- Ungrammatical orders are excluded by local feature clashes (*long that bridge*, *the that bridge*, *a so good bargain*, *those a hundred pages*; pp. 292-294, 301), which is what a replay check can detect.

## Theory strains

1. NP versus DP (Sections 1, 2.2, 2.4; pp. 275-278, 284-287, 295). Three treatments coexist inside HPSG. Netter's DP treatment drops SPR in the NP but needs a MAJOR/MINOR split, the FCOMPL feature, and two extra principles (17, 18). It forces adjective-like determiners (German *welch-*, Italian *questo*) to be [+N, -V], forces determiner and noun to share case and number even where they disagree (Dutch *'s lands hoogste bergen*), and wrongly requires all complements of adpositions to be functionally complete (Dutch *te paard*, *per trein*). A reviewer notes the last point is a problem of Netter's version only.
2. Mutual selection in the specifier treatment (Section 2.1.2, p. 283; p. 305). The noun selects the determiner through SPR and the determiner selects the noun through SPEC. The value type of SPEC has changed (synsem in Pollard and Sag 1994: 45; *semantic-object* in Ginzburg and Sag 2000: 362), and Abeillé et al. (2004) go back to synsem (footnote 23, p. 304).
3. Specifier versus adjunct (Section 2.3.1, p. 288). The usual criteria (obligatory and non-stackable versus optional and stackable) fail: bare plurals and mass nouns lack specifiers, and determiners stack (*il nostro futuro*, Greek demonstrative plus article, *his every wish*). The functor treatment abolishes the distinction.
4. Lexical versus functional categories; the category Det (Section 2.3.1, pp. 288-290). Determiners are categorially heterogeneous: adjectival (Dutch *onze*, *welke*; Italian *nostro*), nominal or pronominal (Dutch *wiens*, *wat*; Italian *loro*; English *that*), adverbial (French *beaucoup*, English *enough*). A separate functional category is called more stipulative.
5. Cost of the functor treatment (Sections 2.3.4, 3.3.2, 3.4.2; pp. 294-295, 300-305). MARKING takes over the work of SPR and multiplies subtypes: *bare*, *incomplete*, *a*, *de*, and more for Italian (Allegranza 2007). Footnote 21 (p. 300) has to stress that MARKING *a* is not the PHONOLOGY of the article. Because SELECT is a HEAD feature, an AP cannot select what its head adjective does not, so the Big Mess Construction needs a dedicated phrase type and the *head-independent-phrase* (pp. 301-303).
6. Constituency of the Big Mess Construction (Section 3.3, pp. 299-304). Ginzburg and Sag's left-branching structure treats *so good a* as a constituent, for which there is no evidence (ex. 38, 39). The functor analysis is challenged by a reviewer for licensing *so big an expensive red house*; the author replies that such strings may be well-formed (ex. 45).
7. Possessive *'s*: a determiner word (Pollard and Sag 1994; Ginzburg and Sag 2000; Sag, Wasow and Bender 2003) or a genitive (Malouf 2000) (footnotes 8 and 20, pp. 283, 299).
8. Weak heads (Section 3.4, pp. 304-306). *de* as weak head is a noun in one context and a verb in another; applied to *these sort of problems*, weak heads would make *sort* plural, which is why Maekawa (2015) rejects them.
9. Gerunds (Section 3.2, pp. 297-299). The analysis needs a reorganized part-of-speech hierarchy, a lexical rule, a subject listed on both SUBJ and SPR, and two construction types. Gerunds were also the original motivation for the DP approach in Transformational Grammar.
10. Predicative nominals and the copula (Section 3.5, pp. 307-308). "Opinions diverge": a lexical rule adding an unexpressed subject (Ginzburg and Sag 2000), a non-branching phrasal projection (Müller 2009), or ordinary nominals with a contentful copula (Van Eynde 2015).
11. Lexicalism versus constructions (Section 3.1, pp. 295-296; Section 4, p. 308). The analysis of idiosyncratic nominals "often requires a relaxation of the strictly lexicalist approach of early HPSG".
12. Non-intersective adjectives (*alleged*, *fake*) need a different semantic treatment from the index-sharing one shown (footnote 6, p. 282); the chapter only points to Pollard and Sag (1994: 330-331).

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter never says which treatment any implemented grammar uses. Which analysis the ERG takes for determiners, possessive *'s*, gerunds, and the Big Mess Construction has to be read off the grammar itself.
- How the specifier treatment licenses determinerless NPs (bare plurals, mass nouns, proper names) is not explained: the adjective entry (7) requires a non-empty SPR on the noun, but nothing is said about how that requirement is discharged without a determiner (a unary rule, a lexical alternative, or an empty determiner). This matters directly for the no-empty-elements and unary-rule assumptions.
- Nominals with no overt noun (elliptical or "null-headed" nominals such as *the red one* / *the poor*, *two of them*) are only touched through the titles Nerbonne and Mullen 2000 and Arnold and Spencer 2015. They are an obvious place where empty heads have been proposed.
- Mutual selection through SPR and SPEC, when SPEC is of type synsem, looks as if it produces a cyclic feature structure (the determiner's synsem contains the noun's synsem, which contains the determiner's synsem on SPR). The chapter does not raise this; it is worth checking against what the replay engine's unifier allows.
- Quantifier scope in MRS, and how it differs from the STORE mechanism shown here, is not discussed.
- Relative clauses are excluded on purpose (footnote 2, p. 278; see chapter 14). Coordination inside NPs, numerals beyond *a hundred pages*, measure phrases, partitives, proper names, and compounds are not treated.
- The title of Kay and Sag 2012 mentions "discontinuous dependencies" (as in *so good a bargain that I can't resist*, the degree word and its extraposed clause), but the chapter says nothing about that dependency.
