# Chapter 12: Control and Raising (Anne Abeillé)

Handbook pages 489-535 (text 489-527, references 527-535). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter presents the HPSG treatment of raising and control as a lexical and mainly semantic distinction. Raising predicates (seem, expect, likely) share the whole synsem of one of their arguments with the unexpressed subject of their non-finite complement and assign that argument no semantic role; control predicates (want, persuade, eager) only coindex the two and do assign a role. Both classes take an unsaturated VP (or predicative XP) complement, never a clause or small clause, and both have the same tree; the argument for the phrasal complement is backed with Mauritian short/long verb forms. The analysis is extended to pro-drop (Italian) and to Balinese voice, where "subject" and "first ARG-ST member" come apart, and it is revised with the head feature XARG to cover obligatory control of an expressed subject, copy raising, and tag questions. Copular verbs and English auxiliaries are treated as subtypes of raising verbs, with a short account of inversion, the AUX feature, VP ellipsis, and pseudogapping.

The chapter is theoretical. It does not mention parsing or any implemented grammar or system (DELPH-IN, LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju are all absent), so every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is "not discussed". The only links to the DELPH-IN world are that the lexical descriptions use "an MRS-inspired semantics" (Copestake et al. 2005; p. 499) and that Bender and Flickinger (1999) is cited for tag questions (p. 514). Most of the chapter supports the design. The caveats are mostly about how much weight falls on the choice of lexical type, about non-binary structures, and about arguments that have no token.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Flat (non-binary) structures: two-complement VPs and the inversion construction

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 2.1, pp. 500-501 (Figure 3); Section 2.2, p. 505 (Figure 6); Section 3.2, p. 520 (Figure 8); Section 4.1, p. 524 (constraint 89 and footnote 25)
- What the chapter says: Object-raising, object-control, and transitive copular verbs are drawn with a ternary VP (V NP VP, V NP AP). Subject-auxiliary inversion is licensed by `initial-aux-ph`, "a specific (non-binary) construction", a subtype of head-subject-complement phrase whose daughters are the head, the subject, and all complements at once, and whose head must be [AUX +, INV +]. Polar interrogative clauses are subtypes of it. Footnote 25 records the alternative in Sag, Wasow and Bender (2003): the inverted auxiliary is a verb with two complements and no subject, which needs no extra phrase type but "requires an alternative valence description for auxiliary verbs".
- Why it matters: the planned tree encoding and replay assume unary and binary rules. The chapter's structures have to be binarized, and for inversion the two analyses put the cost in different places: a dedicated phrase type (a rule name in the tree) or a second lexical description (a choice inside the tag). The chapter does not say how a binary-branching grammar would state either.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 2. The raising/control distinction lives entirely in the lexical type; the tree is the same

- Severity: medium (for the UD bridge and for the tagger; none for replay)
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / tree-encoding / semantics
- Reference: Section 2, p. 497; Section 2.1, pp. 498-500; Section 2.2, pp. 503-505
- What the chapter says: "control and raising verbs have the same syntactic arguments"; "the syntactic structures are the same as for control verbs" (p. 500, Figures 2-3 against 5-6). The difference is token identity of synsem (raising, constraint 30) against coindexing (control, constraint 37), plus whether the matrix relation has a role for the shared argument (31-32 against 38-39).
- Why it matters: for replay this is ideal, since the right semantics follows once the lexical type is fixed. But nothing in the tree shape signals the difference, so the tagger must get the lexical type right from context, and a conversion from a dependency tree into an HPSG derivation has to recover the type from the lemma. A wrong choice (control for raising) will often still unify, giving a "strict" analysis with a wrong role assignment; it fails only with expletive, idiomatic, non-nominal, or absent subjects (examples 4-10, pp. 491-492).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Several lexical descriptions per predicate, and predicates that switch class

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (size and ambiguity of the lexical-type part of the tag)
- Reference: Section 2.1, p. 502 (35); Section 2.2, p. 506 (41); Section 1.2, p. 493, footnote 4; Section 2.5, p. 514, footnote 19; Section 4.2, p. 526 (93)
- What the chapter says: Verbs that also take a finite clause need "another lexical description" (seem with expletive it and S; expect, want, promise with S), treated as valence alternations "available for some items (or some classes of items) but not all". Some verbs allow subject, object, or joint control (propose, footnote 4). Some verbs behave as raising or as control depending on the agentivity of the subject (English begin, stop; French menacer, promettre; footnote 19). Elliptical auxiliaries get "an additional lexical description".
- Why it matters: each alternation multiplies the lexical types the tagger must distinguish for one word form, and several of the distinctions (agentivity, joint against single control) are semantic and not visible in the local string.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Ellipsis after auxiliaries: an unexpressed VP on ARG-ST and inherited complements

- Severity: medium (for MRS as a by-product), low for replay
- Assumption affected: semantics / no-empty-elements / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 4.2, pp. 525-526 (92-94); also Section 2.1, p. 502 (34) and Section 1.1, p. 492, footnote 3
- What the chapter says: VP ellipsis (I will) and pseudogapping (I will the play) "could be captured" by an extra entry for the auxiliary (93) whose ARG-ST contains a VP of the non-canonical synsem type pro, and whose COMPS list is the COMPS list of that unexpressed VP. An empty inherited list gives VP ellipsis, a non-empty one gives pseudogapping. Some raising verbs and control verbs also allow a null complement (John just started; John tried). The chapter notes that the line between VP ellipsis and null complement anaphora "is not always easy to make". Auxiliaries can also be stranded in non-restrictive relatives (Kim was singing, which Lee wasn't).
- Why it matters: no empty element is posited, which fits the design. But the entry does not say what the elided VP means or how many complements it passes up: the inherited COMPS list is whatever the tree supplies, and the remnant in pseudogapping (tacos, the play) is a complement of a verb that is not in the sentence. Replay will succeed, yet the semantics obtained is incomplete, and the UD side has to attach the remnant to the auxiliary. The wording "could be" shows that the analysis is a sketch.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter refers to Nykiel and Kim (Chapter 19) and Sag et al. (2020).

### 5. Complex predicates: the complement may be a bare V, with argument inheritance

- Severity: medium for the many-languages goal, low for English
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 2.1, p. 499, footnote 8; Section 3.2, p. 519, footnote 21; Section 4.2, p. 526, footnote 26
- What the chapter says: The category of the complement in the raising types is deliberately not fixed as VP, "because it may be a V in some Romance languages with a flat structure" and in verb-final languages where matrix and non-finite verb form a verbal complex (German, Dutch, Japanese, Persian, Korean). French auxiliaries are "generalized" raising verbs that inherit "not only the subject but also any complement" of the participle (Abeillé and Godard 2002), following Hinrichs and Nakazawa (1989, 1994) for German; the same is done for Dutch (Bouma and van Noord 1998) and Persian. In some languages copular constructions are complex predicates too.
- Why it matters: with argument inheritance the valence of a head is known only after it combines with its verbal complement, and the complements of the lower verb are sisters of (or attach above) the higher verb. Derivation trees for these languages will not look like the English VP-embedding trees, and the mapping to dependency trees, where each argument hangs from its semantic head, becomes non-local. The chapter leaves the details to Chapters 10 and 11.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. List append with an open list in the raising constraints

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (what the feature-structure engine must support)
- Reference: Section 2.1, p. 499 (30); Section 2.4, p. 510 (53); Section 4.2, p. 526 (93)
- What the chapter says: The raising types are stated with list concatenation over a list of unknown length: ARG-ST is the complement's SUBJ list appended to the rest, and that list may be empty, so that subjectless complements (German impersonal passives under scheinen, example 10; Mauritian example 43) fall out. Balinese voice (53) and the elliptical auxiliary (93) are stated with append as well.
- Why it matters: the chapter uses append as a relational constraint on descriptions. An engine that only does unification of typed feature structures needs these constraints recast (for example fixed-length lists per lexical type, or a list encoding where append is unification). The chapter gives no such recasting.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. XARG: information about the subject must stay visible on saturated phrases

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: semantics / other (feature geometry the engine and the relaxed schemas must carry)
- Reference: Section 2.5, pp. 513-515 (59-64); Section 4.1, p. 525 (91)
- What the chapter says: Obligatory control of an expressed subject (Romanian, Japanese, Persian; Mauritian pans with an optional coindexed clitic subject, example 59), control of possessives in idioms (lose one's temper), copy raising (Peter looks like he's tired; There looks like there's going to be a storm), and tag questions all need access to the subject of a phrase whose SUBJ list is already empty. Sag and Pollard (1991) proposed a semantic feature EXT-ARG; Sag (2007) a head feature XARG whose value is the first syntactic argument of the head. Tag auxiliaries (91) are "special adjuncts" that coindex their pronominal subject with the XARG of the clause they modify, share TENSE with it, and reverse its polarity through a function `not`. The revised raising and control types (64) are stated on XARG, and the chapter adds that "this approach does not work for those languages allowing subjectless verbs".
- Why it matters: these constructions replay correctly only if the grammar percolates XARG, so coarse universal schemas for the relaxed output need it too. The polarity function in (91) is a relational constraint, not unification. Copy raising and tags create coreference between two overt tokens that a tree-shaped dependency analysis does not show.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed (Bender and Flickinger 1999 is cited for tag questions, without description).

### 8. Arguments with no token: pro subjects and unexpressed controlled subjects

- Severity: low for English, medium for pro-drop languages
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / UD-bridge / semantics
- Reference: Section 2.4, pp. 509-510 (50-51); Section 4.2, p. 526 (93)
- What the chapter says: Following Bouma, Malouf and Sag (2001), all syntactic arguments are on ARG-ST and only canonical ones on the valence lists. Null-subject verbs have a first argument of the non-canonical synsem type pro on ARG-ST and an empty SUBJ list (Manning and Sag 1999). Raising and control then work unchanged on ARG-ST (Italian posso venire, voglio venire).
- Why it matters: this is compatible with the no-empty-elements assumption and needs no extra leaf. The cost is that each finite verb token needs a tag that says whether its subject is realized, and the resulting semantic argument has no token for the dependency side to point at.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. "Subject" is not always the first argument (Balinese voice)

- Severity: low for English, medium for a universal relaxed schema and for the UD bridge
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / other (universal schemas)
- Reference: Section 2.4, pp. 510-513 (52-58)
- What the chapter says: In Balinese (Wechsler and Arka 1998) the objective-voice verb maps its second ARG-ST member to SUBJ and the agent to COMPS (53-54). Only the SUBJ member can be raised or controlled, whichever ARG-ST position it has. Commitment verbs additionally require the controllee to be an agent, so the lower verb must be in agentive voice (57); "nothing else has to be added".
- Why it matters: a coarse schema or a UD conversion that equates subject, first argument, and agent will be wrong for such languages. Control has to be stated on SUBJ (or XARG) and binding on ARG-ST.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 10. Controller choice and joint control are semantic

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 1.2, pp. 492-493 and footnote 4; Section 2.2, pp. 502-503 (Figure 4, 36); Section 1.1, p. 490 (3)
- What the chapter says: Which argument controls follows from the semantic class of the relation (orientation, commitment, influence), organized in a type hierarchy. Joint control (John proposed to Mary to go to the movies together) needs "a cumulative (i+j) index". Arbitrary or anaphoric control (Buying a coat can be expensive) is set aside as a different phenomenon and gets no analysis.
- Why it matters: the type hierarchy handles the standard cases by plain coindexing. A summed index is not a product of unification, and the arbitrary-control cases leave an unbound argument in the semantics.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 11. AUX fixed by the construction, and a contrast that depends on stress

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / other (information not in the token string)
- Reference: Section 4, pp. 522-524 (87-88)
- What the chapter says: In Kim and Sag (2002) AUX is a lexical head feature. In Sag et al. (2020) it is "both lexical and constructional": non-auxiliaries are [AUX -, INV -], auxiliaries are unspecified for AUX and get the value from the construction they occur in, "except for unstressed do, which is [AUX +]" and must occur in auxiliary-only constructions (*John does work, with unstressed do). First-person aren't is lexically [INV +], better is [INV -].
- Why it matters: construction-specified features are exactly what replay by unification handles. The do contrast depends on stress, which a text token does not carry, so a text parser must either accept or reject John does work without the conditioning information.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 12. Smaller points

- Heavy-NP shift reorders the two complements of object-raising and copular verbs (examples 24 and 72; Section 1.3, p. 496; Section 3.1, p. 517). The chapter uses it as a constituency test and gives no analysis; the binary rule set must license both orders. Severity: low. Assumption: tree-encoding. DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.
- Polish case on predicative adjectives under control (Section 2.2, pp. 504-506, example 40): coindexing "does not prevent full sharing", so the analysis allows both shared nominative and default instrumental, with "a specific constraint" added for some verbs. One tree is then compatible with two case assignments unless the lexical type decides. Severity: low. Assumption: deterministic-replay. DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.
- Verbless clauses exist in AAVE, French, creoles, Slavic, and Semitic languages (Section 3.1, p. 516, example 69). The chapter mentions them only to deny that copular verbs take clausal complements and gives no analysis. Severity: low. Assumption: other. DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.
- In Mauritian, some adverbs trigger the short verb form and are treated as complements by Hassamal (2017) (Section 2.3, p. 507, footnote 12). A complement/adjunct mismatch with dependency annotation is likely. Severity: low. Assumption: UD-bridge. DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- No movement and no empty categories. "Raising" is "just a cover term, since no raising move is taking place" (Section 2, p. 497). The unexpressed subject is an element of the complement's SUBJ list, never a leaf. Pro-drop and ellipsis use non-canonical synsem types on ARG-ST, again without leaves (pp. 509, 526).
- One continuous tree shape for raising and control (Figures 2, 3, 5, 6, pp. 501, 505), and the same shape for copular verbs (Figures 7-8, p. 520) and auxiliaries (Section 4, p. 522). All examples in the chapter are continuous.
- The semantics follows from the lexical type plus structure sharing: once the tree and the lexical types are given, sharing of SUBJ (raising) or of the index (control) is forced by unification, with no choice left. The descriptions are stated in "an MRS-inspired semantics" (p. 499).
- Object-raising verbs are two-complement verbs, with no exceptional case marking and no small clause (Section 1.3, pp. 495-496; Section 3.1, pp. 515-517). The NP after expect or consider is an ordinary object, which matches a surface-oriented dependency analysis.
- Auxiliaries, the copula, and infinitival to are verbs (subject-raising, [AUX +]), with no Infl category (Section 4, pp. 522-523 and footnote 24). The whole class is covered by lexical types plus a small number of constructions.
- A lexical type hierarchy with two dimensions (part of speech, argument selection; Figure 1, p. 498) captures raising and control verbs, adjectives, and nouns, which suits a tagger whose labels are lexical types.
- Subjectless complements are covered by the same constraint (the shared SUBJ list may be empty; p. 499), so no special rule is needed.
- The Mauritian short/long form alternation is a lexical constraint on the verb's own COMPS list (46, p. 508), checkable locally.
- The Balinese voice facts follow from the control analysis plus the voice types, "and nothing else has to be added" (p. 513).

## Theory strains

- Raising Principle (Section 2.1, pp. 500-502; restated in the conclusion, p. 527). Pollard and Sag's (1994: 140) principle is a prose biconditional over lexical entries, meant to stop raising verbs from dropping their VP complement. It is contradicted by null complements of some raising verbs (John just started) and by VP ellipsis with auxiliaries, so it "should be reformulated in terms of argument structure". The reformulation is not given, and the conclusion still relies on the principle to rule out other non-canonical linkings.
- Two analyses of raising and control inside the chapter: sharing or coindexing SUBJ (30, 37) against coindexing XARG (64) (Section 2.5, pp. 513-515). The XARG version covers expressed controlled subjects, copy raising, and tags, but "does not work" for languages with subjectless verbs. In (64) raising is also stated as coindexing, differing from control only in that the control index must be referential, which sits uneasily with the earlier argument that raising needs full synsem identity (case preservation in Icelandic and German, pp. 495, 497-498). The chapter does not reconcile the two.
- EXT-ARG as a semantic feature (Sag and Pollard 1991) against XARG as a head feature (Sag 2007) (Section 2.5, p. 513); both weaken the locality of selection that Section 3.1 (p. 517) uses as an argument against small clauses.
- Semantic control theory and its exceptions (Sections 1.2 and 2.2, pp. 492-493, 502-503). Controller choice is said to follow from the semantic class, but Romance verbs of mental representation and speech report are subject-control without commitment or orientation semantics; some verbs allow subject, object, and joint control; Visser's generalization has English counterexamples (Pat was promised to be allowed to leave) and fails in German.
- Verbs with both raising-like and control-like behavior depending on agentivity (Section 2.5, p. 514, footnote 19), which blurs a distinction the analysis encodes as two lexical types.
- Multiple lexical descriptions for clausal-complement variants (35, 41; pp. 502, 506) are called valence alternations that hold for some items and not others, without a stated generalization.
- AUX: lexical feature (Kim and Sag 2002) against lexical-and-constructional feature (Sag et al. 2020) (Section 4, pp. 522-524). Unstressed do needs a special lexical stipulation.
- Inversion: a dedicated flat head-subject-complement construction against an auxiliary entry with two complements and no subject (Section 4.1, p. 524, footnote 25).
- Ellipsis: the border between VP ellipsis and null complement anaphora "is not always easy" to draw (Section 4.2, p. 525); the pro-VP entry (93) is offered tentatively.
- Copula: predicational be as a raising verb against identity be, which is not a raising verb (Section 4, p. 522, footnote 23, citing Van Eynde 2008 and Müller 2009; also Van Eynde 2015 on p. 518). A few predicative-complement verbs (feel) are control verbs (p. 519).
- Case under control: Polish needs both sharing and non-sharing options plus a verb-specific constraint (Section 2.2, pp. 504-506).
- Framework competition: against transformational raising, ECM, and small clauses (Sections 1.3 and 3.1, pp. 494-496, 515-517; the Movement Theory of Control is set aside in footnote 5, p. 494); with LFG the analysis is said to agree (functional against anaphoric control, footnote 1, p. 490; conclusion, p. 527). Jacobson's (1990) function-composition view supplies the null-complement test that the chapter then weakens (footnote 3, p. 492).
- Feature geometry: Henri and Laurens (2011) and Sag et al. (2020) are written in Sign-Based Construction Grammar and "translated" into Constructional HPSG (Sag 1997) for the handbook (footnote 13, p. 509; p. 524).
- Mauritian: finite clausal complements are analyzed as extraposed so that they do not trigger the short form (Henri 2010; Section 2.3, p. 508), and some adverbs as complements because they do (footnote 12, p. 507). Both moves keep the morphological generalization at the cost of the usual complement/adjunct division.

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not statements from the chapter.

- The chapter says nothing about how any implemented grammar encodes raising and control. How the ERG types its raising and control verbs, whether it uses XARG for tags and copy raising, and how it binarizes two-complement VPs and inversion must be checked in the grammar itself.
- The MRS-level difference between raising and control (a shared variable against no argument position for the raised element) is only implicit in the lexical descriptions; the chapter does not discuss scope, handle constraints, or how the raised argument's absence appears in MRS.
- UD's distinction between xcomp and ccomp, and enhanced dependencies for controlled subjects, are relevant to caveat 2 and are not mentioned in the chapter.
- Tough-constructions, control into adjuncts, and partial or split control beyond the one footnote on joint control are absent.
- Scope ambiguities of raising verbs with quantified subjects (a standard argument in the raising literature) are not discussed.
- The Raising Principle is a constraint on the lexicon as a whole. Whether it can be enforced inside a type hierarchy, or is only a design guideline for grammar writers, is left open by the chapter.
