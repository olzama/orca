# Chapter 17: Idioms (Manfred Sailer)

Handbook pages 777-809 (text 777-799, appendix 800-801, references from 802). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter surveys idioms in the broad sense of multiword expressions (prototypical idioms, support verb constructions, formulaic expressions, collocations) and sorts their irregularity into lexical, syntactic, semantic, pragmatic, and statistical idiomaticity (Section 2). It then sets out the pre-HPSG background: holistic insertion in Mainstream Generative Grammar, the link between semantic decomposability and syntactic flexibility in Wasow, Sag and Nunberg (1984) and Nunberg, Sag and Wasow (1994), and its GPSG implementation (Section 3). The core (Section 4) classifies HPSG analyses by how local they keep the grammar, using four hypotheses (Strong/Weak Locality, Strong/Weak Non-locality): the early lexical account of Krenn and Erbach (1994), the phrasal account of Riehemann (2001), the mixed "two-dimensional" account of Sailer, Richter, and Soehn, and the recent fully lexical accounts of Kay, Sag and Flickinger (2015) and Bargmann and Sailer (2018). Section 5 lists neglected phenomena and challenges from languages other than English and German.

The chapter is theoretical and says very little about parsing. Its only statement about implemented grammars is footnote 21 (p. 790): versions of Riehemann's approach are in practical parsing systems (Villavicencio and Copestake 2002), and Flickinger (2015) identifies idioms in parser output from bits of semantic representation. Everything else under "DELPH-IN stance" below is "not discussed". The bearing on the parser is moderate: idioms mostly use regular syntax, and the trend the chapter describes (ever more lexical analyses) fits a design built on lexical types and local rules. The open points are where the idiom check happens, what the semantics of an idiom is, and syntactically irregular idioms.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Idiom licensing may not be a local property of any rule application

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 4, pp. 784-785 (hypotheses 4-7); Section 4.2, pp. 788-790 (constraint 12, footnotes 19 and 21); Section 4.3, pp. 791-793
- What the chapter says: Pollard and Sag (1994) hold the Strong Locality Hypothesis: rules and principles refer to one node and its immediate daughters. Several idiom analyses go beyond it. Riehemann (2001) gives each idiom a phrase type whose WORDS value contains all words the phrase dominates, so the idiom phrase can require the idiomatic words anywhere below it; the chapter calls the percolation mechanism "rather complex" and says that it "undermines the locality intuition" (fn. 19). The two-dimensional theory gives idiomatic words a REQ value that names a licenser (a lexeme, a semantic operator, or a phonological unit) and a domain stated as a syntactic barrier, for example "the smallest complete clause dominating it" (p. 793). The Weak Non-locality Hypothesis lets a sign constrain its internal structure "at arbitrary depth" (p. 785).
- Why it matters: replay applies named rules to their daughters. A condition that holds between an idiom phrase and words deep inside it, or between a word and a licenser somewhere in its clause, is checked by replay only if the grammar percolates the needed information through every intermediate node, or if a separate check runs after replay. In the second case the derivation tree is no longer the complete recipe for well-formedness.
- DELPH-IN stance: footnote 21 (p. 790). "Since the problem of free occurrences of idiomatic words is not an issue for parsing, versions of Riehemann's approach have been integrated into practical parsing systems (Villavicencio and Copestake 2002)". Flickinger (2015), part of a system for parsing and machine translation, identifies idioms "by bits of semantic representation - analogous to the elements in the WORDS set". The chapter adds that this "does not constitute a theoretical modeling of idioms; it does not exclude ill-formed uses of idioms but identifies potential occurrences of an idiom in the output of a parser". So the implemented practice reported here is a check on the output, after the parse.

### 2. Idiomatic words as extra lexical entries: more lexical types, and a literal/idiomatic choice per token

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (size and ambiguity of the lexical-type part of the tag)
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 786-787 (8, 10); Section 4.2, pp. 788-790; Section 4.4, pp. 793-794
- What the chapter says: All lexical accounts add separate entries for idiomatic words that are homophonous with ordinary words (idiomatic spill and beans; idiomatic kick, the, bucket), told apart by a lexeme-identity feature (LEXEME, LISTEME, LID). Kay et al. (2015) extend this to all syntactically regular idioms, so the and bucket get meaningless idiomatic entries. Findlay (2017) objects that this is "a massive proliferation of lexical entries for otherwise homophonous words" (p. 794). In Riehemann's account nothing stops an idiomatic word from occurring without the rest of its idiom (p. 790).
- Why it matters: the tagger has to choose between the literal and the idiomatic lexical type for each token of an idiom, and the choices must agree across tokens that can be far apart (passive, topicalization, relative clauses). Where the grammar enforces lexeme-specific selection (the idiomatic verb selects a complement with the idiomatic lexeme value, pp. 786, 793), an inconsistent choice makes replay fail, which is the desired behavior. Where the grammar does not restrict the free occurrence of idiomatic words, an inconsistent choice replays successfully and gives a wrong "strict" analysis.
- DELPH-IN stance: only footnote 21, as above: free occurrence of idiomatic words is "not an issue for parsing". The chapter does not say how many idiomatic entries implemented grammars have.

### 3. What the semantics of an idiom is, and where it comes from

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 786-787; Section 4.2, p. 789; Section 4.4, pp. 793-794; Section 5.1, p. 797
- What the chapter says: The analyses disagree on the source of the idiomatic meaning of non-decomposable idioms. Krenn and Erbach put it all on the verb, which selects the phonology of its complement (10). Riehemann gives the idiomatic words empty semantics and has the idiomatic phrase contribute the meaning as a constructional contribution, in MRS (p. 789). Kay et al. put all meaning on the idiomatic head and make the other words meaningless. Bargmann and Sailer have each part contribute the whole meaning redundantly. For decomposable idioms each part carries a piece of the idiomatic meaning. All HPSG accounts "basically ignore the literal meaning" (p. 797).
- Why it matters: MRS as a by-product of replay yields the idiomatic meaning only if the tagger chose the idiomatic entries (lexical accounts) or the idiomatic phrase type (phrasal account). Under the practice reported in footnote 21, the parser output carries the literal predicates and the idiom is recognized afterwards from them, so the MRS obtained by replay is the literal one. Semantically empty words also mean that some tokens contribute no predicate.
- DELPH-IN stance: footnote 21 only (Flickinger 2015 identifies idioms from bits of semantic representation in the parser output). The chapter notes that Riehemann (2001) is formulated in Constructional HPSG with MRS (p. 788).

### 4. Syntactically irregular idioms and "words with spaces"

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 2, p. 779; Section 4.3, pp. 791-792 (14)
- What the chapter says: Completely fixed idioms (of course, ad hoc) "are often called words with spaces" (Sag et al. 2002). Some idioms are not built by the productive rules of English syntax (by and large, trip the light fantastic). The two-dimensional theory gives trip the light fantastic a phrasal lexical entry (14) in which "the constituent structure of the phrase is not specified, but the phonology is fixed", apart from the phonology of the head daughter. Only the head daughter is described; the rest is a fixed string.
- Why it matters: a word with spaces is one lexical item over several tokens, so leaves are not tokens unless the tokenizer or the tree encoding joins them. A phrasal lexical entry with unspecified internal structure has no derivation subtree made of named rules, so the tree encoding needs either a multi-token leaf or special rules for such idioms. For UD, such expressions would correspond to several tokens (the chapter does not mention UD).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. Sag et al. (2002) is cited only for the three-way classification fixed / semi-fixed / flexible.

### 5. Idiom parts related through pronouns and relative clauses

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: semantics / deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 786-787 (examples 9 and 11)
- What the chapter says: Idiom parts can be pronominalized (spill all the beans ... spill them all) and can head a relative clause whose verb is not part of the idiom (Parky pulled the strings that got me the job). Krenn and Erbach put the lexeme identifier inside INDEX so that it is shared with phrases, traces, and pronouns. That predicts that the subject of got in (11) has the idiomatic lexeme value, which contradicts the assumption that only idiomatic pull selects such an argument. The chapter leaves the problem open.
- Why it matters: in "spill them" the idiomatic verb occurs with a pronoun, so the idiom cannot be recognized or licensed from the lexical types within one clause. Any consistency check on the tagger's idiomatic choices, and any identification of idioms from the semantics, has to deal with these cases.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Defaults and probabilities are outside the formal foundations

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / other
- Reference: Section 4.2, pp. 788-790, footnote 23
- What the chapter says: Riehemann (2001) states the relation between an idiomatic word and its ordinary homonym with a default operator: the idiomatic word is like the ordinary one except for the type of the word and of its predicate. Sag (1997) introduced defaults into HPSG. Footnote 23: "neither defaults nor probabilities are well-defined part of the formal foundations of theoretical work on HPSG".
- Why it matters: an engine that replays by plain unification has to compile such defaults away before it can apply the idiom constraints.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed (the chapter does not say how the implemented versions in footnote 21 treat the default).

### 7. Selection for phonology, whole signs, and traces in the early lexical account

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements / other
- Reference: Section 4.1, pp. 785-787 (10), footnote 20 on p. 789
- What the chapter says: Krenn and Erbach (1994) let valence lists contain whole signs, so a head can select the phonology and internal structure of its complement (kick selects PHON <the, bucket>), which the chapter counts as a possible violation of strong locality. Extraction is blocked because "the complement cannot be realized as a trace". Riehemann's account blocks extraction on the assumption that "extracted elements are not members of the valence lists" (fn. 20). The phonology selection wrongly excludes kick the social/figurative bucket (p. 787).
- Why it matters: these accounts rely on machinery (signs on valence lists, traces) that the design's assumptions exclude. Later accounts replace it with lexeme-identity selection, which is local and compatible.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Idioms in other languages: greater flexibility, underspecified structure, archaic forms

- Severity: medium for the many-languages goal, low for English
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / other
- Reference: Section 2, p. 780; Section 3, p. 783; Section 4.3, p. 792; Section 5.1, pp. 795-796 (16, 17); Section 5.2, pp. 797-798
- What the chapter says: In Dutch and German, semi-fixed idioms undergo verb-second and some fronting (Schenk 1995), and "noncompositional idioms are syntactically versatile" (Nunberg et al. 1994: 514). Soehn (2006) handles this with "underspecified descriptions of the constituent structure dominated by the idiomatic phrase". German idioms alternate between a definite article, a possessive, and a free dative possessor, with idiom-specific restrictions and no formal analysis yet. Not yet addressed in HPSG at all: clitics in idioms (Romance, Greek), aspectual alternations (Slavic, Greek), anti-passive, causative, and inchoative alternations, idiom parts in special positions (Hungarian focus), and archaic structures, case inflections, and function words preserved only in idioms (Norwegian, Dutch, Modern Greek). For the last group "it is far from clear what the best way would be to integrate such cases into an HPSG grammar" (p. 798).
- Why it matters: a grammar for these languages needs idiom-only lexical entries or rules for archaic forms, and the idiom check of caveat 1 has to work across verb placement and fronting. The chapter does not say that idioms require discontinuous constituents.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Lexical variation inside idioms

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (lexical-rule chain in the tag)
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 778-779, footnote 6; Section 5.1, p. 795 (15)
- What the chapter says: Some idioms allow systematic substitution of the verb (give / get / have the creeps) or of a noun by a near synonym (German Semmeln / Brötchen / Schrippen). The chapter suggests lexical rules that "change the lexeme itself", unlike standard lexical rules, or hierarchies of LID values. Neither is worked out, and the extent of the phenomenon is unknown.
- Why it matters: if adopted, the first option adds lexical rules of a new kind to the unary chain encoded in a tag.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- Idioms "largely obey the principles of grammar" (p. 781). Semi-fixed and flexible idioms have regular internal syntax, so the ordinary rules and the ordinary tree encoding cover their structure (pp. 779, 789).
- The chapter describes "a development towards ever more lexical analyses", ending with "a lexical account for all syntactically regular expressions" (pp. 798-799). Kay et al. (2015) keep the Strong Locality Hypothesis: idioms are handled by idiomatic lexical entries plus lexeme-specific selection (LID), with no idiom-specific phrase rules (p. 793). This is the form that fits a tagger choosing lexical types and a replay engine applying local rules: the idiom is licensed by unification of the selected LID value. The chapter calls the paper evidence for "the empirical robustness" of strong locality.
- Under both locality hypotheses every sign must be licensed by the lexicon or the grammar, which "precludes any en bloc-insertion analyses" (p. 785). No HPSG account reviewed inserts ready-made multi-node trees, with the exception of the phrasal lexical entries for syntactically irregular idioms (caveat 4).
- Borsley (2004) shows that an idiosyncrasy spanning two levels of embedding (the comparative correlative) can still be analyzed with special local phrase types; the chapter notes that "we can always encode a non-local construction in terms of a series of idiosyncratic local constructions" (p. 797). Kay and Fillmore (1999) give a lexical analysis of What's X doing Y? (p. 796).
- Footnote 21 (p. 790): the theoretical problem of free occurrence of idiomatic words "is not an issue for parsing", and idiom handling exists in practical parsing systems (Villavicencio and Copestake 2002; Flickinger 2015).
- Riehemann (2001) is stated in MRS, with idiomatic predicates as elementary predications (p. 788), so idiomatic meaning is expressible in the semantic representation the design targets.
- Bender (2001: 292) sketches HPSG with frequencies or probabilities attached to lexical and phrasal types, and Riehemann (2001) argues for an experience-based HPSG (p. 790). (My remark: this is the closest the chapter comes to a statistical component over grammar types, which is what the tagger is.)

## Theory strains

- Decomposability and flexibility (Section 3, pp. 782-783; Section 4.1, p. 787; Section 5.2, p. 798). The correlation "non-decomposable = semi-fixed" from Wasow et al. (1984) and Nunberg et al. (1994) underlies GPSG, Krenn and Erbach, and Sailer (2000). It fails for German, Dutch, and French, and even for English (attested passives of kick the bucket; Bargmann and Sailer 2018, Fellbaum 2019). Sheinfux et al. (2019) argue from Hebrew that opacity and figurativity, and not decomposability, decide flexibility. The chapter's verdict: the relation "is less direct than assumed".
- Number of flexibility classes (Section 2, pp. 779-780). Sag et al. (2002) have three levels, Horn (2003) splits the flexible class, Fraser (1970) has six classes, and Ruwet (1991) and Schenk (1995) dispute the cross-linguistic validity of the classification.
- Locality (Section 4, pp. 784-785). The chapter finds it "remarkable that a grammar framework that denies a core-periphery distinction would start with a strong assumption of locality, and, consequently, of regularity". This contrasts with Construction Grammar (Fillmore et al. 1988), where constructions can be arbitrarily deep. Pollard and Sag (1994) do not discuss idioms, and their architecture "does not have a direct place" for them (p. 783).
- Is weak non-locality empirically empty? (Section 5.1, p. 797). Since a non-local construction can always be recoded as a series of idiosyncratic local constructions, the chapter asks whether the Weak Non-locality Hypothesis has any empirical content.
- Lexical versus phrasal analysis (Sections 4.1-4.4, 6). Four competing families. Krenn and Erbach: too restrictive (excludes kick the social bucket) and the LEXEME-in-INDEX device gives wrong predictions for relative clauses (p. 787). Riehemann: explicit cross-classification of idiom types with constructions may miss generalizations; the WORDS percolation has no motivation outside idioms and "is not compatible with the intuitions of most HPSG researchers"; free occurrence of idiomatic words is unsolved (pp. 789-790). Two-dimensional theory: presupposes a notion of regularity that not all linguists share, and has unclear criteria for constructional versus collocational treatment (p. 793). Recent lexical accounts: proliferation of homophonous entries and loss of the unit status of idioms (Findlay 2017; p. 794). The author's own conclusion: "the right balance between phrasal and lexical aspects in the analysis of idioms has not yet been fully achieved" (p. 799).
- Empty versus redundant semantics (Section 4.4, pp. 793-794). Kay et al. make the and bucket meaningless and stipulate that the English passive cannot apply to verbs with a semantically empty object. Bargmann and Sailer make every part carry the whole meaning and derive inflexibility from construction-specific constraints (discourse constraints on English passive subjects). Pure selection (Kay et al.) competes with collocational licensing (Bargmann and Sailer).
- Stipulative devices (Sections 4.1-4.3). A dummy THETA-ROLE value nil to block the passive lexical rule (p. 787); a lexicon "built in such a way" that no other word selects the idiomatic lexeme (p. 786); the COLL and REQ features with a hierarchy of irregularity types (pp. 791-792); the Predictability Hypothesis (13).
- Bound words (Section 2, p. 778, footnote 5). The grammar is supposed to keep words such as trice inside their idiom, yet uses outside the idiom are attested, though rare; likewise bucket list (p. 790).
- Defaults and probabilities (footnote 23, p. 790). Used in Constructional HPSG idiom analyses and proposed for experience-based HPSG, but not a well-defined part of the formal foundations.
- Acknowledged gaps (Sections 2 and 5). Figurative interpretation; pragmatic idiomaticity ("little work"); statistical idiomaticity, whose status "for a competence-oriented framework such as HPSG is unclear" (p. 781); the literal meaning, ignored by all HPSG accounts (p. 797); lexical variation; possessor alternations; phraseological patterns; archaic structures in idioms; and the restriction of nearly all HPSG idiom work to English and German (p. 797).
- Against other frameworks (Section 3, pp. 781-782; footnote 24). MGG's en bloc insertion (Chomsky 1965, 1981) is taken as an "unquestioned assumption" there and criticized by Ruwet (1991) and Nunberg et al. (1994). The lexical analysis of non-decomposable idioms was also proposed in Minimalism (Everaert 2010; G. Müller 2011). Findlay's (2017) criticism of lexical accounts comes from outside HPSG.

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter does not say how the current ERG treats idioms (its idiom rules over MRS, its multiword lexical entries, or how many there are). Footnote 21 points to Villavicencio and Copestake (2002), Flickinger (2015), and handbook Chapter 25 (Bender and Emerson) for this.
- Universal Dependencies is not mentioned. UD's treatment of fixed expressions (the fixed, flat, and compound relations) and how it would map to words with spaces or to idiomatic lexical entries has to be worked out elsewhere.
- Tokenization is not discussed, so the chapter is silent on whether a word with spaces is one leaf or several.
- The chapter does not discuss discontinuity or word order domains for idioms, even for German verb-second and fronting; it only mentions Soehn's underspecified constituent structure.
- Light verb constructions and complex proper names are mentioned in passing as kinds of multiword expression but receive no analysis here. Particle verbs are not mentioned.
- Processing claims (idioms processed faster than free combinations) are mentioned only as an argument for unit status; nothing is said about parsing efficiency or ambiguity from idiomatic entries.
