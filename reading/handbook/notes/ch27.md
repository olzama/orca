# Chapter 27: Gesture (Andy Lücking)

Handbook pages 1201-1250 (text 1201-1234, then references).

## Summary

The chapter surveys co-verbal hand and arm gestures and how HPSG-style grammars have integrated them with speech. It introduces gesture classes (iconic, deictic, beat, emblem, interactive), gesture phases, and attribute-value representations of gesture form. It then reviews three families of integration schemes: the early multimodal chart parsing work of Johnston and colleagues, analyses of pointing (Rieser; Kühnlein et al.; Lascarides and Stone; Alahverdzhieva, Lascarides and Flickinger), and analyses of iconic gestures (Lücking's exemplification with vector semantics; Alahverdzhieva et al.'s underspecified RMRS predicates). It closes with pointers to corpora, robotics, learning, aphasia, and open problems.

**Bearing on the parser: small.** The chapter is about input in a second modality that a text parser never sees. Nothing here threatens the design for written text. The caveats below only matter if the parser is ever applied to transcribed spoken dialogue or extended to multimodal input, so they are all rated low.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Multimodal input: leaves on two layers

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens (and continuity)
- Reference: Section 3.2, pp. 1212-1214 (Figure 6, examples 9-10)
- Description: Gesture-aware grammars take input on a speech layer and a gesture layer. The multimodal chart parser of Johnston (1998) and Johnston et al. (1997) "consists of two or more layers and allows for layer-crossing charts"; an edge is a set of spans from both layers. A gesture daughter is a leaf that is no token of the word string and has its own time span, so a derivation is no longer a tree over one token sequence.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed for DELPH-IN engines. The chapter says a conventional constraint-based grammar "rests on a 'unimodal' parser", that multimodal chart parsing "is still a topic of computational linguistics" (citing Alahverdzhieva et al. 2012), and points to Bender and Emerson (Chapter 25).

### 2. Rules conditioned on timing and prosody

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (inputs the text does not carry)
- Reference: Section 3.2, p. 1214 (10); Section 3.4, pp. 1220-1221 (15); Section 3.5, pp. 1222-1224 (16), (18)
- Description: All integration schemes constrain the combination by information outside the word string: TIME values with OVERLAP or FOLLOW relations (gesture may follow its affiliate by at most four seconds in Johnston's scheme; strict overlap in the Situated Prosodic Word Constraint), and prosody (PHON nuclear_or_pre-nuclear; ACCENT marked on the affiliate). Replaying such a rule needs time stamps and accent marks on the leaves. Alahverdzhieva et al. also have a phrase-level scheme in which the stressed element must be a semantic head.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed as an engine matter. The chapter notes that such constraints can be stated through an interface to metrical trees (Klein 2000) or information packaging (Engdahl and Vallduví 1996).

### 3. Pro-speech gestures and gesture-dependent utterances in transcripts

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens
- Reference: Section 1, pp. 1202-1204 (examples 1, 3, 4)
- Description: In "mixed syntax" a gesture fills a position usually filled by a syntactic constituent ("He is a bit [gesture]"). With demonstrative *so* 'like this' the utterance "is not even interpretable without the gesture", and exophoric demonstratives are incomplete without a demonstration act. The chapter gives no HPSG analysis of pro-speech gestures.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed.
- My own remark (not from the chapter): in a text transcript of such an utterance the constituent is simply missing, so strict replay would fail and the relaxed path would apply.

### 4. Gesture semantics is underspecified and resolved outside the grammar

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics (MRS as by-product)
- Reference: Section 3.4, p. 1221; Section 3.5, pp. 1225-1227 (20)-(21), Figures 13-14
- Description: Alahverdzhieva et al. (2017) use Robust MRS, in which scope and also the arity of elementary predications are underspecified. Each form feature of a gesture maps to an underspecified predicate (for example hand_movement_circular) under a scopal operator [G]; a type hierarchy then expands it into alternative predications (observer vs. character viewpoint) in a later "gesture resolution process". The derivation therefore fixes only an underspecified representation; the final meaning depends on resolution in context. Lücking's alternative adds a new semantic MODE *exemplification* and enriches lexical entries with conceptual vector meanings (CVM).
- DELPH-IN stance: The cited analysis is stated in (R)MRS with RELS, HCONS and C-CONT, and with an ERG-style predicate (_mud_n_1) for the noun; no implementation in an engine is described.

### 5. Holds: a gesture stays available for attachment

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: continuity; other (adequacy of typed feature structures)
- Reference: Section 3.4, pp. 1218-1220 (example 14); Section 5, p. 1233
- Description: A post-stroke hold can last over several turns, and a gesture held through a whole utterance must pick its affiliate by accent. The outlook says this "semantic endurance" "may call for a more sophisticated algebraic treatment of speech-gesture integration than offered by typed feature structures" (Rieser 2015).
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed.

## Support for the design

- Integration schemes are ordinary binary mother-daughter rules. The basic integration scheme "implements a mother-daughter structure along the lines of a context-free grammar rule" with two daughters (Section 3.2, p. 1214); the Situated Prosodic Word Constraint and the sg-ensemble scheme likewise combine one speech daughter and one gesture daughter (pp. 1220, 1222). A derivation with gestures is still a tree of named binary rules.
- In Kühnlein et al. (2002) the pointing gesture "takes a syntactic position within the linearized inputs according to the start of the stroke phase" and combines like a modifier (HEAD|MOD) with an N-bar (Section 3.4, pp. 1218-1219, Figure 10). On that analysis a gesture can be treated as one more leaf in the sequence.
- Semantic composition in the most developed account is MRS-style: the mother's RELS and HCONS are the concatenation of the daughters' plus a constructional contribution (C-CONT), as in (15) and Figure 13 (pp. 1220, 1226). This is the same compositional regime the replay engine implements.
- The semantic contribution of the gesture is integrated "using standard unification techniques" (Section 3.5, p. 1222).
- No empty elements are used in any analysis presented.

## Theory strains

- **What licenses affiliation** (Section 1, pp. 1205-1206; Section 3.4, p. 1220 with footnote 7; Section 3.2, p. 1214). Schemes disagree on the temporal condition: overlap or following within four seconds (Johnston), strict overlap (Alahverdzhieva et al., with relaxations for direct pointing), no time constraint (Lücking 2013). The chapter itself says temporal synchrony "is too weak to be an indicator of affiliation" and that affiliation without any overlap exists. Footnote 7 leaves open whether deictic gestures attach to the accented word or to a deictic word.
- **Where gesture meaning comes from** (Section 3.1, p. 1212; Section 3.5, pp. 1221-1227). "There is nearly no semantic work" on why an iconic gesture has the meaning assigned to it; most accounts presuppose it. Competing proposals: Rieser's empirically extracted depiction typology with lambda calculus, Lücking's exemplification (reversed denotation) with vector semantics, and Alahverdzhieva et al.'s underspecified RMRS predicates. The chapter notes that the question may not belong to grammar at all.
- **Several semantic formalisms side by side** (Sections 3.4-3.5, pp. 1218-1227). Lambda terms in CONT, RESTR lists with a new MODE value, RMRS, and Type Theory with Records are all used for the same phenomena, with no common format.
- **Is grammar itself multimodal?** (Section 1, p. 1207; Section 3.4, p. 1217). Fricke (2012) argues that deictic words such as German *so* make grammar inherently multimodal; the chapter leaves the question open ("irrespective of the question whether grammar is inherently multimodal"). Related: gestures as a communicative product vs. a by-product of speaking (p. 1208).
- **Projection of gesture content** (Section 3.5, pp. 1227-1228). Schlenker's "cosupposition" vs. Hunter's (2019) derivation from dialogue coherence; "So far, there is no connection from such projections to HPSG."
- **Coverage gaps** (Section 3.6, p. 1229; Section 5, p. 1233). No HPSG account of interactive gestures; their "natural home" is said to be dialogue theory. Open problems: incomplete trajectories (gestalt completion), telling significant from negligible form features, and holds.
- **Adequacy of typed feature structures** (Section 5, p. 1233). Semantic endurance may need a richer algebra than typed feature structures (Rieser 2015).

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter does not say whether any gesture grammar runs in LKB, ACE, TRALE or another engine, or how time and prosody constraints would be passed to such an engine.
- It does not discuss how gesture annotation would relate to dependency treebanks; nothing bears on the UD bridge.
- Sign languages are explicitly set aside (p. 1211), so nothing here speaks to simultaneity in sign language grammar, which would be the harder test of the leaves-equal-tokens assumption.
- Speech-specific phenomena that a transcript parser would meet (disfluency, laughter) are only mentioned in passing (pp. 1206, 1230, 1233).
