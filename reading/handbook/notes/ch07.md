# Chapter 7: Case (Adam Przepiórkowski)

Handbook pages 245-274 (text to p. 265, then references). Notes taken strictly from the chapter; my own remarks are marked as such.

## Summary

The chapter surveys HPSG work on grammatical case, most of it from the 1990s and early 2000s. Section 2 (pp. 246-256) covers structural case assignment: the structural/lexical split in the case type hierarchy (Heinz and Matiasek 1994; Pollard 1994), the configurational Case Principle stated on phrases, and the non-configurational alternative that resolves structural case on ARG-ST (Przepiórkowski 1996b, 1999a,b; Meurers 1999a,b), including the "case tier" treatment of Finnish adjuncts. Section 3 (pp. 256-264) covers case syncretism (one form satisfying two case requirements) and "case underspecification" (coordination of unlike cases), with the type-hierarchy solutions of Levine et al. (2001), Levy and Pollard (2002), Daniels (2002) and Sag (2003), and the competing ellipsis-based accounts. Section 4 (pp. 264-265) lists other work: the Grammar Matrix case library (Drellishak 2008), Nias, Korean case spreading, and Hungarian case affixes versus postpositions.

Bearing on the parser: moderate and mostly indirect. Nothing in the chapter threatens an ERG-first design, since English case is nearly trivial. The chapter matters for the multilingual goal (German, Polish, Icelandic, Finnish, Korean) and for the replay engine's type system, because case is the place where plain unification was once thought to fail.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Structural case is resolved by a separate principle, not by the rule or the lexical entry

- Severity: medium (multilingual goal); low for the ERG
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 249-253 (Figure 1; examples 7-16)
- What the chapter says: since Pollard (1994) and Heinz and Matiasek (1994), lexical entries "generally" mark arguments only as `str` (structural) or with a specific lexical case such as `ldat` (pp. 249-250). The resolution of `str` to nominative, accusative, or genitive is done elsewhere, by implicational constraints. In the configurational version (9, 12-14, p. 251) the constraint is stated on phrases and inspects `DTRS|HEAD-DTR|...|SUBCAT`. In the non-configurational version (15-16, p. 253) it is stated on the category of a word and inspects ARG-ST with list concatenation (`1 nelist ⊕ <...> ⊕ 2`).
- Consequence for us: a derivation tree of rule names records which rules applied, but these case principles are not rules. They are global implications with complex antecedents, and they must hold in addition to the rule applications. A replay engine that only unifies rule and lexical constraints leaves `str` unresolved unless the grammar writer has compiled the principle into lexical types or rules. Constraints 15-16 also need list operations over ARG-ST that go beyond plain unification.
- DELPH-IN stance: the chapter reports that in the Grammar Matrix case library (Drellishak 2008) case systems "are described – via constraints on specific lexical types – by specifying case values of elements on ARG-ST" (p. 264). That is, case is compiled into lexical types. No other platform is discussed.

### 2. The case of a structural argument depends on the highest ARG-ST it reaches, not on where it is realized

- Severity: medium (multilingual goal); low for the ERG
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 247-250 (Icelandic 3-6), pp. 253-254 (German 17)
- What the chapter says: with raising to object, a structural argument appears on several SUBCAT/ARG-ST lists and "should be assigned specific morphological case according to its position on just one of them – the highest one" (pp. 250-251). Quirky (lexical) case, by contrast, is fixed by the lowest verb and "the higher raising verbs must honour this assignment" (p. 247). In (17), p. 253, the fronted constituent `[ein/einen Außenseiter gewinnen]` has "the same configurational realisation in both examples" yet the subject is nominative under `wird` and accusative under `läßt`.
- Consequence for us: the case value of a token cannot be read off its local tree configuration. If case-marked forms are distinguished in the tagger's label (through lexical type or inflectional rule), the correct label depends on a verb that may be arbitrarily far up a raising chain, and identical local subtrees carry different labels. The tagger sees the whole sentence, so this is learnable, but replay must propagate the case requirement down through argument sharing for the check to mean anything.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Non-configurational case assignment changes the geometry of ARG-ST

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (feature geometry of the engine and of converted grammars)
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 252-254
- What the chapter says: for constraints 15-16 to work, "values of ARG-ST must be lists of slightly more complex objects than synsem" (p. 253): each element has an `ARG` value and a boolean `REALIZED` (later `RAISED`, p. 254), and "additional principles must make sure that values of REALIZED are instantiated properly".
- Consequence for us: grammars from this strand of the theory do not share the ARG-ST geometry of other HPSG grammars. Any cross-grammar tooling (inspection, the UD bridge reading arguments off ARG-ST) cannot assume ARG-ST is a list of synsems.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Adjuncts as complements: some adjuncts sit on ARG-ST/DEPS and take part in case assignment

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 2, pp. 254-256 (Finnish 18-19)
- What the chapter says: in Finnish, adverbials of measure, duration, and frequency "behave just like objects with respect to case assignment" (p. 255). The HPSG formalization relies on the "adjuncts as complements" approach of Bouma et al. (2001), "upon which at least some adjuncts are added to ARG-ST (perhaps renamed to DEPS)" (p. 255), ordered by obliqueness: SUBJ > OBJ > MEASURE > DURATION > FREQUENCY. The first structural element gets nominative, whether it is subject, object, or adjunct; later ones get accusative.
- Consequence for us: under this analysis an adverbial is selected by the verb and enters the tree as a complement, so the derivation no longer distinguishes arguments from these adjuncts by rule name. A converter that maps head-complement daughters to core argument relations and head-adjunct daughters to modifier relations would mislabel them. Case alone does not identify the grammatical function either (a nominative NP may be subject, object, or adjunct).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Case syncretism: one token must satisfy two different case requirements

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (also UD-bridge)
- Reference: Section 3, pp. 256-258 (examples 20-25, Figure 2)
- What the chapter says: in coordination, free relatives, and parasitic gaps, a syncretic form satisfies two case requirements at once (Polish `kogo` ACC/GEN, German `Frauen`, English `who`). "Such examples were at one point considered problematic not only for HPSG, but for unification-based theories in general (Ingria 1990)" because "the attempt to unify accusative and dative should fail" (pp. 257-258). The fix (Levine et al. 2001) enriches the hierarchy so that `nom` and `acc` have a common subtype `p-nom-acc`; nominals carry "pure" types (`p-nom`, `p-acc`, `p-nom-acc`), heads require the non-pure ones.
- Consequence for us: with a flat case hierarchy, replay fails on grammatical sentences, which would push them to the relaxed path for the wrong reason. With the enriched hierarchy, plain unification works, so the engine needs no new machinery, only a grammar that encodes syncretism in its types. The lexical type the tagger assigns to a syncretic form should then be the neutral one, not a disambiguated one. For the UD bridge, such a token has two case values at once in the HPSG analysis; a conversion that expects one case per token has to choose. (My remark: the chapter does not mention UD.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter says hierarchies of the Figure 2 kind "are still needed in contemporary HPSG, regardless of the analysis of coordination" (p. 263), citing Alotaibi and Borsley (2013), with a footnote that Crysmann (2017) reanalyzes that case without one (p. 264, fn. 14).

### 6. Coordination of unlike cases needs a join computation and strains the type hierarchy

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 3, pp. 259-263 (examples 26-27, Figures 3-6)
- What the chapter says: a head with a disjunctive case requirement can take a coordination of, for example, a genitive and an accusative NP (26). The coordinate phrase needs a case like `acc+gen` ("union", versus the "intersection" of syncretism, p. 259). In Daniels (2002), "a new principle is needed to determine the case of a coordinate structure": take the non-pure versions of the conjuncts' cases, "find their (lowest) common supertype", and assign the corresponding pure type (p. 261). Because objects are sort-resolved in standard HPSG, extra maximal types (`p-nom+acc`) must be added for every non-maximal type; the alternative (Sag 2003) is "a simple change to the framework's foundational assumptions" that lets objects bear non-maximal types (p. 260). The hierarchy "grows double exponentially with the number of grammatical cases": three cases give 18 visualized nodes standing for 35 types, and four (German) is "next to impossible to visualise" (pp. 262-263). It gets worse if case is combined with person, gender, and number (Daniels 2002; Crysmann 2005).
- Consequence for us: the mother's case in a coordination rule is a least upper bound of the daughters' values followed by a type mapping. Unification computes greatest lower bounds, so this step is outside what a pure unification engine does when it applies a named rule; it needs either a relational constraint or precompiled rule variants. The double-exponential hierarchy is a practical cost for type-table size in any engine if a grammar adopts this analysis for a language with many cases.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter notes that "after the early 2000s, such complex case hierarchies do not appear in HPSG work" (p. 263).

### 7. The competing ellipsis accounts of coordination are stated over word order domains

- Severity: medium (high for any grammar that adopts them; none for the ERG as far as this chapter says)
- Assumption affected: continuity (also leaves-equal-tokens)
- Reference: Section 3, p. 263 and fn. 12
- What the chapter says: the unlike-case coordination data may be "special cases" of unlike category coordination handled by ellipsis. "Such ellipsis accounts are usually formulated within the linearisation approach of Reape (1992; 1994) and Kathol (1995)" (Crysmann 2008; Beavers and Sag 2004; Chaves 2006, 2008). These have "come under attack" (Levine 2011; Kubota and Levine 2015) and been defended (Yatabe 2012, 2016; Yatabe and Tam 2021). "It is difficult to predict at the moment" which side prevails (p. 263).
- Consequence for us: if the theory settles on ellipsis in order domains, the analysis of these coordinations is not a concatenative tree whose leaves are exactly the tokens: conjuncts are larger than what is pronounced. The alternative that keeps trees concatenative is the complex hierarchy of caveat 6. Either way one of our assumptions pays. The chapter gives no details; chapters 16 (coordination) and 19 (ellipsis) are the places to check.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Arguments that are not tree constituents: gaps and pronominal affixes

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 2, p. 252
- What the chapter says: configurational case principles "are restricted to locally realised arguments" and are not necessarily compatible with the traceless extraction analyses "dominant since Pollard & Sag (1994: Chapter 9)" or with cliticization analyses "in which the clitic is realised as an affix rather than as a tree-configurational constituent" (Miller and Sag 1997 on French; Monachesi 1999 on Italian). An argument can be realized "tree-configurationally, or as a gap to be filled higher on, or as an affix".
- Consequence for us: arguments on ARG-ST do not map one-to-one onto daughters in the derivation. For affixal clitics, the argument lives inside the verb's token, so a dependency-per-argument conversion has no separate leaf to attach. (My remark: the chapter does not discuss tokenization or UD.) The traceless half of this passage supports the design; see below.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Cross-linguistic mapping between case, grammatical function, and valence is not uniform

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 4, pp. 264-265
- What the chapter says: (a) Nias is reanalyzed by Crysmann (2009) as nominative-accusative "with the sole argument of intransitive verbs mapped to the grammatical function of object, rather than subject" (p. 264). (b) In Ryu's (2013) analysis of Korean case spreading, dependents of nominal arguments are raised to the verb's valence lists, "resulting in multiple elements within the SUBJ list of a single verb" (pp. 264-265). (c) Thuilier (2011) argues that Hungarian case affixes and postpositions are distinct and reclassifies essive `ként` as a postposition (p. 265). (d) The Grammar Matrix covers split ergativity, Austronesian focus-case interaction, and direct-inverse systems (p. 264).
- Consequence for us: language-independent conversion rules of the form "first valence element = subject" or "case X = function Y" will not hold across grammars. In (b) a possessor-like dependent of a noun is syntactically a subject of the verb, so the HPSG head differs from the head a dependency treebank would likely choose. (c) bears on where the token boundary and the case feature are placed.
- DELPH-IN stance: (d) is Grammar Matrix work and uses lexical-type constraints on ARG-ST (p. 264). (a) to (c) are presented as theoretical analyses; the chapter does not say whether they are implemented.

## Support for the design

- English case is lexical in the founding text: "Nominative case assignment takes place directly within the lexical entry of the finite verb" (Pollard and Sag 1994: 30, quoted p. 246). Nothing beyond lexical types is needed for the ERG.
- The non-configurational approach makes case "a local phenomenon, an intimate relation between a head and its dependents" (p. 254), resolved at the word level where "head information and argument structure information – but not constituent structure – are available" (p. 252). This fits a design in which the tagger's lexical type carries the decisive information.
- The Grammar Matrix, the DELPH-IN multilingual starter kit, already encodes a wide typological range of case systems purely as "constraints on specific lexical types" on ARG-ST (Drellishak 2008; p. 264). This is the strongest support in the chapter for the multilingual aim: case systems of many kinds have been reduced to lexical types plus unification.
- The dominant extraction analyses "do not assume traces" (p. 252), in line with the no-empty-elements assumption.
- Syncretism, once claimed to show "the limits of unification" (Ingria 1990), is handled by an ordinary type hierarchy with common subtypes (Figure 2, p. 258). Daniels's (2002) version is "fully integrated" in the usual type hierarchy (p. 260). A standard typed-feature-structure engine suffices for the neutrality half of the problem.
- The fronted-constituent analysis of (17) assumes that the fronted material is a single constituent (p. 253), that is, an ordinary continuous subtree; the chapter notes this is "not completely uncontroversial" (fn. 6).

## Theory strains

- Configurational versus non-configurational case assignment (Section 2, pp. 251-254). The phrase-level Case Principle (Heinz and Matiasek 1994; Pollard 1994; also Yoo 1993, Bratt 1996, Grover 1995, Przepiórkowski 1996a) is criticized as conceptually wrong for a head-dependent relation and as incompatible with traceless extraction and affixal clitics. Yet Ryu (2013) returns to configurational rules for Korean, and the chapter concedes that multiple valency subjects are a "challenge for the non-configurational case assignment" (Section 4, p. 265).
- REALIZED versus RAISED (Section 2, pp. 253-254). Przepiórkowski's first version ties case to local realization; Meurers (1999a,b) shows with German fronting data that this "does not always hold" and that raising status is what matters. The repair adds a diacritic boolean feature to every ARG-ST element plus principles to instantiate it, with "further improvements" in Müller (2013: Section 17.4). The machinery is stipulative: the feature exists only to tell the case principle where to apply.
- How to encode structural versus lexical case (Section 2, p. 249). Sag et al. (1992) use two attributes, CASE and DCASE; later work uses `str` and `lex` subtypes. The hierarchy of Figure 1 cross-classifies morphological and syntactic case and must stipulate which combinations exist (no structural dative, no lexical nominative in German).
- Pollard and Sag (1994) had no theory of case (Section 2, p. 246); a footnote concedes that richer case systems need a structural/inherent distinction. The simplest principle (first `str` = nominative, the rest accusative) fails under raising to object in any language that has it (pp. 250-251).
- Status of the fronted constituent in (17) (p. 253, fn. 6): Kiss (1994) gives apparent counterexamples to single constituency; Müller (2003, 2005, 2021) defends it.
- Syncretism and coordination of unlikes: three competing treatments (Section 3, pp. 258-263). (i) Enriched type hierarchies with "pure" types (Levine et al. 2001; Daniels 2002) or lattices outside the type hierarchy (Levy 2001; Levy and Pollard 2002); the two are "isomorphic". (ii) Abandoning sort resolution so objects can bear non-maximal types (Sag 2003), a change to "foundational assumptions". (iii) Ellipsis in order domains (Beavers and Sag 2004; Crysmann 2008; Chaves 2006, 2008) or distribution of the head's requirements to conjuncts (Yatabe 2004, fn. 12). The hierarchies are "often-perceived – both within and outside of HPSG" as leading to "very complex type hierarchies for case and rather inelegant constraints" (pp. 261-262; Sag 2003: 272; Dalrymple et al. 2009), with double-exponential growth.
- Ellipsis-based coordination is itself disputed (Section 3, p. 263): attacked by Levine (2011) and Kubota and Levine (2015), defended by Yatabe (2012, 2016) and Yatabe and Tam (2021). The chapter declines to predict the outcome.
- What category a coordination of unlike categories bears is unresolved (p. 263): "it is not clear what categories should be borne by the coordinate structure as a whole", with a pointer to "the inconclusive fn. 10 in Sag 2003: 277".
- Whether syncretism hierarchies are needed at all outside coordination (pp. 263-264, fn. 14): Alotaibi and Borsley (2013) use one for Modern Standard Arabic; Crysmann (2017) reanalyzes without it.
- Gaps in coverage acknowledged by the chapter: differential case marking has been treated in LFG "but apparently not (so far) in HPSG" (p. 254); most HPSG case work concerns European languages (p. 254); HPSG is "not widely known for its approach to grammatical case" and is mentioned only in passing in the standard case handbooks (Section 1, p. 245).
- Competition with other frameworks: structural/inherent case is imported from GB (p. 246); the union/intersection duality comes from Categorial Grammar (Bayer 1996; p. 259); LFG handles unlike coordination by distributivity (fn. 12, p. 263). In the other direction, the chapter claims HPSG can formalize the "case tier" generalization (Maling 1993) more readily than LFG, where adjuncts are an unordered set (p. 255).
- Unusual argument mappings adopted to save a generalization (Section 4, p. 264): Nias intransitive sole arguments as objects (Crysmann 2009); Korean multiple elements on SUBJ (Ryu 2013), with 16 semantic relations licensing nominative spreading and 10 licensing accusative spreading.

## Not covered here

All items in this section are my own remarks, not claims of the chapter.

- The chapter does not say how the ERG or any other large DELPH-IN grammar (for example a German or Japanese one) actually treats case, structural case, or syncretism. Only the Grammar Matrix is mentioned. Whether implemented grammars use `str`/`lex` types, a case principle, or fully lexical assignment has to be checked in the grammars.
- TRALE, Enju, LKB, and ACE are not mentioned. The non-configurational principles (15-16) use list concatenation and complex antecedents; the chapter does not say on which platform, if any, they have been implemented.
- Nothing on semantics or MRS: the chapter does not discuss whether case contributes to semantic composition, apart from noting semantic conditions on Polish partitive genitive (p. 259) and Korean case spreading (p. 264).
- Nothing on UD or dependency conversion; all UD-bridge consequences above are my inferences from the analyses described.
- Case stacking and case attraction (Malouf 2000) are mentioned in one sentence (p. 254) with no analysis. Case stacking could matter for the label inventory (several case values on one token), but the chapter gives no basis for judging.
- Passive and nominalization are used as motivation (pp. 246-247), but the lexical rules that would manipulate case are not shown; see the chapters on argument structure and on the lexicon.
- Agreement in case within the NP (determiner, adjective, noun) is not covered.
