# Chapter 26: Grammar in dialogue (Andy Lücking, Jonathan Ginzburg and Robin Cooper)

Handbook pages 1155-1199 (text 1155-1186, references 1186-1199). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter surveys how HPSG and closely related frameworks treat language use in face-to-face interaction. Section 2 reviews the CONTEXT attribute of Pollard and Sag (1994) and its extensions: contextual indices and background conditions, information structure (Engdahl and Vallduví 1996), mutual beliefs (Green 1996), and Ginzburg's argument that static context must be replaced by an update semantics with FACTS, QUD, and LATEST-MOVE. Section 3 argues from clarification requests (reprise fragments) that both unification-based and model-theoretic constraint-based grammars lose the meanings of sub-constituents, and from the status of HPSG's semantic objects that a type-theoretic foundation (TTR, Type Theory with Records) is preferable. Section 4 sketches HPSG-TTR signs inside the dialogue gameboard of the theory KoS, with conversational rules and lexical entries for an interjection, "hello", and "yes"; the appendix gives a small HPSG-TTR fragment.

The chapter has little direct bearing on parsing. It does not mention DELPH-IN grammars, the ERG, LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, derivation trees, parsing algorithms, or dependency grammar. Its one contact with DELPH-IN practice is a paragraph on (Robust) Minimal Recursion Semantics (Section 3.2, p. 1171). Every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is therefore "not discussed" unless stated. The points below concern the semantics delivered by replay and the kinds of input (spoken, fragmentary) the parser would meet; none of them touches continuity, the tree encoding, or the absence of empty elements directly.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. The final feature structure does not keep the content of sub-constituents (reprise fragments)

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: semantics (MRS as a by-product of replay)
- Reference: Section 3.1, pp. 1167-1169 (examples 9 and 10)
- What the chapter says: A reprise fragment such as "Finagle?" after "Did Bo finagle a raise?" queries exactly the standard content of the reprised expression (the strong Reprise Content Hypothesis of Purver and Ginzburg 2004: 288), at any depth of syntactic embedding. In unification-based semantics, structure sharing identifies slots of the head with the contributions of its arguments and modifiers, so once the VP content is composed "one cannot recover the V content from the VP content"; unification "appears to be too strong a mechanism to provide contents at all levels". The same argument is applied to the model-theoretic reading of Pollard and Sag (1994): once constraints describing the total objects for "finagle a raise" are accumulated, the larger set of objects for "finagle" alone is no longer available. The chapter concludes that a mechanism is needed that tracks the semantic contribution of each constituent, and states: "We do not know of any such attempts within constraint-based grammars".
- Why it matters: The plan treats the semantics as the by-product read off the feature structure at the end of replay. According to the chapter, that final structure does not contain the uninstantiated content of each sub-constituent. If per-constituent contents are ever needed (clarification, alignment of semantic pieces to tokens), they have to be recorded at each replay step. (That replay could record them is my inference; the chapter does not discuss implementations.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 2. MRS is not an autonomous semantic component

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 3.2, p. 1171
- What the chapter says: (R)MRS (Copestake, Flickinger, Pollard and Sag 2005; Copestake 2007) is "HPSG's most popular underspecification mechanism". Its formulas may have unfilled argument slots and can be assembled in various ways, but "resolving such underspecified representations is not part of the grammar formalism, so (R)MRS representations do not provide an autonomous semantic component for HPSG". The chapter contrasts it with Lexical Resource Semantics (Richter and Sailer 2004), which does address the question of how feature-structure types correspond to semantic types.
- Why it matters: The "strict" output of the parser is an underspecified representation. It is a description that still has to be resolved and interpreted outside the grammar; the chapter does not count it as a semantics with a model-theoretic interpretation of its own.
- DELPH-IN stance: This is the only passage that touches DELPH-IN practice. It names (R)MRS as the most popular mechanism and says its resolution lies outside the grammar formalism. No system is named.

### 3. Non-sentential utterances: content depends on the dialogue state, not on the derivation

- Severity: medium for dialogue or spoken data, low for edited text
- Assumption affected: semantics, deterministic-replay (a derivation as a complete recipe for the semantics)
- Reference: Section 1, p. 1157; Section 2.4, pp. 1165-1166 (example 8); Section 4, pp. 1176-1177 (entry 22), p. 1179, p. 1181 (entries 25 and 26)
- What the chapter says: Dialogue contains non-sentential utterances (short answers, reprise fragments, acknowledgments, greetings, feedback sounds). The same word plays different roles in different contexts: "Cambridge" is an answer in one turn and an accept move in the next, and the repetition is not redundant. Subsentential utterances "project to Austinian propositions by resolving elliptical expressions in context", using the content of the maximal element of QUD (path QUD.FIRST.CONT; Ginzburg 2012: 68). The entry for "yes" has as its content the application of the maximal polar question in QUD to an empty record. "Hello" requires empty MOVES and QUD. The feedback sound "ehh hehh" has an entry whose content refers to the PENDING utterance.
- Why it matters: For such utterances the derivation tree alone does not determine the content; the content is a function of the sign and the dialogue gameboard. A sentence-level parser can deliver only the context-independent part, with the contextual parameters (DGB-PARAMS) left open. The root of the derivation is also not a clause. (The consequence for root conditions and for the UD bridge is my inference; the chapter does not discuss either.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Prosody and information structure: constituency that is not syntactic, and meaning differences with identical strings

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics, tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 2.2, pp. 1161-1162 (examples 4 and 5)
- What the chapter says: Focus, marked by sentence accent in English, can change truth conditions while the string stays the same ("John only showed Mary the PICTURES" versus "John only showed MARY the pictures"). In Engdahl and Vallduví (1996) the values FOCUS, LINK, and TAIL under CONTEXT|INFO-STR are instantiated by structure sharing, driven by accent. Prosodic constituency "is not isomorphic to syntactic constituency", so prosodic structures cannot be built in parallel to syntactic trees. Klein (2000) uses metrical trees independent of syntactic trees and keeps composition syntax-driven. Haji-Abdolhosseini (2003) gives up syntax-driven composition: syntax, phonology, and information structure are parallel features of a common list of domain objects.
- Why it matters: With text input, accent is absent, so the focus-dependent part of the meaning cannot be determined by the tagger or by replay and must stay open. An architecture of the Haji-Abdolhosseini kind uses domain objects, so a grammar written that way would not be a plain concatenative tree. This concerns only grammars that model prosody.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. CONTEXT is a separate part of the sign beside CONTENT

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Sections 2.1-2.3, pp. 1158-1164 (structures 2, 3, 5, 6)
- What the chapter says: In Pollard and Sag (1994) indexicals get their value by identifying the semantic index with a contextual index (C-INDS: speaker, addressee, time, location), and presuppositions and conventional implicatures sit in BACKGROUND (for example, that the referent of "she" is female). Green (1996) adds mutual-belief conditions for common nouns and proper names. The chapter notes that only the speaker is straightforwardly given by the context, that the notion of speaker itself needs extending (footnote 2, p. 1160), and that a fuller model of speech acts and meaning transfer is still needed (Kathol et al. 2011: 94).
- Why it matters: If a grammar follows this architecture, the output of replay has two parts, and a semantics extracted from CONTENT alone omits the contextual part. The chapter does not relate CONTEXT to MRS.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Disfluencies, laughter, gesture, overlap

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens, other (input coverage)
- Reference: Section 1, pp. 1156-1157 (example 1); Section 5, p. 1182
- What the chapter says: Face-to-face dialogue contains disfluencies, prolongations, laughter, coughs, pauses, overlapping turns, and pointing gestures. These are usually abstracted away from the grammar ("written language bias", Linell 2005), and the chapter reports the view that they are serious challenges to current linguistic theory. Integration of non-verbal means is listed as an open problem.
- Why it matters: Transcribed speech (including spoken UD treebanks) contains tokens of these kinds. The chapter offers a lexical entry for one feedback sound (entry 22, p. 1177) and no analysis of disfluent or interrupted structures, so for such input the relaxed path would be the normal case. (The link to spoken UD treebanks is my remark.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Incremental processing

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (processing order)
- Reference: Section 5, p. 1182
- What the chapter says: People process word by word or on smaller units, which is central to turn taking. One research direction is incremental grammar and dialogue gameboard models (Hough et al. 2015; Demberg et al. 2013; Poesio and Rieser 2011).
- Why it matters: The planned parser tags a complete sentence and replays a complete tree. This matters only if the parser is used in an interactive dialogue system.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. HPSG-TTR composes meaning by predication and merge, not by unification

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (the engine applies rules by unification)
- Reference: Section 3.1, p. 1169; Section 4, p. 1175; Appendix, pp. 1183-1185 (27, 36, 37)
- What the chapter says: The authors state that HPSG-TTR "leave[s] the formal background of standard HPSG" although the representations "look deceptively similar to attribute-value matrices". TTR has a counterpart to unification, the merge of record types. Semantic composition "rests on predication rather than unification", so there is no analog of the semantic compositionality principle of Sag et al. (2003). There is also a coercion operation that moves parts of DGB-PARAMS to Q-PARAMS for non-referential uses of NPs (31, p. 1184).
- Why it matters: A grammar written in HPSG-TTR could not be replayed by a typed-feature-structure unifier alone. This is relevant only if such grammars become a target.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- Concatenation of daughters: the Phon Principle of the fragment (36, p. 1185) makes the mother's PHON the list of the daughters' PHON values. The fragment has no word order domains.
- Lexical rule chains: "Words ... are usually the result of lexical rules, whose input are lexemes" (Appendix, p. 1184). This matches a tag that encodes a lexical type plus a chain of lexical rules.
- Interjections and particles are ordinary lexical signs with a HEAD value (interjection, partcl) and a content (entries 22, 25, 26; pp. 1177, 1181), so they are leaves like any other token.
- Fractal architecture: phonological, syntactic, and semantic information is present in every sign from words to clauses (Section 2.2, p. 1161; Section 3.1, p. 1167; Appendix, p. 1183). Each node of a replayed derivation therefore carries its own semantics at the time it is built. The chapter calls this "crucial" for clarification interaction; it is also what makes per-node recording (caveat 1) possible. (The link to replay is my inference.)
- MRS is described as HPSG's most popular underspecification mechanism (Section 3.2, p. 1171).
- One qualification: a headed phrase in the fragment has a head daughter and a list of non-head daughters (34, p. 1185), so the fragment does not restrict rules to unary and binary. The chapter says nothing more on arity.

## Theory strains

- Unification-based versus model-theoretic HPSG. Pollard and Sag (1987) treat language as partial information and need unification; Pollard and Sag (1994) and its derivatives describe total objects, are model-theoretic, and rest on RSRL (Richter 2004a; King 1999), with no need for unification. Within the model-theoretic camp, models are read as classes of linguistic tokens (King 1999) or as types construed as equivalence classes of utterance tokens (Pollard 1999). The chapter speaks of "formal and conceptual controversies and developments that take place in HPSG anyway". Section 3.1, pp. 1168-1169; Section 3.2, p. 1170.
- Reprise content versus both formalisms. Neither unification nor constraint accumulation keeps the content of sub-constituents available; the authors know of no attempt to fix this within constraint-based grammars. Section 3.1, pp. 1167-1169.
- Status of HPSG's semantic objects. The (parameterized) states of affairs of Pollard and Sag (1994) have no direct model-theoretic interpretation and must be translated into situation-theoretic formulas (Ginzburg and Sag 2000: Section 3.6). Penn (2000: 63) concludes that feature structures here encode data structures for formal devices that represent knowledge. Section 3.2, pp. 1169-1170.
- Three competing answers for semantics: logical forms in the feature logic (Nerbonne 1992; Richter and Sailer 1999; Lexical Resource Semantics, Richter and Sailer 2004); (R)MRS, which the chapter says does not address the problem; and TTR (Cooper 2008; Ginzburg 2012), which the authors favor as "a strong competitor". Section 3.2, pp. 1170-1171; Section 1, p. 1158.
- Generalized quantifiers versus clarification data. Quantified NPs denote sets of sets on the standard analysis, but answers to clarification requests give individuals or sets of individuals; the alternative uses witness sets (Purver and Ginzburg 2004; Ginzburg and Purver 2012; Lücking and Ginzburg 2018). Section 3.2, pp. 1171-1172.
- Possible-worlds propositions versus structured meanings. Sets of possible worlds are too coarse (slingshot argument); TTR types are intensional. Within TTR there are two notions of proposition (propositions as types, Cooper 2005a; Austinian propositions, Ginzburg 2011), and the authors choose the second. Section 3.2, p. 1172; Section 4, p. 1176.
- Static CONTEXT is insufficient. The CONTEXT representations of Pollard and Sag (1994), Engdahl and Vallduví (1996), and Green (1996) lack dialogue context and the dynamics of common ground; the given/new view of information structure cannot explain non-redundant repetitions. The authors invert the strategy: start from an interactive semantic framework and embed an HPSG variant in it. Section 2.3, p. 1164; Section 2.4, pp. 1165-1166.
- Green's (1996) mutual-belief analysis: the standard use of "dog" restricted to the family Canis is "too permissive", and the experiencer group should vary over communities and genres (footnote 4, p. 1163). A fuller model of speech acts and meaning transfer is still needed (Kathol et al. 2011: 94). Section 2.3, pp. 1163-1164.
- Prosody and syntax: syntax-driven composition with independent metrical trees (Klein 2000) versus parallel features on a list of domain objects (Haji-Abdolhosseini 2003). Section 2.2, p. 1162.
- Competence versus performance and the written language bias. Results in dialogue semantics are said to be inconsistent with the traditional competence/performance distinction (Ginzburg and Poesio 2016; Kempson et al. 2016); knowing how to use feedback signals is claimed to be part of linguistic competence. Section 1, pp. 1157-1158; Section 4, p. 1177.
- Competing dialogue frameworks: KoS, PTT, and SDRT. Section 5, p. 1182.

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims of the chapter.

- The chapter does not say how the ERG or other DELPH-IN grammars treat fragments, interjections, or greetings (root conditions, fragment rules), or whether they have any counterpart of CONTEXT.
- It does not discuss how MRS composition relates to the reprise-content argument, for example whether the elementary predications contributed by each lexical item, which stay identifiable in an MRS, answer the demand for trackable constituent contents.
- It does not discuss information structure in MRS (ICONS) or any implemented treatment of focus.
- It does not discuss disfluency structure (reparandum, repair) or how spoken-language treebanks annotate it, which would matter for the UD bridge on spoken data.
- It gives no formal comparison of TTR merge with typed-feature-structure unification, so it is open whether an HPSG-TTR grammar could be compiled to something a unification engine can replay.
