# Chapter 30: HPSG and Lexical Functional Grammar (Stephen Wechsler and Ash Asudeh)

Handbook pages 1395-1446 (text 1395-1436, references from 1436). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter compares HPSG with LFG topic by topic: phrase structure and headedness, grammatical functions and valence, head mobility, agreement and case, affixal pronouns, lexical mapping, long distance dependencies, nonconfigurationality, and raising and control. Its thesis is that the two frameworks can express roughly the same analyses, and that their typical differences come from their origins: HPSG from context-free grammar and the study of locality, LFG from the idea that different surface means (position, case, agreement) express the same grammatical functions. Section 12 is an introduction to Glue Semantics, the semantic theory used with LFG; the authors explicitly decline to compare it with MRS. The chapter is written at the level of theory. It names the Grammar Matrix, CoreGram and ParGram projects once (Section 12.1, p. 1427) and MRS twice, and says nothing about the ERG, LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, derivation trees, or parsing algorithms. Every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is therefore "not discussed" unless stated.

The bearing on the parser design is moderate. The most relevant passages are those where HPSG analyses use devices outside the design assumptions (empty verbal heads, order domains, ternary branching), and the passage on the status of "subject" in HPSG, which matters for the UD bridge.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Order domains for nonconfigurational languages

- Severity: high for the multilingual goal, low for English
- Assumption affected: continuity
- Reference: Section 10, pp. 1420-1423 (examples 29-33; the HPSG comparison is on p. 1423)
- What the chapter says: Warlpiri allows any order of words in the clause, subject only to the auxiliary being in second position; a noun and its modifier can be separated ("child ... small", example 29b, c). One of the two HPSG approaches the chapter describes is the order domain approach (Donohue and Sag 1999): the words are combined into constituents like those of a configurational language (the words for "two small children" form an NP in every permutation), and a DOM list holds the phonological forms and may be ordered freely relative to the order of the daughters, recursively up to the clause. The DOM order determines pronunciation.
- Why it matters: Under this analysis the derivation tree is not a continuous tree over the token string, so a tag sequence that decodes to a concatenative tree cannot express it. (This consequence is my inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed as such. The chapter presents Bender (2008) as the alternative HPSG approach (see caveat 2), without saying that it comes from an implemented grammar.

### 2. Non-cancellation: a second, non-tree level of structure inside the sign

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: continuity, UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 10, pp. 1423-1424; also Section 1, p. 1396, and Section 11, p. 1425
- What the chapter says: In the non-cancellation approach (Bender 2008), a list-valued feature called COMPS is not reduced when a complement is realized, and it is shared between a phrase and its head daughter, so it is visible on non-terminal nodes. Special phrasal types relate a non-head daughter to a position on that list (for example head-2nd-comp-phrase, where the second item is the object). The item stays available, so another word elsewhere in the clause can combine with the head and modify the object through a different phrasal type. The chapter says this "effectively introduces into HPSG correlates of c-structure and f-structure" and that the phrasal types are equivalent to LFG annotated phrase structure rules. Mueller (2008) uses non-cancellation for depictive predicates in English and German.
- Why it matters: This approach keeps the tree continuous, at the cost of a larger rule inventory indexed by argument position, and of discontinuous noun phrases that are not constituents in the tree. For the tree encoding, the rule label must then encode which argument position a daughter fills. For the UD bridge, the dependent of a split NP attaches in the tree to the verbal projection while its dependency head is the noun. (Both consequences are my inference.) The same mechanism can violate the restriction of raising and control to subjects (Section 11, p. 1425).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. Bender (2008) is cited only as a version of HPSG.

### 3. Empty elements: head movement with an empty verb, and gaps

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 1, p. 1396; Section 5, pp. 1406-1407; Section 9, p. 1418
- What the chapter says: Both theories allow phonologically empty nodes, "although researchers in both theories tend to avoid them unless they are well-motivated" (Sag and Fodor 1995 is cited). For head mobility (verb-initial and verb-second clauses), HPSG has two approaches. The constructional one licenses a distinct structure. The other, due to Borsley (1989), posits a phonologically empty element in the canonical verb position that is structure-shared with the fronted verb, treating head position like a non-local dependency. For long distance dependencies, both frameworks have accounts with gaps (Pollard and Sag 1994: Chapter 4) and without gaps (Sag et al. 2003: Chapter 14; Bouma et al. 2001). Gaps have been motivated by the "(controversial)" claim that the linear position of an empty category matters for weak crossover and other binding phenomena.
- Why it matters: The trace-based analyses conflict with the no-empty-elements assumption. The chapter confirms that a traceless alternative exists for both phenomena, so the assumption is tenable, but for a grammar written with a verb trace (the chapter points to German verb placement and to Mueller 2021, Chapter 10) a derivation would contain a leaf with no token.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Flat (ternary) structure for subject-auxiliary inversion

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules
- Reference: Section 5, p. 1407
- What the chapter says: In the constructional approach to "Is she mad?", "assuming ternary branching", the subject and the predicate are sisters of the fronted auxiliary, so "she mad" is not a constituent. The fronting can be seen as a consequence of the flattening, since English is head-initial. "This analysis is common in HPSG" (Sag et al. 2020 is cited).
- Why it matters: A common theoretical analysis of English inversion uses a rule with three daughters, which the design excludes. The chapter does not say whether a binary version is available or what implemented grammars do. If the derivations to be encoded are binary, the trees will differ from the ones in this literature in the constituency of inverted clauses.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. HPSG has no uniform formal notion of "subject"

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 4, pp. 1404-1405; Section 3, p. 1403
- What the chapter says: LFG identifies the subject in every language with the attribute SUBJ. HPSG identifies it, "if at all", as a particular element of ARG-ST, and which element can vary by language. An English subject is usually the first element. A German subject must be defined with reference to CASE (Reis 1982), because in subjectless German sentences with arguments the first ARG-ST element is a non-subject (for example a dative). The HPSG valence feature SUBJ (or SPR) is closer to Chomsky's definition of subject as an NP in a structural position than to a grammatical function. Some HPSG work adds grammatical function features (qVAL in Kropp Dakubu et al. 2007; ERG in Pollard 1994).
- Why it matters: The UD bridge must assign relations such as nsubj and obj. The chapter says that a rule name or valence slot does not give the grammatical function in a language-independent way, so the mapping from derivations to UD relations has to be defined per grammar, and for German-type languages it needs case information from the feature structure and cannot be read off the tree alone. (The UD consequence is my inference; the chapter does not mention UD in this section.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Affixal pronouns versus agreement markers

- Severity: low for English, medium for the multilingual goal
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens, UD-bridge, semantics
- Reference: Section 7, pp. 1413-1415 (examples 20-21)
- What the chapter says: In Chichewa (Bresnan and Mchombo 1987), the object marker on the verb is an incorporated pronoun, so a co-occurring NP ("the hunters") is a postposed topic linked anaphorically, not the object. The subject marker is ambiguous: an agreement marker when a subject NP is present, a pronoun when none is. Telling the two apart "can be a subtle and controversial matter". In HPSG the analysis can be stated by giving the pronominal affix pronominal semantics in CONTENT, with an exclusive disjunction between the pronominal inflection and a phrase filling the same relation.
- Why it matters: An argument and its semantics can come from an affix inside a token, so one token contributes more than one predication and an overt NP that looks like an object is not one. The tagger must choose between the two lexical variants of the subject marker depending on whether a subject NP is present elsewhere in the sentence, and the UD tree must attach the NP as a dislocated topic in one case and as an argument in the other. (These consequences are my inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. Requirements that some element supply a feature (output filters)

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 6, pp. 1410-1413 (examples 14-19); Section 1, p. 1396, footnote 1
- What the chapter says: LFG constraining equations check the finished f-structure for the presence of a feature that some other element must have supplied. This mechanism is "absent from HPSG and indeed ruled out by current foundational assumptions of HPSG theory" (Richter 2021); the early ANY value (Shieber 1986) has been removed. Two cases are given. English nominative pronouns need a finite verb ("She did!" versus "*She!", "Her!"). In Serbo-Croatian (Wechsler and Zlatic 2003), dative or instrumental case assigned by a verb or noun must be morphologically realized by at least one element in the NP, so NPs made only of undeclinable items are excluded from exactly those positions. The chapter says this is easy with an output filter "but rather difficult otherwise". Footnote 1 adds that neither framework "actually employs a unification operation"; unification only produces structures resembling theirs.
- Why it matters: A replay engine that only unifies cannot state "this feature must have been contributed by someone". A grammar for Serbo-Croatian-type facts would need another device, and the chapter offers no HPSG analysis. If no device is used, replay overgenerates on such sentences, which is harmless for parsing grammatical text. (My inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Filler and gap may differ in category

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 4, pp. 1405-1406 (example 8); Section 9, p. 1420
- What the chapter says: "That he was sick, we talked about for days" is good although "about" cannot take a clause in situ. HPSG accounts (Borsley and Crysmann 2021, Section 9) allow the fronted phrase and the gap to differ in certain features; in LFG the split between matching and non-matching features is built into the separation of f-structure and c-structure.
- Why it matters: Replay of a filler-gap rule must not identify the filler with the extracted argument in full; which features are shared is a grammar-specific choice. A grammar with full identity will fail on such sentences and send them to the relaxed path. (My inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. One syntactic analysis, several scope readings

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 12, p. 1426; Section 12.1, pp. 1427-1428; Section 12.3, pp. 1431-1435 (examples 45, 54, 55)
- What the chapter says: "Everybody called somebody" has one syntactic analysis and two readings; in Glue Semantics the two readings are two proofs over the same f-structure. The interest in underspecified semantic representations arose from large-scale grammar development, so that scope ambiguities can be "compactly encoded without the need for full enumeration". MRS (Copestake et al. 2005) and Lexical Resource Semantics are named as the HPSG examples, and MRS is said to share with Glue the goal of an underspecified theory of scope. A detailed comparison of Glue and MRS is declined.
- Why it matters: A derivation determines the semantics only up to scope. This agrees with the design as long as the "by-product" is an underspecified MRS and scope resolution is left to a later step.
- DELPH-IN stance: MRS is named as an influential underspecified approach, linked to the LinGO/Grammar Matrix and CoreGram efforts (p. 1427). No detail.

### 10. Control that is not defined on subjects

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge, semantics
- Reference: Section 11, pp. 1424-1426 (examples 34-38)
- What the chapter says: In the basic HPSG theory (Pollard and Sag 1994: 132-145), only the outermost dependent of the complement can be raised or controlled, as a consequence of list cancellation. Some languages seem to allow control of non-subjects. In the common Tagalog type (Kroeger 1993), the controllee must be the Actor, whatever its grammatical relation, so the relation is defined on argument structure. Equi is identity of index, raising is identity of the whole subject.
- Why it matters: For English the basic theory holds and the controlled argument can be recovered from the feature structure after replay. For Tagalog-type languages the grammar needs a control mechanism over ARG-ST or semantic roles, and the UD bridge cannot assume that the controlled dependent is the subject. (My inference.)
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- HPSG is close to context-free grammar. An HPSG grammar "largely consists of constraints on local sub-trees", and would be a CFG if the nodes were not complex feature structures (Section 2, p. 1397, citing Sag et al. 2003: 83). This is the property that makes a derivation tree of rule names plus local replay a sensible representation.
- Long distance dependencies are decomposed into a series of local dependencies, with SLASH passed up "by means of strictly local constraints" (Section 9, pp. 1418-1420). Gapless accounts exist (Sag et al. 2003: Chapter 14; Bouma et al. 2001), in which an ARG-ST item is placed on SLASH instead of a valence list. This fits the no-empty-elements assumption.
- Researchers in both frameworks tend to avoid empty elements unless well motivated (Section 1, p. 1396).
- The HPSG lexicon is monotonic if lexical rules are formulated as unary branching rules (Section 8, p. 1418, citing Davis and Koenig 2021). This matches the treatment of lexical rules as unary chain elements in the tag.
- A constructional (traceless) analysis of head mobility is available and common in HPSG (Section 5, p. 1407), although the version described is ternary (caveat 4).
- Both frameworks are "fully explicit and computationally implemented" (abstract, p. 1395), and semantics is built into HPSG signs from the start (Section 12, p. 1426), so semantics as a by-product of syntactic composition is the framework's own design. Underspecified semantics (MRS) was motivated by large-scale grammar development (Section 12.1, pp. 1427-1428).
- LFG annotated phrase structure rules "can often be directly translated" into HPSG schemata (Section 3, p. 1401, example 7), and analyses in one framework "can often be translated into the other" (Section 13, p. 1436). The f-structure is close to a dependency representation (my remark), so this is indirect support for a bridge between derivations and a function-labeled structure; the non-cancellation approach shows such a level can be carried inside an HPSG sign.
- Glue Semantics presupposes nothing about syntax except headedness, and has been paired with HPSG (Asudeh and Crouch 2002b) and with Universal Dependencies (Gotham and Haug 2018) (Section 12.2, p. 1428). This is a pointer for the relaxed output path, where semantics would have to be composed without a full HPSG derivation. The chapter does not develop it.

## Theory strains

- Status of grammatical functions (Section 4, pp. 1404-1405). HPSG has no formal correlate of "subject" across languages; the subject is a language-specific position on ARG-ST or is defined through case. Some HPSG work adds LFG-style function features (Kropp Dakubu et al. 2007; Pollard 1994). The authors present this as a difference of orientation, and it leaves cross-linguistic statements about subjects without a formal anchor in HPSG.
- Head mobility: constructional (flat) analysis versus an empty verbal head (Borsley 1989) (Section 5, pp. 1406-1409). Two competing HPSG analyses; LFG gets the effect without an empty element through co-heads and optional c-structure nodes.
- No output filters in HPSG (Section 6, pp. 1410-1413). The Serbo-Croatian dative/instrumental realization condition and the distribution of English nominative pronouns are handled simply by LFG constraining equations and are "rather difficult otherwise". HPSG foundations rule the device out (Richter 2021); the earlier ANY value was removed. This is the chapter's clearest case of something HPSG cannot state directly.
- Agreement marker versus incorporated pronoun (Section 7, pp. 1413-1415). The distinction is "subtle and controversial". LFG has a dedicated device (PRED 'pro' and uniqueness of PRED); the HPSG translation needs a stipulated exclusive disjunction.
- Linking (Section 8, pp. 1415-1418). The HPSG macro-roles Actor and Undergoer are defined disjunctively and have no unique semantic entailment; they "name whatever semantic roles map to the subject and object" of a transitive verb (footnote 15: the Undergoer of "undergo" is its object). The authors judge that Lexical Mapping Theory and the macro-role theory express virtually the same mappings, so the choice between them is not empirical so far. Footnote 13 records a dispute with Mueller (2018) over whether the Glue-based approach of Asudeh et al. (2014) is "phrasal".
- Gaps versus gapless extraction (Section 9, p. 1418). Both exist in both frameworks; the evidence for gaps (weak crossover and linear position) is called controversial.
- Nonconfigurationality: order domains (Donohue and Sag 1999) versus non-cancellation (Bender 2008) (Section 10, pp. 1423-1424). Both add a level beyond the tree: a DOM list for order, or a persistent COMPS list that works like an f-structure. The chapter stresses that LFG handles these data with its basic architecture, while HPSG needs an extra device either way.
- Locality as a claim of the theory (Section 2, p. 1397; Section 11, p. 1425). HPSG was designed to embody a theory of locality, but non-local devices were added later, and the framework can be used "by syntacticians with no interest in locality". The subject-only restriction on raising and control follows from list cancellation in Pollard and Sag (1994), yet mechanisms that violate it have been proposed, and Tagalog suggests the restriction is not universal. The predictive content of the locality claim is therefore weakened.
- Ordered valence lists versus unordered functions (Section 1, p. 1396); AVMs as descriptions in HPSG versus as models in LFG (p. 1396); neither framework uses a unification operation (footnote 1).
- Semantics (Section 12.1, p. 1427). HPSG has used Situation Semantics (Pollard and Sag 1994; Ginzburg and Sag 2000), then MRS and Lexical Resource Semantics side by side; there is no single HPSG semantics. The authors claim Glue handles quantifiers in non-subject position and scope ambiguity without syntactic operations or type shifting, and decline a comparison with MRS.

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not statements from the chapter.

- The chapter does not say how the ERG or any other implemented grammar analyzes subject-auxiliary inversion, extraction, or verb placement, or whether those grammars use binary rules, traces, or order domains. The caveats above concern the theoretical literature; which of them apply to the ERG has to be checked against the grammar itself.
- No direct comparison of MRS with Glue, and nothing on how MRS is composed during a derivation.
- UD appears only in the citation of Gotham and Haug (2018). The chapter does not relate f-structures to dependency trees, although the resemblance (functions as labeled relations, function words as co-heads of content words) is relevant to the UD bridge and to the UD policy of attaching function words to content words.
- Coordination, ellipsis, and multiword expressions, which are standard test cases for both the tree encoding and the UD bridge, are not treated in this chapter.
- Parsing complexity and generative capacity are mentioned only historically (Section 2, p. 1397: Swiss German and Bambara exceed context-free power). The chapter does not say what formal power HPSG or LFG grammars have.
