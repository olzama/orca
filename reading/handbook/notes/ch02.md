# Chapter 2: The evolution of HPSG (Dan Flickinger, Carl Pollard, Thomas Wasow)

Handbook pages 47-87 (text ends on p. 71; the rest is references). Source read: `reading/handbook/txt/ch02.txt`, whole chapter body.

## Summary

The chapter is a history of the framework, with little technical analysis. It traces HPSG from its precursors (GPSG and Pollard's Head Grammar) through the Hewlett-Packard NL project, the early architecture (no metarules, lexical rules, list-valued SUBCAT and SLASH, a handful of schematic rules), and the two books Pollard and Sag 1987 and 1994. It then names two later theoretical stages (the valence and trace revisions of Pollard and Sag 1994 chapter 9, and the construction type hierarchy of Sag 1997 and Ginzburg and Sag 2000), and describes the LinGO project, the ERG, MRS, DELPH-IN, TRALE and other platforms and applications. The closing section discusses the relation of HPSG to statistical NLP, Minimalism, Construction Grammar and SBCG, and the growing role of corpus and experimental data.

Bearing on the parser design is modest. Most of what matters is historical background on why the design assumptions (concatenation, traces, rule arity) are choices that the theory has made differently at different times.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. Non-concatenative combination and discontinuous constituency are part of the HPSG lineage

- Severity: medium (low for the ERG as described here; it grows with the multi-language goal)
- Assumption affected: continuity
- Reference: section 2.2, pp. 50-52; section 10, footnote 10, p. 67
- What the chapter says: Head Grammar replaced strings with headed strings and added an operation that inserts one string directly to the left or right of another string's head, in addition to concatenation. Pollard 1984 used this for discontinuous constituency in Dutch, and the analysis also works for the Swiss German cross-serial dependencies that Shieber showed to be beyond context-free grammars. The chapter states that HG's handling of word order "seems not to have been pursued further within the HPSG framework", but that two ideas stayed implicit in later HPSG: word order needs data structures richer than strings, and operations other than concatenation. These were "more fully fleshed out" in the linearization grammars of Reape (1992, 1996), Kathol (1995, 2000) and Mueller (1995, 1996, 1999, 2004). Footnote 10 mentions, via Mueller 2020, that "discontinuous constituents" are among the implementation techniques found in HPSG grammars.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter does not say which implemented grammars use discontinuous constituents or how DELPH-IN grammars avoid them; footnote 10 only says the technique exists in HPSG grammars.

### 2. Traces: early HPSG had empty elements; the standard analysis later removed them

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements (and leaves-equal-tokens)
- Reference: section 5, pp. 59-60 (Figure 1 and example (6)); section 6, p. 61
- What the chapter says: In early HPSG the doubly gapped VP "play _ on _" is analyzed with two trace leaves (t) of the form A[SLASH <A>], which correspond to hypothesis-introducing axioms in Lambek-style categorial grammar. Chapter 9 of Pollard and Sag 1994 eliminated traces altogether by introducing three lexical rules for the extraction of subjects, complements and adjuncts. The chapter calls the revised valence analysis the one "that came to be viewed as part of the standard HPSG framework".
- Consequence for the design, as far as the chapter supports it: a traceless analysis moves the gap decision into lexical rules, so it lands in the unary rule chain that the tag must predict for the head word.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter does not say whether the ERG follows the traceless analysis.

### 3. Early schematic rules are not binary

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules (tree-encoding)
- Reference: section 5, pp. 58 and 60
- What the chapter says: Pollard 1985 covered English local grammar with three rules, two of which combine a head with all of its arguments, or all of its non-subject arguments, in one step. Figure 1 shows a ternary node (V, NP, PP). The chapter contrasts this with "the binary branching of the Lambek analysis".
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. List-valued SLASH and the infinite category set

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: other (formal properties; tree-encoding indirectly)
- Reference: section 5, pp. 56-57 and 58-59
- What the chapter says: HPSG replaced the single category-valued SLASH of GPSG with a list (or set) of categories so that constituents with two undischarged dependencies can be analyzed ("Violins this finely crafted, even the most challenging sonatas are easy to play _ on _"). Each schematic rule can then be instantiated in infinitely many ways and context-freeness is given up; "this was not seen as problematic". The Binding Inheritance Principle (later Nonlocal Feature Principle) makes the mother's value the union of the daughters' values.
- Consequence for the design, as far as the chapter supports it: the derivation tree names rules, not categories, so the infinite category set does not enlarge the label inventory; multiple gaps do mean that the information a tag has to carry about extraction is not bounded by one gap per constituent.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Grammar coverage is incomplete, and some treebanks hold analyses the grammar cannot produce

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: section 9, pp. 66-67; section 10, pp. 67-68 and 70
- What the chapter says: Practical HPSG systems "have necessarily included components induced via statistical methods"; without them "the systems cannot deal with the full variety of forms encountered in usage data". The 1328-sentence HP test suite "does not come close to covering the variety of sentence forms that are found in corpora". Treebanks record the analyses intended by the grammarian, and some, "notably the Alpino Treebank" (Dutch), include analyses "which the grammar may not yet be able to produce automatically".
- Consequence for the design: strict replay will fail on some input for coverage reasons, and a treebank is not by definition replayable by its grammar. The chapter makes the second point for Alpino only, not for the ERG treebanks.
- DELPH-IN stance: several DELPH-IN grammars, including the ERG, follow Oepen, Flickinger, Toutanova and Manning 2004: a manually annotated treebank of sentences parsed by the grammar trains a statistical model that picks the most likely analysis at run time.

## Support for the design

- Rule-to-rule semantics (section 4, p. 54): GPSG took from Montague the idea that each phrase structure rule is paired with a semantic rule that computes the mother's meaning from the daughters' meanings, and this was carried into HPSG. This is the premise behind semantics as a by-product of replay.
- MRS (section 7, pp. 62-63): MRS is described as the "more computationally useful semantic formalism" that emerged in LinGO and was elaborated together with the ERG for both parsing and generation.
- Constructional Localism (section 10, pp. 69-70): constructions "license mother-daughter configurations without reference to embedding or embedded contexts"; nonlocal dependencies must be "locally encoded in signs". This is the locality that a rule-by-rule replay relies on. The chapter attributes it to SBCG (Sag 2012) and says it serves formal restriction and computational tractability.
- A small rule inventory (section 5, p. 58): HPSG has "only a handful of schematic rules", unlike a context-free grammar. A small set of rule names is what a tag-based tree encoding needs.
- Lexical rules and the type hierarchy (section 5, pp. 56-57; section 4, p. 56): passive, extraposition and subject extraction are handled by lexical rules on lexical entries, and the lexicon is a multiple-inheritance hierarchy (Flickinger, Pollard and Wasow 1985; Flickinger 1987). This matches a label made of a lexical type plus a chain of lexical rules.
- Traceless extraction as the standard analysis (section 6, p. 61): see caveat 2; the direction of the theory agrees with the no-empty-elements assumption.
- Constructions and multiword patterns without new machinery (section 10, p. 69): a hierarchy of constructs for partially lexicalized expressions such as "Ved X's way PP" involves "no fundamental change to the technical machinery" and "has been a part of the LinGO implementation for many years".
- Compatibility with machine learning (section 10, pp. 67-68): "there is no incompatibility between the use of HPSG and the machine learning methods of mainstream NLP".
- Treebank-trained disambiguation (section 9, pp. 66-67): the existing practice of training a statistical model on grammar-parsed, manually annotated treebanks is the same data source that a tagger would train on.
- Grammar Matrix (section 9, pp. 65-66): a starter kit generalized from the ERG and Jacy, with a customization system, underlies several DELPH-IN grammars. This is relevant to the multi-language aim because those grammars share a core.

## Theory strains

- Word order as a "promissory note" (section 2.2, p. 51): the Constituent Order Principle of Pollard and Sag 1987 "was really more of a promissory note than an actual principle". Competing treatments: Head Grammar head-wrapping (not pursued), versus the later linearization grammars of Reape, Kathol and Mueller.
- Valence representation (section 6, p. 61; section 5, pp. 57-58): a single SUBCAT list (Pollard 1985; Pollard and Sag 1987; chapters 1-8 of Pollard and Sag 1994) versus SUBJ, SPR and COMPS (chapter 9 of Pollard and Sag 1994, following Borsley 1987-1990). The chapter says "issues of valence representation cross-linguistically remain a matter of robust debate".
- Traces versus lexical rules for extraction (section 5, pp. 59-60; section 6, p. 61): the same book (Pollard and Sag 1994) contains both analyses; chapter 9 revises the first eight chapters.
- SLASH as set or list (section 5, pp. 58-59): Pollard 1985 has a list; the Binding Inheritance Principle of Pollard 1986 treats it as a set. The chapter does not resolve this ("list- (or set-) valued").
- Metarules (sections 4-5, pp. 55-57): central to GPSG, eliminated in HPSG because they threaten context-freeness and finiteness (Uszkoreit and Peters 1982; Ristad 1986); replaced by lexical rules and schematic rules. GPSG "avoided this problem by not analyzing" multiple-gap examples.
- Formal foundations (section 5, pp. 60-61): feature structures as partial information (Pollard and Sag 1987, after Rounds and Kasper, Moshier and Rounds) versus feature structures as models with separate descriptions (King 1989; Pollard and Sag 1994). The chapter says the difference seems "not to have had a lasting effect" on theoretical use or on implementation.
- Context-freeness and generative capacity (section 2.2, pp. 50-51; section 5, p. 57): GPSG's context-free hypothesis was refuted by Shieber 1985 and Culy 1985; HG is mildly context-sensitive; HPSG dropped the concern altogether.
- Phrasal constructions versus general schemata (section 6, pp. 61-62; section 10, pp. 68-70): Constructional HPSG (Sag 1997; Ginzburg and Sag 2000) and SBCG (Sag 2012) add hierarchies of phrasal types. HPSG and SBCG insist on locality; much other Construction Grammar work allows direct reference to properties of subordinate constituents (Fillmore 1988). The chapter calls locality the "one important theoretical issue" dividing them.
- What belongs in the grammar (section 10, p. 70): Hofmeister and Sag 2010, Chaves and Dery 2019 and Chaves and Putnam 2020 argue that many island constraints are not grammatical; Shiraishi et al. 2019 make a similar claim for some parallelism effects in Right Node Raising. This reduces "what grammars are responsible for".
- HPSG and Minimalism (section 10, pp. 67-68): the Minimalist Program is "too vaguely formulated for a rigorous comparison"; both incorporate ideas from Categorial Grammar.
- SLASH and Categorial Grammar (section 4, p. 55; section 5, pp. 58-60): the resemblance of SLASH to the CG connectives is "superficial and misleading"; SUBCAT is the true analog of the CG slashes, and SLASH corresponds to hypothetical reasoning in Lambek-style systems.

## Not covered here

These are my own remarks, not claims from the chapter.

- The chapter does not say whether the ERG or other DELPH-IN grammars use traces, discontinuous constituents, or only unary and binary rules. The design assumptions are neither confirmed nor contradicted here; chapters on computational linguistics (chapter 25, Bender and Emerson), constituent order (chapter 10), and unbounded dependencies should settle this.
- MRS is only named. Scope underspecification, and whether the semantics is fully determined by the derivation tree, are not discussed.
- Bouma appears only as an author of the Dutch Alpino grammar and the HDrug system. The work on adjuncts and extraction (Bouma, Malouf and Sag) that the user request points toward is not mentioned in this chapter.
- Nothing in the chapter concerns dependency representations or Universal Dependencies.
- Coordination and ellipsis are mentioned only in passing (coverage of the HP grammar; Right Node Raising as an experimental topic), with no analysis.
