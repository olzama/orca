# Chapter 18: Negation (Jong-Bok Kim)

Handbook pages 811-845 (text 811-839, references 839-845). Section and page references below use the printed handbook page numbers.

## Summary

The chapter surveys HPSG analyses of sentential negation by the form of the negative marker: adverbial negatives (English not, French pas), morphological negatives (Turkish, Japanese, Libyan Arabic, Polish), negative auxiliary verbs (Korean, Basque, Finnish), and preverbal negatives (Italian non, Welsh ddim). The common thread is the lexicalist claim that there is no uniform category Neg or NegP, no verb movement, and no functional projections: each marker gets its position from its lexical description, from lexical rules, from finiteness features of the verb, and from linear precedence constraints. English not is analyzed twice, as a modifier of a non-finite VP (constituent negation) and as a complement of a finite auxiliary added by a lexical rule (sentential negation). Negative auxiliaries and Italian non form a verb complex with the lexical verb through a HEAD-LIGHT schema with argument composition. A final section sketches the Polish genitive of negation and negative concord in French, Polish, and Welsh.

The chapter is theoretical. It does not mention parsing or any implemented grammar or system: DELPH-IN, LKB, ACE, TRALE, Enju, and the Grammar Matrix are all absent, so every "DELPH-IN stance" entry below is "not discussed". The only contacts with the DELPH-IN world are one footnote saying that Warner (2000) and Bender and Lascarides (2013) treat the scope of negation "within the system of Minimal Recursion Semantics" (p. 818, footnote 7) and citations of Crowgey and Bender (2011) and Crowgey (2012) for Basque and for the negative lexical rule (pp. 826, 831). The chapter does not say that these works are tied to implemented grammars.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. English not has two analyses over the same string, with different scope

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / UD-bridge / semantics
- Reference: Section 2.2, pp. 816-818 (lexical description 12, Figure 1); Section 2.3, pp. 818-821 (examples 17-18, 23, lexical rule 22, Figure 2)
- What the chapter says: not is (a) an adverb that modifies a non-finite VP and takes it as the argument of neg-rel, and (b) a member of a special adverb class AdvI that a finite auxiliary takes as an extra complement after the Adverb-Complement Lexical Rule. "The president could not approve the bill" therefore has two structures, `could [not [approve the bill]]` and `[could] [not] [approve the bill]`, which correspond to the narrow-scope and the wide-scope reading of negation.
- Why it matters: the scope difference is carried by the derivation (rule names and one lexical rule on the auxiliary), so replay is deterministic once the tree is given. The burden falls on the tagger, which must choose between two derivations of an identical string on semantic grounds, and on the UD bridge, where both structures would correspond to the same dependency tree (the chapter says nothing about dependencies; this consequence is my inference from the two structures in 23).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. Footnote 7 (p. 818) only points to Warner (2000) and Bender and Lascarides (2013) for scope interactions of negation with modals and quantifiers in MRS.

### 2. Flat head-complement structures with the negator as a sister of the verb

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 2.3, pp. 820-821 (Figure 2), p. 824 (Figure 5)
- What the chapter says: the output of the lexical rule has COMPS <AdvI, VP> and the auxiliary combines with both complements at once in a ternary head-complement phrase (V, not, VP). French is the same with any finite verb: n'aime, pas, Stacy are three sisters.
- Why it matters: the planned tree encoding assumes unary and binary rules. The structures have to be binarized, and the order of complement saturation (negator first, then VP or object) must be fixed by the grammar. The chapter does not address binary branching.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 3. Lexical rules with no visible form change add to the label space

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (size and ambiguity of the lexical-rule chain in the tag)
- Reference: Section 2.3, p. 820 (rule 22, footnotes 9-10), p. 821 (rule 24), pp. 823-824 (French)
- What the chapter says: the Adverb-Complement Lexical Rule maps a finite auxiliary to one that also selects AdvI (not, too, so, indeed). The predicate ellipsis rule (24) applies to the output of that rule. In French the first rule has no AUX restriction, so every finite verb form (including imperative, subjunctive, and present participle, which count as finite in French) can undergo it. Footnote 9 notes that the rule is stated on COMPS but that "more recent variants use the ARG-ST list".
- Why it matters: these rules fit the design, since they are unary steps that a tag can name. The cost is that a finite verb or auxiliary carries one more zero-marked alternative, decided only by the presence of a following negator, and the rules can chain (22 then 24).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. VP ellipsis after not: an unexpressed argument with no semantics supplied

- Severity: medium (for MRS as a by-product), low for replay
- Assumption affected: semantics / no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 2.3, pp. 819-822 (examples 20, rule 24, Figures 3-4, footnote 8)
- What the chapter says: the ellipsis rule turns the VP argument into a non-canonical "small pro" that stays on ARG-ST and is not mapped to COMPS, so "could not" is a complete head-complement phrase with no empty element. never cannot be stranded because a modifier needs a VP to modify.
- Why it matters: no empty category is posited, which agrees with the design. The rule says nothing about how the meaning of the missing VP is recovered, so a successful replay gives an MRS with an unresolved argument.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. One token, two predications, and negators that are not tokens in the grammar

- Severity: medium (high for the multilingual UD bridge)
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 3, pp. 825-827 (examples 30-31, rule 32, Libyan Arabic 33-34, footnotes 12-13); Section 2.3, p. 823, footnote 11; Section 2.2, pp. 816-817 (12); Section 6, p. 835
- What the chapter says: in Turkish and Japanese the negative marker is a verbal suffix "formed in the lexical component" with "no syntactic status"; a lexical rule (32) builds the negative verb, sets HEAD|POL neg, and wraps the verb's content in neg-rel. Libyan Arabic ma-...-s, described as a proclitic and an enclitic, is treated the same way, as a prefix and a suffix added by one rule to one word (following Borsley and Krer 2012). Polish nie is called a prefix on the verbal expression. For French, footnote 11 says that ne can be taken as an inflectional affix optionally realized on the output of the lexical rule (following Abeillé and Godard 2002), while the entry in (12) for constituent negation is given for "ne-pas" as a single adverb.
- Why it matters: in these analyses negation is a step in the lexical-rule chain of the verb's tag, which the tree encoding handles well. The mismatch arises with tokenization: whenever a treebank writes the marker as its own token (French ne/n', Polish nie, Libyan Arabic clitics, "ne pas" as two words), the grammar has no leaf for it, or one leaf for two tokens. The chapter does not discuss tokenization; the conflict with separate-token treebanks is my inference.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter cites Crowgey (2012: 111-112) for "a similar treatment" of the negative lexical rule and refers to Crysmann (2021, Chapter 21) for inflection.

### 6. Verb complexes: HEAD-LIGHT schema, argument composition, and the negator as head

- Severity: medium for the many-languages goal, none for English
- Assumption affected: UD-bridge / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 4, pp. 828-832 (Figure 6, schema 40, Figures 7-8, example 41); Section 5, pp. 833-834 (lexical description 45, Figure 9)
- What the chapter says: the Korean negative auxiliary anh- combines with the bare lexical verb (not with a VP) in a head-light-phrase whose COMPS list is that of the lexical verb, so that argument composition happens "at the syntax level, rather than in the lexicon". The process is recursive with no upper limit on the number of auxiliaries. Basque ez-ditu is analyzed the same way with the opposite order (Crowgey and Bender 2011). Italian non is "an independent lexical head, even though it is a clitic": it selects a verb and that verb's complements, and its HEAD value is "in a sense undetermined", shared with the HEAD value of the verb it combines with.
- Why it matters: the structures are binary and continuous, so tagging and replay are not threatened. The derivation tree for a clause is different from the English one (verb cluster first, objects attached above the cluster), and the head of the cluster is the negative auxiliary or the particle non, while the lexical verb is its complement. A conversion to and from dependency trees, where the lexical verb is normally the head and the negator a dependent, has to invert this headedness (the dependency side is my inference). The underspecified HEAD of non also means that the category of the mother is known only after unification.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 7. A linearization-based analysis with order domains for Italian and Welsh

- Severity: medium (it would be high if adopted; the chapter also gives selectional alternatives)
- Assumption affected: continuity / tree-encoding
- Reference: Section 5, pp. 834-835 (domains 47-48, footnotes 15-16)
- What the chapter says: Borsley (2006), adopting Kathol's (2000) topological approach, places negative expressions through a DOM list of elements assigned to topological fields (first, second, third); Italian non (NEG +) must be in the second field, Welsh ddim and Italian nessuno in the third. This is contrasted with the selectional approach of Borsley and Jones (2000, 2005), where the negative expression or negative verb selects its complements.
- Why it matters: in an order-domain analysis the surface order is computed from DOM lists and field constraints, so the derivation tree no longer determines the string by concatenation, and the planned tree encoding does not apply directly. The chapter presents both approaches without choosing between them.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 8. Word order stated by separate LP constraints

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding / other
- Reference: Section 2.1, pp. 815-816; Section 2.2, pp. 817-818
- What the chapter says: the position of not and pas before a non-finite VP follows from the lexical feature PRE-MODIFIER + "together with an LP rule requiring such adjuncts to precede the head they modify" (reference to Müller, Chapter 10). Phrase types license the combinations; "independently motivated Linear Precedence (LP) constraints" order them.
- Why it matters: the derivation tree as the chapter conceives it names an unordered combination plus an LP constraint, whereas the planned design expects rule names that already fix daughter order. This is a difference in bookkeeping only as long as the constituents stay continuous.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Negative concord: two readings from one structure, chosen by quantifier retrieval at the verb

- Severity: medium (for languages with negative concord; standard English is not discussed)
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / semantics
- Reference: Section 6, pp. 837-838 (examples 53-56, footnotes 17-19)
- What the chapter says: in de Swart and Sag (2002) each negative quantifier is put in QSTORE and retrieved "at the lexical level"; the verb n'aime in "Personne n'aime personne" has "two different ways of retrieving the QSTORE value", giving QUANTS <1, 2> (double negation) or QUANTS <1> after merging the two into one resumptive quantifier (negative concord). pas, as a complement and a lexically stored quantifier, does not merge in standard French, which gives double negation in (55); footnote 19 points to cases where pas does induce concord. Borsley and Jones (2000) use features NEG and NC for Welsh instead.
- Why it matters: the two readings share the syntactic structure and differ only in the QUANTS value of the verb, so a derivation tree made of rule names does not determine the semantics unless the retrieval option is itself recorded as a lexical choice in the tag. The merging of two quantifiers into one is also not an ordinary scope ambiguity. The chapter does not say how this would be rendered in MRS.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter refers to Richter and Sailer (2004) and to Koenig and Richter (Chapter 22, Section 6.2.1) for other treatments.

### 10. Long-distance genitive of negation: case fixed through optional argument composition

- Severity: low to medium (Polish; relevant to the multilingual goal)
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay / UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 6, pp. 835-837 (examples 49-50, principle 51, representation 52)
- What the chapter says: Polish nie plus verb stem introduces NEG +; a Case Principle makes structural non-initial arguments of a NEG + verb genitive. For the non-local case ("Janek nie wydawał się lubić Marii/Marię") Przepiórkowski (2000) lets the raising verb "optionally undergo lexical argument composition", so that the embedded object appears on the ARG-ST of the negated matrix verb and gets genitive; without composition it stays accusative.
- Why it matters: the case of an NP token depends on a zero-marked, optional lexical process on a verb two heads away, so the tagger has to select the composed or uncomposed variant of the raising verb from the case of a distant object. In a dependency tree the object hangs from the lower verb, while its case is licensed by the higher one.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- No empty elements, no movement, no functional projections: "the non-derivational, lexicalist analyses of HPSG license all surface structures by the system of phrase types and constraints" (Section 2.1, pp. 815-816; also pp. 812-813, 827, 838-839). All four negation types are handled with lexical descriptions, lexical rules, and ordinary phrase types.
- Negation as a lexical rule in the verb's derivation: morphological negation is a unary lexical rule on the verb lexeme that adds neg-rel over the verb's content (rule 32, p. 826), and sentential not is licensed by a unary lexical rule on the auxiliary (rule 22, p. 820). Both are the kind of step the planned tag (lexical type plus rule chain) is meant to encode.
- Lexical integrity: affixes are not syntactic nodes, so leaves are whole words (Section 3, pp. 825-827; Section 7, p. 839).
- Ellipsis without an empty category: the elided VP is a non-canonical argument that never reaches COMPS (rule 24, Figure 3, pp. 821-822).
- Semantics composed locally: the modifier not takes the modified VP as ARG1 of neg-rel (12, p. 817), the lexical rule wraps the verb's content (32, p. 826), and Italian non takes its verbal complement's CONT as ARG1 (45, p. 833). In each case the semantics follows from the unification steps.
- Verb complexes in Korean, Basque, and Italian are binary and continuous (schema 40, Figures 7-9, pp. 829-834).
- The chapter's conclusion that the syntax of negation differs across languages while "a certain universal aspect of negation does exist, viz. its semantic contribution" (Section 7, p. 839) is in line with pairing language-specific strict analyses with a coarse shared semantic representation.
- Scope of negation with modals and quantifiers has been treated in MRS (footnote 7, p. 818).

## Theory strains

- Two entries and a special adverb class for not (Sections 2.2-2.3, pp. 816-821). not is both a VP[nonfin] modifier and a complement of finite auxiliaries; the complement use rests on a class AdvI (not, too, so, indeed) introduced by the author ("which I call AdvI", p. 820) and on turning an adverb into a complement, an idea credited to Miller (1992) and Abeillé and Godard (1997) for French (p. 823).
- A counterexample to the never/not contrast (Section 2.3, p. 819, footnote 8). An attested example has never stranded after a modal ("whereas she could never"); the chapter says this "seems to be possible when the adverb expresses a contrastive focus meaning" and gives no analysis, although the account of Figure 4 (p. 822) predicts it to be impossible.
- COMPS-based against ARG-ST-based statement of the lexical rule (p. 820, footnote 9), and the title of Warner (2000), "English auxiliaries without lexical rules", cited for the same analysis that the chapter states as a lexical rule (p. 820).
- Finiteness classes are set per language: the present participle is a subtype of fin in French and of nonfin in English (p. 816, footnote 6; pp. 823-824). The classification carries the whole account of pas placement.
- Status of French ne: part of a single adverb "ne-pas" in (12) (pp. 816-817), "negative morphology (ne-marking)" on the verb (p. 818), or an inflectional affix optionally realized on the output of the lexical rule (p. 823, footnote 11). The chapter does not reconcile these (the observation that they differ is mine).
- Acceptability of "n'avoir pas", "n'être pas" with infinitival auxiliaries is restricted to "certain conservative varieties of French" (Section 2.1, p. 815).
- Clitic or affix: Libyan Arabic ma- and -s are called clitics and then treated as affixes (p. 826); -s is absent when the clause has an n-word or NPI (footnote 13). Italian non is a clitic and at the same time an independent lexical head with an underspecified HEAD value, justified by Monachesi's split between affix-like and word-like clitics (Section 5, pp. 833-834, footnote 14).
- Korean negative auxiliary: VP-complement structure against verb-complex structure (Section 4, p. 828, Figure 6). The chapter argues for the verb complex from adjacency and fragment answers (pp. 828-829).
- Argument composition in the syntax (HEAD-LIGHT schema, p. 830) against argument composition in the lexicon (as in Przepiórkowski's analysis, p. 836).
- Welsh and Italian: selectional analysis (Borsley and Jones 2000, 2005) against linearization with topological fields (Borsley 2006) (Section 5, pp. 834-835, footnote 16).
- Negative concord: lexical retrieval of stored quantifiers with resumption (de Swart and Sag 2002) against NEG/NC features (Borsley and Jones 2000), with further alternatives only cited (Przepiórkowski and Kupść 1999; Richter and Sailer 2004) (Section 6, pp. 837-838). pas does not merge with n-words in standard French but does in other cases (footnote 19).
- HPSG against the derivational NegP view (Section 1, pp. 812-813; Section 2.1, p. 815; Section 7, pp. 838-839). The chapter concedes that the derivational view offers one uniform category and parallel structures across languages, and answers that this misses the particular properties of each negation type. The HPSG side has no single syntactic treatment of negation, only a shared semantic contribution.
- Coverage gaps acknowledged: German nicht "behaves quite differently" and is left to Müller (2016) (p. 813, footnote 5); Korean has both a negative auxiliary and a morphological negative, of which only the auxiliary is analyzed (p. 812, footnote 2); Hausa discontinuous negation (Crysmann 2010) is cited but not presented (p. 813).

## Not covered here

All items in this section are my own remarks, not claims of the chapter.

- The chapter never says how the ERG or any other implemented grammar analyzes not, contracted n't, or do-support. Whether the ERG uses the complement analysis, the modifier analysis, or both has to be checked in the grammar itself.
- English n't (contracted negation, a tokenization question for the tagger) is not discussed at all.
- Negative polarity items, negative inversion ("Never have I ..."), and negative quantifiers in English (nobody, no) are not treated; NPIs are mentioned only in passing for Libyan Arabic.
- The MRS representation of negation (neg-rel as a scopal predication with a handle argument, and how constituent against sentential scope appears in handle constraints) is not given; the chapter uses a simplified CONT with neg-rel and ARG1.
- The chapter's caveats 1, 5, and 6 mention consequences for dependency trees and tokenization; the chapter itself says nothing about Universal Dependencies or tokenization, so those consequences are inferences.
- Discontinuous (bipartite) negation as a real discontinuity problem is only cited (Crysmann 2010 on Hausa), not analyzed.
