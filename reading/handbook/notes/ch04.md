# Chapter 4: The nature and role of the lexicon in HPSG (Anthony R. Davis and Jean-Pierre Koenig)

Handbook pages 125-176 (text pp. 125-167, then references). Source read: `reading/handbook/txt/ch04.txt`.

## Summary

The chapter presents HPSG's lexicalism: words are syntactic atoms (Lexical Integrity), and every distinct syntactic context of a head corresponds to a distinct lexical variant, so lexical entries are informationally rich and phrase-structure schemas are few. It defends this stance against recent challenges (phrases inside words, word-internal ellipsis, suspended affixation, endoclitics) while conceding that the questions are not settled. It then describes how redundancy is avoided: the hierarchical lexicon with multiple inheritance, implicational constraints on substructures of signs, and default (nonmonotonic) inheritance (YADU; Malouf's gerunds). Finally it covers lexical rules (their formalization, their modern form as unary-branching rules, their use for extraction and clitics) and two alternatives based on underspecification: canonical vs. non-canonical synsem types (Bouma, Malouf and Sag 2001; Miller and Sag 1997) and online type construction (Koenig and Jurafsky 1995; Koenig 1999).

The chapter bears on the parser mainly through the lexical end of the tree encoding: what a "lexical type" is, what a chain of lexical rules is, and whether a word is always one continuous leaf. It says nothing about parsing algorithms, MRS, or dependency conversion.

## Caveats for the parser

### 1. The same phenomenon may or may not be a node in the derivation, depending on the analysis chosen

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (also deterministic-replay)
- Reference: Section 5.2, pp. 158-159; Section 5.3, pp. 159-161, 164-165
- Description: Extraction of a complement and affixal (clitic) realization of an argument have two competing treatments. With a lexical rule (Complement Extraction Lexical Rule, Pollard and Sag 1994: 378; Monachesi's Clitic Lexical Rule), the alternation is a unary step that would appear as a rule name in the lexical chain of a token. With the underspecification treatment (Bouma et al. 2001; Miller and Sag 1997), there is no rule at all: one underspecified entry covers both realizations, and constraints on `word` decide which ARG-ST members are `canon-ss`, `gap-ss` or `aff-ss`. Under the second treatment the derivation tree carries no label marking the choice; the choice is resolved by unification with the context. The inventory of labels the tagger must predict therefore depends on which analysis a given grammar adopts, and across grammars and languages the "chain of lexical rules" will not be comparable. The chapter also mentions trace-based analyses as a third option (fn. 12, p. 165), which conflicts with the no-empty-elements assumption (see caveat 9).
- DELPH-IN stance: The chapter does not say which treatment of extraction or clitics the ERG uses. It only says that lexical rules in the ERG, CoreGram and Grammix are unary-branching rules (pp. 130, 156).

### 2. Lexical rules as stated in the theory are relational: the rule name alone need not fix the output

- Severity: medium
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay
- Reference: Section 5.2, pp. 158-159 (rules (35) and (36)); Section 5.1, pp. 155-156
- Description: The CELR in (35) applies to "any" complement in a COMPS list written `<..., 3, ...>`; Monachesi's rule (36) uses the shuffle operation, "since any member of the input's COMPS list can be realized as a clitic". As formulated, one rule name applied to one input licenses several outputs (which complement is extracted, which subset is cliticized). A derivation tree that records only the rule name is then not a complete recipe. The observation that this threatens deterministic replay is my reading of the formulations the chapter displays; the chapter does not discuss it. Separately, the chapter reports that the formal semantics of lexical rules (what is carried over from input to output "by default") is still not settled: Meurers's (2001) formulation "does not always work as intended", per Lahm (2016), although Lahm found no example of practical import.
- DELPH-IN stance: Not discussed beyond the statement that ERG/CoreGram/Grammix implement lexical rules as unary rules with the input as daughter (p. 156). Nothing is said about how implemented grammars make such rules functional.

### 3. Endoclitics and discontinuous lexical items

- Severity: medium (low for English; relevant to the multilingual aim)
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens, continuity
- Reference: Section 2.2, pp. 132-134
- Description: In Udi and Pashto a clitic can sit inside a monomorphemic verb root, splitting it into two pieces with no independent morphological status (examples (5), (6)). European Portuguese pronominal clitics appear word-internally at morpheme boundaries. The HPSG analyses cited (Crysmann 2000, 2001, 2003; Dost 2007) use Reape-style order domains and Kathol's topological fields, and resolve the Udi case "on the basis of discontinuous lexical items", paralleling "HPSG's representation of syntactic discontinuity". A leaf of the derivation is then not a continuous token, and the analysis requires word order domains, which a concatenative derivation tree does not have. The authors concede these analyses "appear to breach the wall of the strictest kind of Lexical Integrity".
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 4. Word-part ellipsis, suspended affixation, lexical sharing

- Severity: medium (low for English)
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens, tree-encoding, UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 2.2, pp. 131-132
- Description: In "over- and under-application" (3), ellipsis targets part of a word. Chaves (2008, 2014) keeps Lexical Integrity by giving words (and phrases) a structured morphophonology list (MP) and letting a single ellipsis rule delete a member of that list. The parser consequence: a token such as "over-" must be licensed as a full word whose second MP member is elided, so the surface token does not match the lexical entry's form, and an ellipsis operation over phonology sits outside rule-by-rule concatenation. For Turkish suspended affixation (4), the chapter leaves open whether Chaves's analysis extends or whether "lexical sharing (where a single word can be the daughter of two c-structure nodes ...)" as in Broadwell (2008) is needed. Lexical sharing would make the derivation a non-tree.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 5. Clitics analyzed as affixes: one HPSG word, possibly several orthographic or UD tokens

- Severity: medium (for Romance and similar languages; low for English)
- Assumption affected: leaves-equal-tokens, UD-bridge
- Reference: Section 3.2, pp. 136-137 (entries (9), (10)); Section 5.2, p. 159; Section 5.3, pp. 160-161
- Description: Following Miller and Sag (1997), French non-subject clitics are prefixes: "y va" is a single lexical entry with FORM `y-va`, empty COMPS, and an ARG-ST member of type `p-aff`. Monachesi (1993) treats Italian object clitics the same way. The leaf of the derivation is then the clitic-plus-verb unit, and the clitic's argument role is discharged inside the word. The mismatch with a tokenization that separates clitics (and so with a dependency treebank that gives the clitic its own node) is my inference; the chapter does not discuss tokenization or UD.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 6. Online type construction: the set of lexical types need not be a closed, declared inventory

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (label inventory)
- Reference: Section 5.3, pp. 161-164 (Figures 7, 8); fn. 11, p. 162
- Description: In the Type Underspecified Hierarchical Lexicon (Koenig and Jurafsky 1995; Koenig 1999), combinations of orthogonal dimensions (for example `fin+trans`, or an entry of *aller* combined with one of its four suppletive stems) are not declared in the grammar; they are "dynamically created when an instance ... needs to be produced or comprehended". Crysmann and Bonami (2016) use the same idea for productive inflection. A tagger label that names "the lexical type" then names a combination that exists nowhere in the grammar source, and the engine would have to construct it. The chapter contrasts this with the standard requirement that the hierarchy be sort-resolved, with every combination reified (Carpenter 1992b; Pollard and Sag 1994), which is the situation the parser design presupposes.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed. The chapter states the standard sort-resolved requirement as typical of HPSG, without reference to implementations.

### 7. Default (nonmonotonic) inheritance

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: deterministic-replay (engine with plain monotonic unification)
- Reference: Section 4.4, pp. 150-155; fn. 10, p. 151
- Description: Default unification (YADU, Lascarides and Copestake 1999) lets more specific types override defeasible information from supertypes; it is used for irregular morphology and for Malouf's (2000) gerunds. A replay engine that only does monotonic unification cannot evaluate such a hierarchy directly. The chapter quotes Malouf (2000: 126) that defaults used this way are "an abbreviatory device", and that "any type hierarchy that uses defaults can be converted into an empirically equivalent one that does not use defaults". Lexical rules are also said to bear "a close relationship to default unification" (Briscoe and Copestake 1999), because input information carries over by default (p. 156).
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed for implemented grammars. The Malouf quotation implies defaults can be compiled out before run time.

### 8. A lexical sign with a phrasal daughter is not excluded

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: tree-encoding (lexical rules only directly above a leaf)
- Reference: Section 2.2, pp. 130-131
- Description: For "a don't-you-dare! look" (2), the authors say nothing in HPSG rules out constructions of the form stem/word -> phrase: a `lexical-sign` may have a `phrasal-sign` as sole daughter. A label scheme that attaches the lexical-rule chain to individual tokens assumes lexical rules never apply above phrasal rules. The authors add that they "do not know of any HPSG work that exploits this possibility".
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 9. Trace-based extraction remains a live alternative

- Severity: low to medium
- Assumption affected: no-empty-elements
- Reference: Section 5.3, p. 165 and fn. 12
- Description: The traceless, underspecification-based account of fillers is adopted "by many", but the chapter flags an "opposing view" and lists trace-based approaches: Levine and Hukari (2006), Müller (2015), Müller and Machicao y Priemer (2019), Müller (2021c). Müller (2015) is the CoreGram paper, so at least one family of implemented grammars is cited among trace-based approaches. Grammars of that family would violate the no-empty-elements assumption.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed for the ERG. CoreGram is cited (via Müller 2015) in the list of trace-based approaches.

### 10. Classical schemata are not binary

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: binary-unary-rules
- Reference: Section 3.4, pp. 140-141
- Description: The chapter describes Pollard and Sag (1994) as having a handful of schemata, "one for a head and its complements" (plural), and says clause structure is "relatively flat". Grammars written to that model have n-ary head-complement phrases.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

### 11. Semantics in the chapter is role-based CONT; one lexeme has several senses sharing forms

- Severity: low
- Assumption affected: semantics
- Reference: Section 3.2, pp. 136-137; Section 4.2, pp. 145-147; Section 5.3, pp. 163-164
- Description: All semantic representations in the chapter are typed relations with role attributes (`go-rel` with THEME/GOAL; `cause-rel` with ACT/UND) and linking is stated as implicational constraints between CONT and ARG-ST. MRS is not mentioned. The chapter stresses that only the stem form and the semantic relation must be stipulated per entry, and that the senses of *aller* ('go to', 'leave', 'fit') share the same four suppletive stems. For MRS as a by-product this means the predicate comes from the choice of entry, which the lexical type plus surface form may not determine when senses share a type.
- DELPH-IN stance: not discussed.

## Support for the design

- Lexical rules are unary-branching rules in implemented grammars. "Lexical rules are essentially equivalent to unary syntactic rules, with the IN attribute corresponding to the daughter and the OUT attribute to the mother ... This is the way lexical rules are implemented in the English Resource Grammar ... as well in the CoreGram Project and the Grammix grammar development environment" (Section 5.1, p. 156; also Section 2.2, p. 130, and Section 5.3, p. 165: "lexical rules are now simply unary-branching rules within the lexical part of the type hierarchy"). This directly supports encoding a token's lexical-rule chain as a sequence of unary rule names.
- Word-internal and word-external sign formation can be treated uniformly as typed mother-daughter configurations (Koenig 1999; ERG; CoreGram) (Section 2.2, p. 130). One replay mechanism can serve both lexical and phrasal rules.
- Principle of surface combinatorics: "if expression A consists of the concatenation of B and C (B ⊕ C), then all grammatical constraints that make reference to B and C are limited to A" (Section 2.1, p. 129). This is the locality and concatenation assumption behind rule-by-rule replay.
- Words are syntactic atoms (Lexical Integrity) (Section 2.1, pp. 128-129). Supports leaves-equal-tokens for the core cases; the authors call the counterexamples "relatively marginal or cross-linguistically rare" and "limited in scope to prosodic or morphophonological information" (p. 134).
- Most combinatorial information is in the lexical entry; phrase-structure rules are "boring and few in number", "only a handful of general phrase-structural schemata" (Section 3.4, p. 140). This supports (a) putting most of the predictive burden on per-token labels and (b) a small set of coarse universal schemas for the relaxed fallback.
- Entries are descriptions, and almost everything in an entry is inferred from its classes: "only the stem form and the relation denoted by the semantic content ... need to be stipulated" (Section 3.2, p. 137; Section 3.1, pp. 134-135). A lexical type label plus the token's stem therefore identifies the entry in the normal case.
- The lexical type hierarchy mixes cross-linguistically common and language-particular classes (Section 4.2, p. 144; Section 4.3, pp. 148-150, Ackerman and Webelhuth's `univ-` vs. `german-` passive types). This fits the long-term multilingual aim and gives natural coarse supertypes (my inference: these could serve as back-off labels).
- Binding and linking are constraints on ARG-ST, not on tree configurations (Section 3.3, pp. 138-140). They need no extra tree information beyond what replay already computes.
- Defaults can be compiled away (Malouf, fn. 10, p. 151), so a monotonic engine suffices in principle.
- HPSG's lexicon is close to Categorial Grammar's, where "two distinct contexts of occurrence correspond to two distinct entries" (Section 3.4, p. 141). The chapter does not mention supertagging; the parallel to CCG-style supertagging is my remark.

## Theory strains

- Lexicalism itself is contested (Section 2.2, pp. 129-134; Introduction, p. 126). Distributed Morphology (Marantz 1997; Bruening 2018) and Construction Grammar (Goldberg 1995) argue lexicalist accounts fail. The authors hold that the arguments "are not especially strong" but "acknowledge that these questions are not yet settled".
- Lexical Integrity vs. endoclitics, word-part ellipsis, suspended affixation (Section 2.2, pp. 131-134). Competing repairs: enriched morphophonology (Chaves), discontinuous lexical items with order domains (Crysmann, Dost), lexical sharing (Broadwell), OT constraint reranking (Anderson 2005). The authors admit a breach of the strictest Lexical Integrity and offer no account of where the limit lies, only that unconstrained violation is unattested.
- Formal status of lexical entries (description vs. described object) "has engendered a fair amount of theoretical work and some debate" (Section 3.1, p. 135).
- Is ARG-ST confined to lexical signs? Ginzburg and Sag (2000) say yes; Przepiórkowski (2001) and others say no. "HPSG researchers have generally not explored this question in depth" (Section 3.2, p. 138).
- Binding: ARG-ST obliqueness vs. configurational c-command (Section 3.3, pp. 139-140). Wechsler (1999) on Balinese is presented as evidence that the lexical account is not a notational variant. The authors explicitly do not argue for the specific binding theory.
- Subtypes of `word` vs. implicational constraints on substructures (Section 4.2, pp. 144-148). The first is called "formally feasible" but "potentially rather inelegant" and redundant with the `head` hierarchy; Ackerman and Webelhuth (1998) nevertheless use it.
- Monotonic vs. default inheritance (Section 4.4, pp. 150-155). Monotonic inheritance "runs into vexing issues"; several definitions of default unification exist; Malouf holds defaults are only abbreviatory.
- Sort-resolved, fully enumerated type hierarchies are "unsatisfying" and "redundant", existing only "to satisfy a formal requirement" (Section 5.3, pp. 161-162). Online type construction is the proposed remedy; its model-theoretic formalization is only sketched (fn. 11).
- Formalization of lexical rules "has proven surprisingly difficult" (p. 127); "a sound basis for the formal details ... is not easily formulated" (Section 5.1, pp. 155-156). Meurers (2001) vs. Lahm (2016). Also: are lexical rules redundancy statements or generative devices (Section 2.1, p. 128)?
- Directionality of lexical rules is questionable for alternations such as causative/inchoative or ditransitive/prepositional (Goldberg 1991, 1995) (Section 5.2, p. 159).
- Extraction: CELR (Pollard and Sag 1994) vs. underspecified gap-ss (Bouma et al. 2001) vs. traces (Levine and Hukari 2006; Müller) (Section 5.3, pp. 160-161, 164-165). The underspecification account "comes at a cost of additional types and constraints for extraction, and a loosening of requirements for the correspondence between the ARG-ST list and the valence lists". The authors doubt that CELR-style rules "are ever motivated" over underspecification.
- Clitics: lexical rule (Monachesi 1993) vs. constraint on `cl-wd` (Miller and Sag 1997) (Sections 5.2-5.3, pp. 159-161).
- Diathesis: lexical rules (Müller 2006, 2010) vs. phrasal constructions (Goldberg 1995) vs. online type construction (Kay 2002) vs. type-based derivational morphology (Riehemann 1998; Koenig 1999) (Section 5.3, p. 165).
- Lexical rules vs. underspecification in general: "there is no general guideline"; "the issue cannot be settled a priori and must be argued on a case by case basis" (Section 5.3, p. 165; Section 6, p. 166).
- Inflection: lexical rules vs. morpheme-based (Emerson and Copestake 2015) vs. realizational lexical types (Crysmann and Bonami 2016) (Section 5.2, p. 158; Section 5.3, p. 164).
- Minor: terminology for lexeme/word and lexical entry/lexical item is not uniform (fn. 1, p. 127; fn. 7, p. 134); DEPS (adjuncts as dependents) is introduced by Bouma et al. and only mentioned in passing (p. 160).

## Not covered here

All items in this section are my own remarks, not claims of the chapter.

- The chapter does not say how the ERG actually treats extraction (lexical rule, unary phrasal rule, or underspecification) or English contracted forms and clitics (*n't*, *'s*). This needs to be checked against the grammar itself; it decides how caveats 1 and 2 apply to English.
- Nothing on MRS, on semantic composition, or on whether semantics is fully determined by the derivation.
- Nothing on tokenization, multiword expressions, or the mapping between orthographic tokens and lexical entries, which is where leaves-equal-tokens is most often tested in practice. Idioms are in another chapter.
- Nothing on dependency structure or on head/dependent relations as they would be read off a derivation for a UD bridge.
- Nothing on how implemented systems (LKB/ACE, TRALE, Enju) restrict lexical rule application (ordering, cycles, spelling-changing vs. non-spelling-changing rules), which determines how long and how varied the unary chain in a tag can be.
- The chapter does not mention supertagging or any statistical use of lexical types.
- The lexeme/word distinction (and which lexical rules are lexeme-to-lexeme vs. lexeme-to-word) is explicitly set aside (fn. 1); it matters for the well-formedness of unary chains in the tree encoding.
- Caveats 2 and 5 rest partly on my inferences from formulations shown in the chapter; they are marked as such above.
